Scalability — Averages Lie

Chapter 1 · Reliable, Scalable, Maintainable Applications · Describing Load & Performance
Course progress4 / 109 (3.7%)

🌩️ The Problem: Your Dashboard Says 200 ms. Your CEO Is Furious.

It's launch week. Your service is humming. The latency dashboard glows a comfortable green: average response time: 200 ms. Everyone's happy. You go to bed.

Next morning the support queue is on fire. Your biggest customer — the one with the most data, the most activity, the contract that pays your team's salaries — says the app is "unusably slow," sometimes freezing for 4 or 5 seconds. You stare at your dashboard. Still 200 ms average. You re-run the query. Still 200 ms. The numbers say everything is fine. The customer says everything is broken. Who's lying?

Neither. Your average is hiding a brutal truth: while 99% of requests really are fast, the slowest 1% — which always seem to hit your most engaged users — take seconds. And because those users do the most, they trigger the most requests, so they hit the slow tail constantly. The average smears their pain into invisibility.

💡 Why this should bother you

"Scalability" is not a single number you stamp on a system. Before you can say a system "scales," you need a precise way to say (a) how much load it's under and (b) how well it performs — and that second part is where most engineers, and most candidates in interviews, fall flat. They reach for the average, the one statistic almost guaranteed to mislead them. Today we fix that, permanently.

📖 What Is It? Describing Load and Performance

Day 1 named the three concerns of every data system: reliability, scalability, maintainability. Days 2–3 drilled into reliability. Now we turn to the second pillar. Kleppmann is careful here: scalability is not a one-dimensional label ("X is scalable, Y isn't"). It's a way of asking a question:

💡 The core question of scalability

"If the system grows in a particular way, what are our options for coping with the growth?" To even ask it, you need two precise vocabularies: one for load (how much is being thrown at the system) and one for performance (how well the system holds up). Today is about building those two vocabularies. Tomorrow we use them to actually cope with load.

Part 1 — Describing load with load parameters

Load is described with a few numbers Kleppmann calls load parameters. The right ones depend entirely on your system's architecture:

  • Requests per second to a web server (throughput of requests)
  • Ratio of reads to writes in a database
  • Number of simultaneously active users in a chat room
  • Hit rate on a cache
  • …or something subtler and architecture-specific — like the distribution of followers per user on Twitter, which we'll see is the load parameter that breaks the naive design.

The skill is picking the parameter that actually stresses your design — sometimes it's the average case, but often it's the distribution and its extremes.

Part 2 — Describing performance: throughput vs. response time

Once load is defined, you investigate performance two ways:

  • "Load up, resources fixed": when you increase a load parameter and keep CPU/memory/etc. constant, how is performance affected?
  • "Load up, hold performance": when you increase a load parameter, how much must you increase resources to keep performance unchanged?

Two performance metrics dominate:

  • Throughput — records processed per second, or total time to run a job over a dataset. The headline number for batch systems (think Hadoop, which we'll meet on Day 80).
  • Response time — the time between a client sending a request and receiving the response. The headline number for online systems. This is what your furious customer actually feels.
💡 Latency ≠ response time (a precise distinction)

People use these interchangeably; DDIA does not. Response time is what the client sees: the actual service time plus network delays plus queueing delays. Latency is the duration a request is waiting to be handled — latent, awaiting service. Response time is the whole round trip; latency is one ingredient of it. The queueing part of latency is where your tail disasters are born.

Response time is a distribution, not a number

Here's the mental-model upgrade. Even if you fire the identical request a thousand times, you'll get a thousand different response times — because of background processes, network glitches, garbage-collection pauses, a page fault forcing a disk read, TCP retransmits, or just a noisy neighbor. So you should think of response time not as a single value but as a distribution of values you can measure. And that distribution is almost always heavily right-skewed: a dense cluster of fast requests, with a long tail dragging out to the right. That skew is exactly why the average betrays you.

🧠 The Analogy: The Billionaire in the Bar & the Airport Security Line

Percentiles vs. averages → the billionaire walks into a bar

Ten people are drinking in a bar. Their average net worth is modest — call it $80k each. Then Jeff Bezos walks in. Suddenly the average net worth in the room is over $10 billion per person. By the mean, everyone in that bar is a billionaire. In reality, ten people are exactly as broke as before, and one person is extraordinarily rich. The mean got hijacked by a single outlier; the median (the middle person) didn't budge.

Mapping: the bar patrons = your normal fast requests; Bezos = the rare multi-second outlier request; "everyone's a billionaire" = your average response time looking terrible (or, in the reverse failure, looking fine while a few requests are catastrophic). The fix is the same in both worlds: stop reporting the mean, start reporting the median (the typical experience) and the high percentiles (the worst experiences that real users actually hit).

Tail latencies → the airport security line

You arrive at the airport. Usually security takes 5 minutes (your median). But there's a 1-in-100 chance you hit the day the line is 40 minutes (your p99) — a crew shortage, a flagged bag, a broken scanner. You don't plan your arrival around the 5-minute median; if you did, you'd miss your flight one trip out of a hundred. You plan around the tail. And here's the kicker: people who fly constantly — the frequent flyers, your most valuable customers — hit that 40-minute tail far more often than someone who flies once a year, simply because they take so many more trips.

Mapping: the 5-minute typical wait = p50; the 40-minute bad day = p99/p999; the frequent flyer = your power user who fires thousands of requests and therefore reliably experiences your worst-case latency. The customers with the most data and activity are the ones the tail hurts most. That's not bad luck — it's arithmetic.

💡 The aha

Averages describe a world that doesn't exist — a fictional "typical" that's neither the common case nor the painful case. Percentiles describe the world your users actually live in. p50 tells you what's normal; p99/p999 tell you how bad "bad" gets, and your heaviest users live in that tail.

⚙️ How It Actually Works

We'll work the two halves of today's lesson with two interactives. First, the canonical load-parameter case study — Twitter's fan-out problem — animated and stepped. Then a live percentile explorer that lets you watch the mean lie to your face as load rises.

Case study: Twitter's timeline and the right load parameter

Twitter (circa 2012, the DDIA example) has two main operations. Post tweet: a user publishes a message to their followers (~4.6k requests/sec average, ~12k/sec peak). Home timeline: a user views tweets from everyone they follow (~300k requests/sec). Notice: reads outnumber writes by ~65×. That ratio, and the follower distribution, are the load parameters that decide the whole architecture.

Animated architecture: write fan-out

Below is Twitter's chosen design (Approach 2): when you post, the tweet is pushed at write time into a precomputed timeline cache for each of your followers. Reads then become a cheap single lookup. Watch one tweet fan out to three followers' caches.

One write balloons into N writes, one per follower. For an average user that's fine — but the cost is bounded by your follower count, which brings us to the catch. Step through the full story:

Step-through: the two designs and Twitter's hybrid escape

Approach 1 · Read fan-out (pull)
Do the work when someone reads.

Posting = insert one row into a global tweets table. Reading a timeline = find everyone you follow, fetch their recent tweets, merge by time. Writes are trivial; reads are expensive and happen 65× more often.

Approach 2 · Write fan-out (push)
Do the work when someone posts.

Posting = push the tweet into a cached timeline for each follower. Reading = one cheap lookup of your prebuilt list. Reads are blazing; writes amplify by your follower count — fine for most, lethal for celebrities.

💡 What you just watched

Two killers, visible at once. (1) Queueing delay: as utilization climbs toward 100%, a small bump in load sends the tail through the roof (that's the curve flattening rightward) — a server can only process a few things at once, and a few slow requests block everyone queued behind them (head-of-line blocking). (2) Tail latency amplification: flip on fan-out and the typical request gets slow, because if your request needs all 100 backends and any one of them hits its p99, your whole request is slow. The more backends, the more often you draw an unlucky tail.

⚖️ The Trade-offs

The percentile explorer above is your trade-off tool for performance — keep playing with it. The two architectural trade-offs to internalize today:

Trade-off A — where do you pay for the timeline? (read vs. write fan-out)

DimensionRead fan-out (pull)Write fan-out (push)
Cost of posting🟢 Trivial — one insert🔴 Heavy — N writes (N = followers)
Cost of reading timeline🔴 Heavy — query + merge many feeds🟢 Trivial — one cache read
Best when…Writes ≫ reads, or fan-out is hugeReads ≫ writes (Twitter: 65×)
Weak spotRead latency under heavy followingCelebrity with millions of followers
Storage🟢 Lean — store each tweet once🔴 Fat — tweet copied into many caches

Twitter chose write fan-out because reads dominate by 65×, then bolted on a hybrid: a handful of celebrity accounts are excluded from fan-out and fetched at read time and merged into your otherwise-precomputed timeline. The right design wasn't either/or — it was driven by a load parameter (the follower distribution) that only revealed itself once they described load precisely.

Trade-off B — which performance number do you optimize?

MetricTells youWatch out for

⚠️ The trade-off to say out loud

"Chasing higher percentiles has diminishing returns and rising cost. Amazon found p999 worth optimizing because the slowest requests belong to their most valuable customers — but reducing p9999 was deemed too expensive for the benefit. You pick how far down the tail to chase based on who lives there and what they're worth."

🌍 In the Wild

Where these exact ideas show up in systems you'll touch:

  • Amazon (DynamoDB / retail): the famous origin of obsessing over p999. Amazon observed that a 100 ms slowdown drops sales ~1%, and that the slowest requests hit customers with the most data in their accounts — the high-value ones. Their internal SLOs are written in percentiles, not averages.
  • Twitter: the read-vs-write fan-out case study itself, including the celebrity hybrid. The load parameter that drove the whole design was the distribution of followers per user — a textbook example of picking the non-obvious load parameter.
  • Prometheus / Grafana & monitoring stacks: expose latency as histograms and let you query histogram_quantile(0.99, …). Modern observability is built around percentiles precisely because the industry learned averages hide outages. (This is the "detailed monitoring" guardrail from Day 3 made concrete.)
  • CDNs & service meshes (Envoy, Istio): report p50/p90/p99/p999 per route and use hedged / tied requests — fire a duplicate to a second replica if the first is slow — explicitly to fight tail-latency amplification in fan-out calls.
  • Google (the "Tail at Scale" paper): the canonical treatment of why a service that calls 100 backends sees the median user hit a tail — and the mitigations (hedging, micro-partitioning, request reprioritization) the explorer above hints at.

🔍 The SDE 2 Lens

Where this hits your day job

You've almost certainly shipped a "perf is fine, see, average is 200 ms" claim. Today reframes a dozen things you've half-noticed:

  • That flaky integration test or batch job that's "usually fast." "Usually" is p50. The CI timeout it occasionally trips is p99 finally getting measured. Same distribution, you were just only looking at the median.
  • Your Grafana panel labeled "avg latency." Change it to p50/p95/p99 today and you'll discover problems your average has been hiding for months. The mean panel is the single most misleading chart in most dashboards.
  • The "N+1 query" code review comment. That's a read-fan-out vs. precompute decision in miniature — do you join/aggregate on every read, or maintain a denormalized/cached view written ahead of time? Same trade-off as Twitter's timeline, just at function scale.
  • The microservice call that's slow "only sometimes." If your endpoint fans out to several downstream services and waits for all of them, your p50 is roughly their p99. Tail amplification is why "all our services are fast individually but the page is slow" happens.
  • Capacity planning. When someone asks "can we handle 2× traffic?", the honest answer isn't about the average — it's "what does p99 do as utilization climbs past 80%?" Because of queueing, the tail explodes long before the average looks scary.
🔗 The mental upgrade

Stop saying "it's fast." Start saying "p50 is X, p99 is Y, measured at Z load." That one sentence marks the difference between an engineer who guesses at performance and one who measures it. It will change how you read every dashboard for the rest of your career.

🎯 System Design Interview Playbook

When this comes up: essentially every design question. "Design Twitter," "design a news feed," "design a rate limiter," "design a metrics system" — and the moment any interviewer asks "how would you measure if this is fast enough?" or "what happens at 10× load?"

What to say

  • "First let me define the load: what are the requests/sec, the read:write ratio, and is there a skewed distribution — like a few power users or celebrities — that stresses the design?"
  • "For performance I'll specify a target in percentiles: e.g., p99 < 200 ms, not 'average 200 ms', because averages hide the tail my most active users hit."
  • "For the feed I'd default to write fan-out (precompute timelines) since reads dominate, but use a hybrid for high-follower accounts to avoid write explosions."
  • "This service fans out to N downstreams, so I'll budget for tail-latency amplification — hedged requests or a timeout-with-fallback so one slow backend doesn't sink the whole request."
  • "My capacity plan keeps utilization under ~70–80% headroom, because queueing makes the tail blow up as we approach saturation."

The trade-off to articulate

"Push the work to write time (precompute) for read-heavy systems; pull at read time for write-heavy or extreme-fan-out cases; go hybrid when the load distribution has both. And chase percentiles only as deep as the value of the customers living in that tail."

❌ Common candidate mistake

Quoting average latency as the SLO ("we'll keep it under 100 ms average"). Senior interviewers hear this as "I've never run a real production service." Worse: optimizing the mean can actively harm the tail (e.g., batching helps average throughput but adds queueing delay to the slowest requests).

✅ Power move

Proactively say: "Percentiles don't add. I can't take the p99 of each service and sum them to get the end-to-end p99 — I have to measure the p99 of the whole request, ideally with a sliding window like a t-digest or HDR histogram so the numbers stay cheap and accurate." Naming that you can't average or sum percentiles, and that you'd compute them with a forward-decaying reservoir, signals you've actually operated systems at scale.

🃏 Common Misconceptions

Click a card to flip it.

Myth #1

"Average response time tells me how my users feel."

click to flip ↻
Reality

The mean is dragged around by outliers and hides the tail completely. Two systems with identical 200 ms averages can have wildly different p99s (one at 250 ms, one at 5 s). Report p50 for the typical user and p99/p999 for the worst — never the mean alone.

Myth #2

"Scalability is a property — a system either 'is scalable' or it 'isn't.'"

click to flip ↻
Reality

Scalability is a question, not a label: "if load grows this specific way, what are our options to cope?" A system can scale beautifully for reads and fall over on a skewed write pattern. You must name the load parameter before the word "scalable" means anything.

Myth #3

"If each microservice is fast (good p99), the whole request will be fast."

click to flip ↻
Reality

Tail-latency amplification. If a request needs all 100 backends and any one hits its p99, the request is slow. With 100 backends each at 1% tail, ~63% of requests touch a slow one. The end-to-end p99 is far worse than any single backend's p99 — and you can't compute it by averaging or summing.

🧪 Interactive Zone

🤔 Pause & Think — Why do your heaviest users hit the slow tail most often?

Two reasons compound. (1) Probability: if 1% of requests are slow (p99), then a user who makes 1 request has a 1% chance of a bad experience, but a user who makes 200 requests to render their busy dashboard has a 1 − 0.99²⁰⁰ ≈ 87% chance of hitting at least one slow request. More requests = more lottery tickets in the slow draw. (2) Correlation: heavy users often have more data (more followers, more orders, more history), so their individual requests are genuinely heavier and skew toward the tail on their own. The two effects stack: your most valuable customers are statistically guaranteed to experience your worst latency. That's why Amazon optimizes p999 — it's literally the experience of the people paying the most.

🤔 Pause & Think — Why does adding a tiny bit of load near 90% utilization blow up the tail?

Queueing theory. A server processes only a few requests in parallel; the rest wait in a queue. When the arrival rate is well below capacity, the queue is usually empty and waits are short. But as utilization approaches 100%, the queue's average length grows roughly like 1/(1−ρ) — it heads toward infinity. So going from 80% → 90% utilization isn't a 10% slowdown; it can double the queueing delay, and the tail (which is dominated by the unlucky requests that arrive when the queue is already long) explodes faster still. Add head-of-line blocking — one slow request stalls everything queued behind it — and you see why production systems keep meaningful headroom rather than running hot. This is exactly the rightward stretch you saw in the explorer.

⚡ Quick Check #1
Your service's average latency is a healthy 80 ms, but the p99 is 3 seconds. A teammate says "the average is fine, ship it." What's the one-sentence rebuttal?

"The 80 ms average is hiding the fact that 1 in every 100 requests takes 3 seconds — and those slow requests cluster on our most active users, who fire so many requests they hit the tail constantly. We need a percentile-based SLO (e.g., p99 < 300 ms), not an average, or we'll ship a great-looking dashboard and a furious power-user base."

⚡ Quick Check #2
For a read-heavy social feed (reads ≫ writes), do you precompute timelines at write time or assemble them at read time — and what's the one exception?

Precompute at write time (write fan-out), because reads dominate, so you want them cheap — pay the cost on the rarer write. The exception is celebrity / high-follower accounts: fanning their posts out to tens of millions of follower caches is a write explosion, so you exclude them and fetch their tweets at read time, merging them into the otherwise-precomputed timeline. That hybrid is what Twitter actually does.

✅ Key Takeaways

Scalability is a question, not a label. "If load grows this way, what are our options to cope?" You must define the load before "scalable" means anything.
Pick the load parameter that actually stresses your design. Often it's not the average but a distribution's extremes — like Twitter's follower count per user.
Averages lie; use percentiles. Report p50 for the typical user and p99/p999 for the tail. The mean is hijacked by outliers and hides exactly the pain your power users feel.
Tails come from queueing and fan-out. Queueing delay explodes as utilization nears 100%; tail-latency amplification means a request waiting on N backends inherits all their tails. Percentiles don't add or average.
Pay the cost where it's cheapest. Write fan-out (precompute) for read-heavy systems, read fan-out for write-heavy, hybrid when the distribution is skewed — exactly Twitter's timeline design.
