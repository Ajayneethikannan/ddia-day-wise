Scalability — Averages Lie, and Twitter's Fan-Out Problem

Chapter 1 · Reliable, Scalable, Maintainable Applications · Describing Load & Performance
Course progress4 / 109 (3.7%)

🌩️ The Problem: "It works fine on my laptop" Meets 300,000 Reads a Second

Picture early Twitter. A user opens the app and pulls down to refresh their home timeline — the merged stream of tweets from everyone they follow. Simple, right? You'd write the obvious query: find everyone this person follows, fetch their recent tweets, merge them sorted by time, return the top 100. On your laptop, with ten users, it returns in 2 milliseconds. Ship it. 🚀

Now scale it. Twitter was serving roughly 300,000 home-timeline reads per second at peak. That same "obvious" query — a giant fan-in join across the social graph — now has to run 300,000 times a second. The database melts. Refreshes that took 2ms now take seconds, the app shows spinners, and during the World Cup it falls over entirely. The feature didn't change. The load changed, and the design that was perfect at 10 users is catastrophic at 300 million.

💡 Why this should rewire how you think

Scalability is not a property you can label a system with ("is it scalable? yes/no"). It's a question: "if load grows in this specific way, what are our options for coping?" And you can't even ask that question until you can answer two harder ones precisely: How do we describe the load? and How do we measure whether performance is still acceptable? Get those two definitions wrong — and most engineers do, because they reach for the average — and every capacity decision you make afterward is built on sand. Today is about getting the numbers right.

📖 What Is It? Load Parameters & Performance Numbers

Day 1 named Scalability as the second of the three concerns, and Day 3 closed out Reliability. Today we make "scalability" concrete. Kleppmann's framing has two halves, and the whole lesson hangs on them:

Half 1 — Describing load

You describe load with a few well-chosen numbers called load parameters. The right parameters depend entirely on your system's architecture:

  • Requests per second to a web server (throughput of incoming traffic)
  • Ratio of reads to writes in a database
  • Number of simultaneously active users / sessions
  • Hit rate on a cache
  • For Twitter: the distribution of followers per user — because that, not the raw tweet rate, is what actually determines the work.

The skill is choosing the parameter that captures where the work actually is. Twitter's marquee insight: the load isn't "tweets per second" (a modest ~4,600/s). It's fan-out — the number of timelines each tweet must be delivered to. A celebrity with 30 million followers generates one write that explodes into 30 million downstream actions. The follower distribution is the load parameter that matters.

Half 2 — Describing performance

Once you can describe load, you ask: "when I increase a load parameter and keep resources fixed, how is performance affected?" and "how much do I need to grow resources to keep performance unchanged?" To answer either, you need a crisp performance metric. Two show up constantly:

MetricDefinitionWho cares
ThroughputRecords/requests processed per unit time (e.g. req/s, or total time for a fixed dataset)Batch systems (Hadoop, ETL) — Chapter 10
Response timeTime between a client sending a request and receiving the response — what the user actually feelsOnline systems (web, APIs) — what we focus on today
💡 Latency ≠ response time (a precision most people get wrong)

DDIA is careful here. Response time is what the client sees: includes network delays, queueing delays, and the actual service time. Latency is the duration a request is waiting to be handled — latent, awaiting service. They're related but not identical. In interviews, using these precisely signals you actually understand the stack instead of repeating buzzwords.

The big one: response time is a distribution, not a number

Here's the idea that separates senior engineers from juniors. Response time is not a single value — every identical request returns a slightly different time, because of context switches, network packet loss, GC pauses, a background job stealing CPU, TCP retransmits. So you have a distribution. Report it with percentiles, never the mean:

  • p50 (median) — half your requests are faster, half slower. The "typical" experience.
  • p95 / p99 / p99.9 (tail latencies) — 95th, 99th, 99.9th percentile. "1 in 100 requests is at least this slow." This is where the pain hides.

The mean (average) is nearly useless: it doesn't tell you how many users experienced the delay, and a single 10-second outlier can drag the average up while telling you nothing about the typical or the tail. Amazon famously specifies internal response-time requirements at the p99.9 — because the slowest 0.1% of requests often belong to the customers with the most data, i.e. the most valuable accounts. We'll see why the average lies in a moment.

🧠 The Analogy: The Coffee Shop & the Airport Security Line

Percentiles → the morning coffee queue

Imagine a barista who serves 99 customers in 30 seconds each, then customer #100 orders 12 complicated drinks and takes 10 minutes. The average wait that morning looks fine-ish — the one big order gets diluted across 100 people. But ask the actual humans: 99 of them had a great experience (your p50 is 30s), and one had a miserable one (your p100 is 10 minutes). Now imagine that "one slow customer" isn't random — it's always your highest-spending corporate catering client, because big orders take long. That's Amazon's p99.9 insight: the tail is where your most important customers live.

Mapping: each customer = a request; service time = response time; the average = the misleading single number your dashboard shows; the 10-minute customer = the p99.9 tail; "big orders take longest and come from your best clients" = slow requests correlate with data-rich, high-value accounts.

Tail latency amplification → waiting on the slowest of N friends

You and 9 friends agree to leave for a movie only once everyone has passed through airport security. Each person clears security quickly 95% of the time, but 1 in 20 gets randomly pulled for a 20-minute pat-down. Any single person is probably fine. But with 10 people, the chance that at least one gets pulled is 1 − 0.95¹⁰ ≈ 40%. The group's wait is the slowest person's wait — so the group is delayed almost half the time, even though each individual is rarely delayed.

Mapping: the group = a single user-facing page; each friend = one backend service call the page makes; "leave only when all are through" = the page can't render until every call returns; the rare pat-down = a backend's p95/p99 slow request. This is tail latency amplification: when one request fans out to many backends, the rare slow tail of each one becomes the common experience of the whole page.

💡 The aha

Both analogies attack the same instinct: "on average it's fast, so we're fine." Averages hide the people who suffer (the coffee tail), and fan-out turns rare suffering into common suffering (the airport group). The right unit of measurement isn't the average request — it's the user, at the percentile that hurts.

⚙️ How It Actually Works

Let's ground both halves of the lesson in Twitter's real architecture — the canonical "describing load" case study — and then watch percentiles behave.

Animated architecture: fan-out-on-write delivering a tweet

The naive approach (fan-out on read) does the expensive merge at read time — terrible when reads outnumber writes 65:1. Twitter's answer: do the work at write time. When a user tweets, push a copy into a precomputed home-timeline cache for each follower. Reads become a trivial lookup. Watch one tweet fan out to three followers' caches:

Blue dashes = the write flowing outward. One tweet became three cache inserts. That's the trade: we pay more work at write time (which is rare) so that read time (which is constant and high-volume) becomes a cheap lookup. Average follower count ≈ 75, so each tweet really triggers ~75 inserts — totally fine... until a celebrity tweets and it becomes 30 million inserts. Hold that thought for the trade-offs.

The math: tail latency amplification

Why obsessing over p99 of a single backend matters so much: when a user request fans out to N parallel backend calls and must wait for all of them, the probability the whole page hits a slow tail is 1 − (1 − p)^N. A "rare" 1% slow tail per service becomes near-certain across a page of many calls.

Per-backend chance of a slow (p99) request p = 1% (0.01) Page makes 1 backend call: 1 − 0.99^1 = 1% slow pages Page makes 10 backend calls: 1 − 0.99^10 = 9.6% slow pages Page makes 100 backend calls: 1 − 0.99^100 = 63% slow pages 😱 Lesson: a "1-in-100" slow backend becomes a "2-in-3" slow PAGE once you fan out to 100 services. The tail you ignored is now your median user's experience. This is why p99/p99.9 — not the average — is the number that governs real systems.

⚖️ The Trade-offs

The Twitter case is a perfect trade-off study: do the work at write time, or at read time? There's no universally right answer — it depends on the load parameters, especially how lopsided your read:write ratio is and how skewed your follower distribution is.

The deeper principle

"Scalability" doesn't mean "make it fast." It means knowing where your load actually concentrates and moving the expensive work to the cheaper side of the read/write ratio. The same move recurs everywhere: precompute vs compute-on-demand, materialized views vs queries, eager vs lazy. You'll meet it again in column stores (Day 24), replication (Day 32), and stream processing (Day 90).

🏢 In the Wild

Twitter — the hybrid timeline

Exactly as DDIA describes: Twitter moved from fan-out-on-read (the naive join) to fan-out-on-write (precomputed home-timeline caches) to handle ~300k reads/s. But a few mega-celebrities broke pure push, so they use a hybrid: tweets from ordinary users are fanned out to followers' caches at write time; tweets from celebrities are not, and are merged in at read time when a follower loads their timeline. Load parameter that drove the whole design: the distribution of followers per user, not tweet volume.

Amazon — p99.9 service-level objectives

Amazon specifies internal response-time requirements on the 99.9th percentile, not the average. Their reasoning is the one from today: the slowest requests usually belong to customers with the most data — accounts that have made many purchases, i.e. the most valuable customers. They also found that a 100ms increase in latency cut sales by ~1%, and others reported a 1-second slowdown dropping customer satisfaction by 16%. Tail latency is money.

Google, Netflix, Dropbox — SLOs and tail latency

Every serious SRE shop reports latency as percentiles on dashboards (p50/p95/p99/p99.9) and writes SLOs against the tail, never the mean. Google's "The Tail at Scale" paper is the canonical text on tail latency amplification — techniques like hedged requests (send to two replicas, take the first to answer) and tied requests exist specifically to tame the p99 when a request fans out to thousands of servers.

Prometheus / monitoring tooling

Why does your metrics stack store histograms instead of just averaging? Because you can't recover percentiles from an average after the fact. Tools like Prometheus use histogram buckets (and HDR-histograms / t-digest algorithms exist) precisely so you can ask "what was p99 in that 5-minute window?" — and so percentiles can be aggregated across many machines correctly (you can't average two machines' p95s to get the fleet p95).

🔍 The SDE 2 Lens

Where this shows up in your day job

You touch these numbers constantly — usually without realizing the average on your dashboard is quietly lying to you.

  • Your Grafana/Datadog "avg latency" panel that looks healthy. Add a p95 and p99 panel right next to it. The day they diverge from the average is the day a slice of your users started suffering and your average hid it. Senior engineers instinctively look at the tail first.
  • That endpoint that "feels slow sometimes but the metrics look fine." Classic average-masking-the-tail. The mean is dominated by the many fast requests; the complaints come from the p99. Pull the percentiles and you'll find the smoking gun.
  • The N+1 query / microservice fan-out in your service. Every backend call you add to a request multiplies tail-latency exposure (1 − (1−p)^N). That "just one more service call" can move your page from 1% slow to 10% slow. This is why batching and parallelizing-then-bounding matters.
  • The cache you added "to make it faster." You implicitly chose fan-out-on-write (precompute) over compute-on-read. Now you own cache invalidation and the celebrity/hot-key problem. The Twitter trade-off is the same decision you make with every materialized view or denormalization.
  • The load test that reports a single average number. Push back. Demand percentiles and the load parameters used (concurrency, read/write mix). A load test without percentiles tells you almost nothing about real user pain.
💡 Ohhh, THAT'S why

...your SRE keeps saying "what's the p99?" when you proudly report a low average, and why on-call dashboards are full of percentile lines. They learned the hard way that the average is the most comfortable lie in observability.

🎯 System Design Interview Playbook

When this comes up

Constantly — and early. It's the heart of "Design Twitter / a news feed," "Design Instagram," and it shows up as the back-of-envelope step in every design: "What's the expected QPS? Read or write heavy? What latency target?" Whenever the interviewer asks you to "estimate the load" or "what are your SLAs," they're testing exactly today's material.

What to say (the checklist)

  • Pin down the load parameters first: "Before designing, let me state the load — expected QPS, read:write ratio, and the key skew, e.g. followers-per-user distribution. The right design depends on these."
  • Specify latency as a percentile target: "I'll target p99 < 200ms for the timeline read, not an average — the tail is what users feel and what hides our worst cases."
  • Make the fan-out decision explicitly: "Reads dominate ~65:1, so I'll fan out on write — precompute each user's feed — to keep reads to a single cache lookup."
  • Handle the skew / hot key: "Pure push breaks for celebrities, so I'll go hybrid: push for normal users, pull-and-merge for high-follower accounts above a threshold."
  • Name tail-latency amplification: "Since the page fans out to many services, I'll watch p99 per backend and consider hedged requests, because rare tails compound across fan-out."

The trade-off to articulate

"It's a read-cost vs write-cost trade. Fan-out-on-write spends at write time to make the high-volume reads cheap — great when reads ≫ writes — but explodes for high-fan-out authors. The hybrid moves the threshold case to fan-out-on-read. I'm choosing where to pay based on the load parameters, not guessing."

⚠️ Common candidate mistake

Two killers. (1) Quoting an average latency as the SLO ("it'll be ~50ms on average") — instant junior signal; always speak in percentiles. (2) Jumping straight to "I'll use a NoSQL database and a cache" before stating the load parameters — you can't justify any design choice until you've described the load it must handle.

⭐ Power move

Say: "Let me derive the numbers. ~500M tweets/day ≈ 5,800/s average, call it ~12k/s peak writes. Reads are ~65× higher, so ~300k/s timeline reads. Since reads dominate, I fan out on write. But the follower distribution is heavy-tailed, so I special-case celebrities with read-time merge, and I'll set a p99 SLO with tail-latency amplification in mind for the multi-service render." Deriving load parameters out loud, then letting them drive the architecture, is the clearest staff-level signal you can give.

🤔 Pause & Think #1 — Your dashboard shows a healthy 80ms average, yet support is flooded with "the app is so slow!" complaints. What's going on, and what do you look at?

How can the metric say fine while users say broken?

Answer: The average is masking the tail. Suppose 95% of requests are ~40ms and 5% are ~900ms (a slow path — a cache miss, a cold shard, a data-rich user). The mean works out around 80ms and looks great, but 1 in 20 requests is nearly a second — and across a session with many requests, most users hit that slow path at least once, so they feel a slow app. The fix in your investigation: stop looking at the average. Pull up p95, p99, and p99.9. You'll see the tail spike the average is hiding. Then segment — by endpoint, by customer, by shard — because tail requests usually correlate with something specific (the largest accounts, a hot partition, a GC-pausing node). This is precisely why DDIA insists on percentiles and why Amazon specs p99.9: the average is the most comfortable lie in monitoring.

🤔 Pause & Think #2 — Your social app is read-heavy today, so you fan out on write. A new "groups" feature lets one post go to a 5-million-member group. What breaks, and what do you do?

You changed a load parameter. Which one, and what's the consequence?

Answer: You just introduced a heavy tail into the fan-out distribution — the exact celebrity problem. A single post now triggers 5 million timeline inserts, which can saturate write capacity, delay delivery (people see the post minutes late), and create a thundering write spike. The senior move is the hybrid: keep fan-out-on-write for normal-sized targets, but for any post whose fan-out exceeds a threshold, skip the push and merge it in at read time instead (fan-out on read for that subset). You're routing by the load parameter's value. You might also rate-limit/queue the fan-out, deliver asynchronously, or shard the group's feed. The meta-lesson: a new feature silently changed a load parameter, and the design that was correct yesterday isn't anymore — which is exactly why "scalability" is a question you keep re-asking, not a box you tick once.

⚡ Quick Check #1

A page renders by making 50 parallel backend calls and waits for all of them. Each backend returns a slow (≥1s) response 2% of the time. Roughly what fraction of page loads will be slow — and what's the lesson?

P(slow page) = 1 − (1 − 0.02)50 = 1 − 0.9850 ≈ 1 − 0.364 ≈ 64%. Almost two-thirds of page loads are slow, even though each backend is "98% fast"! This is tail latency amplification: when a request fans out to many dependencies and waits for all, the rare per-service tail becomes the common whole-page experience. Lessons: minimize the number of synchronous fan-out calls, attack the p99 of each dependency (not the average), and consider hedged/tied requests or returning partial results. It's why the percentile of every dependency — not its average — governs the user experience.

⚡ Quick Check #2

Your system is 99% reads, 1% writes. A teammate proposes computing each user's feed on demand at read time ("simpler, no cache to invalidate"). Why is this likely the wrong call, and what's the right framing?

Computing on read puts the expensive work on the high-volume side. With a 99:1 read:write ratio, you'd run the costly merge 99× as often as you'd run a cheap write. That's fan-out-on-read, and it's backwards for a read-heavy workload. The right framing: move the expensive work to the cheaper side of the read/write ratio — fan out on write (precompute the feed once per write, serve cheap reads). Yes, you now own cache invalidation and the hot-key/celebrity problem, so guard the heavy tail with a hybrid. "Simpler to build" is not the same as "scales under the actual load parameters" — let the read:write ratio drive the decision, not implementation convenience.
