Scalability — Averages Lie, Percentiles Tell the Truth

Chapter 1 · Reliable, Scalable, Maintainable Applications · Describing Load and Performance
Course progress4 / 109 (3.7%)

🌩️ The Problem: "But It Was Fast in Testing!"

Picture this. Your team ships a new checkout service. In load tests it serves requests in 50 ms. The dashboard shows a comforting flat green line: average latency 52 ms. Everyone celebrates. You go live.

Within a week, support tickets pile up. "The site froze when I clicked Pay." "It took 8 seconds and then timed out." But your dashboard still reads 52 ms average. The graph is green. Engineering insists "it's fast." Customers insist "it's broken." Both are looking at the same system — and both are right.

Here's the gut-punch: the customers complaining are very often your most valuable ones. The user with 200 items in their cart, the account with years of order history, the power user whose request touches the most data — they are precisely the ones who hit the slow path. The average hid them. And a single web page often makes dozens of backend calls, so if just one of those calls is slow, the whole page is slow. The slow tail isn't a rounding error — it's the experience of the people you can least afford to lose.

💡 Why this should bother you

"Average response time" is one of the most dangerously misleading metrics in our industry. It can stay flat and healthy while a growing slice of real users suffers terribly. Today we learn to describe load and performance with numbers that don't lie — the vocabulary every senior engineer uses to reason about scale, and the single fastest way to sound like you know what you're doing in a system design interview.

📖 What Is It? Scalability Is a Question, Not a Label

Over Days 1–3 we covered Reliability — keeping the system working correctly even when things go wrong. Today we open the second of DDIA's three concerns: Scalability — the system's ability to cope with increased load.

The first thing Kleppmann does is take away your favorite word. "Scalable" is not a one-dimensional label you can stamp on a system. Saying "X is scalable, Y is not" is meaningless. Scalability is a way of asking a precise question:

💡 The core question of scalability

"If the load grows in this particular way, what are our options for coping with it?" — e.g., "If we go from 10,000 to 100,000 concurrent users, how do we keep response times acceptable while keeping cost sane?" You can't answer that until you've defined two things: load (the input) and performance (the output).

Step 1 — Describe the load with load parameters

Load is described by numbers Kleppmann calls load parameters. The right ones depend entirely on your system's architecture. Examples:

  • Requests per second to a web server (throughput of an online service)
  • Ratio of reads to writes in a database
  • Number of simultaneously active users in a chat room
  • Hit rate on a cache
  • ...and sometimes a subtler one — like the distribution of followers per user, which (as we'll see) is the parameter that actually broke Twitter.

The skill is picking the parameter that actually drives the cost in your system. Pick the wrong one and you'll optimize the wrong thing.

Step 2 — Describe performance: throughput vs response time

Two different questions, often confused:

  • Throughput — how many things can we process per unit time? (records/sec for a batch job, requests/sec for a service). "How much work, total?"
  • Response time — how long does the client wait between sending a request and receiving the answer? "How long does one request take?"

A crucial distinction Kleppmann draws: response time ≠ latency. Response time is what the client sees — service time + network delays + queueing delays. Latency is the duration a request is waiting to be handled — latent, awaiting service. They get used interchangeably in casual talk, but the response time is the number your user actually feels.

Step 3 — Response time is a distribution, not a number

This is the heart of today's lesson. If you send the same request to a server over and over, you'll get a different response time every single time — because of random extras: a context switch, a TCP retransmit, a garbage-collection pause, a page fault, a noisy neighbor on the same machine. Response time is best thought of as a distribution of values you can measure, not a single number.

That's why the average (the arithmetic mean) is a bad summary: it tells you nothing about how many users actually had a bad experience, and it's easily dragged around by a handful of outliers. The better tool is percentiles.

MetricWhat it meansAlso called
p50 (median)Half of requests are faster than this, half slower. The "typical" user experience.50th percentile
p95 / p9995% / 99% of requests are faster than this. The 5% / 1% slowest users are worse."tail latencies"
p999Only 1 in 1,000 requests is slower. Amazon watches this because the slowest requests often belong to the richest customers (most data).99.9th percentile

Percentiles used in your SLOs/SLAs ("99% of requests under 200 ms") are the contract that actually protects users. We'll build a percentile with our own hands in a moment.

🧠 The Analogy: The Coffee Shop & the Airport Security Line

Throughput vs response time → the coffee shop

Imagine a café. Throughput is "how many coffees do we serve per hour?" Response time is "how long does my coffee take from the moment I order?" These are different goals that can even conflict. The barista could maximize throughput by batching — making twelve lattes in one go — but the first customer who ordered now waits ages. Maximize your response time by serving you instantly, and total throughput drops. Real systems constantly trade one against the other.

Mapping: coffees/hour = throughput (requests/sec); your personal wait = response time; the batching trade-off = exactly the tension between latency-optimized and throughput-optimized systems.

Why averages lie → the airport security line

You arrive at airport security. On average, the line takes 5 minutes — sounds fine. But today there's one disabled scanner, a confused traveler with a packed bag, and a shift change. Most people clear in 3 minutes (p50), but you get stuck behind the chaos and it takes 40 minutes (p99). You miss your flight. The "5-minute average" was technically true and completely useless to you. The airport that cares about not losing you measures the worst experiences, not the typical one.

Mapping: the 5-min average = mean latency (hides pain); your 40-min wait = the p99 tail; missing your flight = the customer who abandons the cart. Note the killer detail: you only need to be unlucky on one line. If your trip requires three separate queues, your odds of hitting at least one slow line go up — which is exactly tail latency amplification, where a page that calls many backends is only as fast as its slowest call.

💡 The aha

The average answers "how is the system doing?" The percentile answers "how are my users doing — including the unlucky ones?" Senior engineers instinctively reach for p95/p99, because those are the numbers that correlate with churn, abandoned carts, and angry tweets. The mean is for press releases; the tail is for engineering.

⚙️ How It Actually Works: Twitter's Fan-Out Problem

DDIA's signature example for "describing load" is Twitter circa 2012 (~4.6k tweets/sec average, ~12k/sec peak). The interesting number wasn't tweet volume — it was something subtler. Let's see why the right load parameter changes the entire architecture.

Twitter has two main operations:

  • Post a tweet — easy: ~4.6k writes/sec average.
  • View your home timeline — show recent tweets from everyone you follow: ~300k reads/sec.

Notice reads dwarf writes by ~65×. There are two ways to build the timeline.

Approach 1 — Fan-out on read (pull)

Store tweets in one big table. When you open your timeline, run a query: "find everyone I follow, find their recent tweets, merge by time." Simple to write — but at 300k requests/sec, each doing an expensive multi-way join, the read load is crushing.

Approach 2 — Fan-out on write (push)

Maintain a per-user "mailbox" (a cache of their timeline). When you post a tweet, immediately push a copy into the mailbox of every one of your followers. Reads become trivial — just read your pre-computed mailbox. But writes get expensive: posting one tweet might mean thousands of writes.

Animated architecture: one tweet fanning out to followers

Watch a single tweet from a user with a handful of followers get copied into each follower's timeline cache at write time. Reads (not shown) then become a cheap single lookup.

One write → four writes here. Fine. But what if the author has 30 million followers? Now a single tweet detonates into 30 million writes. That's the celebrity problem — and it's why the distribution of followers per user, not the raw tweet rate, is Twitter's true load parameter.

Step-through: how Twitter's hybrid approach actually works

Step 1 of 5 · A user posts a tweet
Someone hits "Tweet." The system must now make this tweet appear in the home timeline of everyone who follows them. The naive choice (recompute every timeline on read) is too expensive at 300k reads/sec, so Twitter precomputes timelines on write — mostly.

The math: why follower distribution is the real load parameter

Pure fan-out-on-write cost = (tweets/sec) × (average followers per tweeting user). The average might be ~75, but the distribution has a monstrous tail — a few accounts have tens of millions of followers. Those few accounts dominate the total write load all by themselves.

Fan-out-on-write total writes/sec = Σ (followers of each posting user) Ordinary user posts: 1 tweet → ~75 timeline writes ✅ cheap Celebrity posts: 1 tweet → 30,000,000 timeline writes ❌ a write storm Twitter's hybrid fix: • Normal accounts → fan-out on write (reads stay cheap) • Celebrity accounts → fan-out on read (skip the storm; merge in at read time) Lesson: the load parameter that matters here isn't "tweets/sec". It's the SHAPE of the followers-per-user distribution.

This is the whole point of "describing load": choosing the parameter that actually drives cost. Get that wrong and you'd scale tweet ingestion (already trivial) while the timeline fan-out quietly melts.

🏢 In the Wild

Twitter — the canonical hybrid timeline

The example above isn't hypothetical: it's Twitter's actual evolution, described by their engineers and immortalized in DDIA. They started with fan-out on read, hit a wall, switched to fan-out on write, then discovered the celebrity write-storm and landed on the hybrid they still use — push for the masses, pull-and-merge for the handful of accounts with enormous follower counts.

Amazon — the cult of p99.9

Amazon famously monitors the 99.9th percentile, not just p50, because the customers with the slowest requests are often those with the most data — i.e., the most valuable accounts who've bought the most. They also found that a 100 ms increase in latency measurably reduced sales. The Dynamo paper (the ancestor of DynamoDB) is built around tight tail-latency SLAs, not averages.

Google — tail at scale

Google's famous "The Tail at Scale" paper shows that when one user request fans out to hundreds of backend services, the slowest backend determines the user's latency — so even a rare p99 hiccup on each service becomes the common experience for the overall request. Their answer: techniques like hedged requests (send a duplicate to a second replica and take whichever returns first) to chop the tail.

Monitoring stacks — Prometheus, Datadog, Grafana

Every modern observability tool reports latency as histograms and percentiles (p50/p90/p95/p99), not just a mean — because the industry learned this lesson the hard way. When you see a Grafana panel with a p99 line above a p50 line, you're looking at today's lesson rendered in production.

CDNs & caches (Cloudflare, Redis)

The classic "cope with load" move you already know: a cache hit rate is itself a load parameter, and pushing reads to a CDN/edge cache is fan-out-on-read's cousin — precompute and store close to the reader so the expensive origin work happens rarely. (We'll dig into coping strategies tomorrow.)

🔍 The SDE 2 Lens

Where this shows up in your day job

You already touch all of this — you just may not have had the vocabulary to name it. Here's the translation.

  • Your "average latency" dashboard is lying to you. The next time someone says "p50 looks fine," ask for the p99. The bug reports that don't reproduce on your machine are usually living in the tail — that one slow query, that one user with 10× the data.
  • The N+1 query you keep getting flagged for in review. That's a fan-out-on-read problem in miniature: cheap to write, brutal at read time as data grows. The fix (a join, a batch fetch, or a denormalized/precomputed view) is fan-out-on-write thinking.
  • That materialized view / denormalized table your team maintains. Precomputing a result so reads are cheap, at the cost of more work and storage on write — that is fan-out on write. Now you know the trade-off you signed up for: write amplification and the risk of stale/duplicated data.
  • "Works fine locally, slow in prod." Prod has more data (load parameter changed) and real queueing/GC/noisy-neighbor effects (the random extras that create the tail). Local testing measures p50 in an empty system; prod is the p99 with a full cart.
  • The timeout you set on a downstream call. You're implicitly choosing a percentile: "I'll wait up to X ms, which covers 99% of healthy responses, and give up on the slow tail." Set it from the measured p99, not a vibe.
  • The "hot key" / "hot partition" incident. The celebrity-follower problem generalizes: any single key that's vastly more popular than the rest (a viral product, a giant tenant) is a skewed load parameter that breaks your nice uniform assumptions. (More in Day 46 · Skewed Workloads 🔒.)
💡 Ohhh, THAT'S why

...your SRE team set the SLO as "99% of requests under 300 ms" instead of "average under 300 ms." The percentile SLO is a promise to the unlucky users; the average SLO is a promise to nobody in particular. And it's why "just add a cache" is a real architectural decision, not a band-aid — you're moving cost from read time to write/refresh time.

🎯 System Design Interview Playbook

When this comes up

Constantly. The Twitter/Instagram/news-feed question is a top-5 system design classic and is literally this lesson. But the thinking applies everywhere: "Design a URL shortener / chat app / notification system — how does it scale?" Any time the interviewer says "now we have 100× the traffic," they're testing whether you can describe load and reason about performance precisely.

What to say (the checklist)

  • Quantify the load first, with the right parameter: "Let me estimate reads/sec vs writes/sec and the read:write ratio — and check whether there's a skew, like a few super-popular accounts, because that changes the design."
  • State performance goals as percentiles: "I'll target p99 < 200 ms, not just a good average, because tail latency is what users feel and it drives churn."
  • Reach for fan-out-on-write for read-heavy feeds: "Reads dominate, so I'll precompute timelines on write (push) to keep reads a single cheap lookup."
  • Pre-empt the celebrity / hot-key problem: "Pure push breaks for accounts with millions of followers, so I'll go hybrid — pull-and-merge for celebrities."
  • Separate throughput from latency: "Batch/async paths optimize throughput; the user-facing read path optimizes latency. I'll keep them on different tiers."

The trade-off to articulate

"There's a read-cost vs write-cost trade-off. Fan-out on write makes reads cheap by paying at write time and storing redundant copies; fan-out on read keeps storage lean but pays on every read. The right answer depends on the read:write ratio and the follower distribution — so I'd measure those, and likely go hybrid."

⚠️ Common candidate mistake

Calling a design "scalable" without ever defining load or performance, and quoting average latency. Also: picking fan-out-on-write and forgetting the celebrity write-storm — or picking fan-out-on-read and not noticing reads will dominate. Interviewers are specifically listening for whether you notice the skew.

⭐ Power move

Say: "I'd describe load with the parameter that actually drives cost — here it's the followers-per-user distribution, not raw tweet rate — and I'd hold the system to a p99 SLO, watching for tail-latency amplification since one page fans out to many backends. To cut the tail I'd consider hedged requests." Naming load parameters, p99, tail amplification, and hedged requests in one breath signals staff-level thinking and instantly separates you from the pack.

🤔 Pause & Think #1 — Why does a single page often feel slower than any one of its backend calls?

Each backend has a great p99. So why is the page slow?

Answer: This is tail-latency amplification. Suppose rendering one page requires 20 independent backend calls, and each backend is slow (above its p99) only 1% of the time. The page is fast only if all 20 are fast. Probability all 20 are fast ≈ 0.99²⁰ ≈ 0.82 — so roughly 18% of page loads hit at least one slow backend. A "1-in-100" event on each service becomes a "nearly 1-in-5" event for the user. That's why a fan-out architecture can feel sluggish even when every individual component looks healthy on its own dashboard, and why Google invests in tail-cutting tricks like hedged requests (fire a backup request, take the first to return). The lesson: at scale, you must engineer the tail, because the user experiences the maximum of many latencies, not the average of one.

🤔 Pause & Think #2 — Your CEO wants "the average response time under 100 ms." Why push back, and what do you propose instead?

It sounds like a perfectly reasonable goal. What's wrong with it?

Answer: An average target lets you "pass" while a meaningful slice of users suffers — you could hit a 90 ms average with a p99 of 4 seconds, and the average gives you no incentive to fix those 4-second users (who are often your highest-value, most-data customers). Averages are also easily skewed by a few outliers and tell you nothing about how many people had a bad time. Propose a percentile-based SLO instead: e.g., "p99 < 300 ms and p50 < 80 ms." That explicitly bounds the worst common experience. Bonus senior move: tie it to a business metric ("every 100 ms of added latency costs us ~X% conversion") and instrument with histograms so you can actually compute those percentiles correctly — note you can't average percentiles across servers; you need the underlying distribution (e.g., HDR histograms).

⚡ Quick Check #1

A startup's photo-sharing feed uses fan-out on write. It works great until a celebrity with 40M followers joins and every post causes timeouts and write spikes. Without abandoning fan-out-on-write for everyone, what's the fix?

Go hybrid. Keep fan-out on write for normal accounts (so the common case stays read-cheap), but flag high-follower accounts and skip their fan-out — instead, fetch their recent posts at read time and merge them into each viewer's precomputed timeline. The expensive write storm disappears for the handful of mega-accounts, while the millions of ordinary accounts still enjoy fast reads. The deeper lesson: the design must respond to the follower-distribution load parameter, treating the long tail (celebrities) differently from the body (everyone else).

⚡ Quick Check #2

You add one extremely slow request (10 seconds) to a batch of 100 requests that are all ~50 ms. Roughly what happens to the mean vs the p99, and which should you report?

The mean moves only a little: (99×50 ms + 10,000 ms) / 100 ≈ 149 ms — it nearly triples but still looks "fast-ish," and it describes nobody's actual experience (no request took 149 ms). The p99 jumps to capture the slow one: with 100 samples the 99th percentile lands right on that 10-second outlier. Report the percentiles (and ideally show p50, p95, p99 together). The mean smears the outlier across everyone and hides that one user had a catastrophic 10-second wait. This is exactly what the Percentile Lab above demonstrates — go add a spike and watch the gap.
