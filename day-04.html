Scalability — Describing Load & Performance

Chapter 1 · Reliable, Scalable, Maintainable Applications · Describing Load and Performance
Course progress4 / 109 (3.7%)

🐳 The Problem: The System That Was "Fast" Right Up Until It Died

Picture Twitter, circa 2008–2010. The product is simple: people post short messages, and when you open the app you see a home timeline — a merged, time-sorted feed of every tweet from everyone you follow. The engineering is also simple, at first. A tweet is one row in a table. Loading your timeline is one query: "find all tweets by the people I follow, sort by time, return the latest 100." On a laptop with ten users, it returns in a millisecond. Ship it.

Then growth happens. Millions of users. Hundreds of millions of tweets. And that one innocent query becomes a monster: for a user following 500 people, every single timeline load has to scan and merge tweets across 500 different users' posting histories, sorted by time — and it has to do this tens of thousands of times per second, because reading timelines is what people do all day. The database buckles. Latency climbs. The infamous "fail whale" becomes Twitter's unofficial mascot.

Here's the gut-punch: nobody wrote bad code. The query was correct. The servers were healthy. Reliability was fine — nothing failed. The system simply could not keep up with its own success. And when the on-call engineer pulled up the dashboard, the average response time looked totally acceptable — yet a huge slice of users were staring at a spinner.

💡 Why this should rewire how you think

Two separate lies are hiding in this story, and today we expose both. Lie #1: "Our system is scalable" — a meaningless sentence until you say scalable along which load dimension. Lie #2: "Our average latency is 100ms, so users are happy" — averages systematically hide the users who are suffering most. Scalability isn't a property you have or don't have. It's a precise conversation about load and performance — and today you learn to speak it fluently.

📖 What Is It? Two Vocabularies You Must Master

Day 1 introduced scalability as one of the three concerns. But "is the system scalable?" is, in Kleppmann's words, the wrong question — it's binary and lazy. The right question is: "If the system grows in a particular way, what are our options for coping with that growth?" To even ask that, you need two precise vocabularies.

Vocabulary 1 — Describing LOAD (load parameters)

Load is described with numbers called load parameters. The right choice depends entirely on your system's architecture. Examples:

  • Requests per second to a web server (throughput of incoming work).
  • Ratio of reads to writes in a database (is it read-heavy like Twitter timelines, or write-heavy like a logging system?).
  • Number of simultaneously active users in a chat room.
  • Hit rate on a cache.
  • And for Twitter specifically — the one that matters most — the distribution of followers per user (the fan-out).

The lesson: there is no universal "load." You pick the parameters that actually stress your design. Twitter's primary scaling challenge wasn't tweet volume (~4.6k writes/sec at the time) — it was the fan-out: each write of a tweet had to be delivered to all of that user's followers, and the average user has many followers.

Vocabulary 2 — Describing PERFORMANCE

Once load is defined, you ask two questions about performance:

  • When you increase load and keep resources fixed — how is performance affected? (Does latency climb? Does throughput plateau?)
  • When you increase load and want to keep performance unchanged — how much do you need to grow resources?

And here's the part most engineers get wrong. Performance is measured with two distinct ideas that people sloppily conflate:

TermWhat it actually means
ThroughputHow many things you process per unit time — e.g. records/sec in a batch job, or requests/sec for an online service. A volume.
Response timeThe time between a client sending a request and receiving the response. This is what the user experiences — it includes network delays and queueing delays, not just processing.
LatencyThe duration a request is waiting to be handled — latent, awaiting service. Not the same as response time, though often used interchangeably.
💡 The single most important idea today

Response time is not a single number — it's a distribution. The same request, asked a thousand times, comes back with a thousand slightly different times: most fast, a few wildly slow (a GC pause hit, a TCP retransmit, a cache miss, a noisy neighbour). So you must never describe performance with an average (the arithmetic mean). Use percentiles instead: the median (p50), and especially the tail — p95, p99, p999. The tail is where your most important, most active customers live.

🧠 The Analogy: The Coffee Shop & the Airport Security Line

Load → the morning rush at a coffee shop

A café that serves 10 customers an hour is trivially "scalable." Now make it a Monday-morning rush near a train station: 300 customers in 20 minutes, half ordering complicated oat-milk-half-caf drinks, a delivery courier needing 40 cups at once. Suddenly "can the café handle the load?" splinters into specific questions: customers per minute? drinks per order? ratio of simple coffees to elaborate ones? do bulk orders block the line? Those are your load parameters. The barista (your server) didn't change — the shape of the demand did, and only by naming its dimensions can you decide whether to add a second espresso machine or a second barista.

Mapping: customers/minute = requests/sec; simple vs elaborate drinks = read/write mix; the 40-cup courier order = a celebrity tweet fanning out to millions; one barista = fixed resources. "Is the café scalable?" is unanswerable; "can it serve 15 drinks/min with a 30% bulk-order rate?" has an answer.

Performance → the airport security line (why averages lie)

You arrive at airport security. The sign says "average wait: 8 minutes." Reassured, you stroll up — and get stuck behind a family of six with a stroller, a laptop that won't come out of its bag, and a forgotten water bottle. You wait 35 minutes and miss your flight. The average was honest; it just didn't describe you. Most travellers really did clear in ~8 minutes (that pulled the average down), but the unlucky tail waited far longer — and the tail is exactly the people who write angry reviews and miss flights.

Mapping: each traveller = a request; clearing security = a response; the 8-minute average = the misleading mean; the 35-minute stuck traveller = the p99 tail latency. The brutal twist: the customers who hit the slow tail are often your heaviest users — the ones with the most luggage (most data, most items in cart, most followers). Amazon found exactly this: the slowest requests belonged to customers with the most purchase history, i.e. the most valuable ones.

💡 The aha

And here's tail latency amplification, the part that breaks intuition: if loading one airport gate's status requires checking every security lane, then your wait is the slowest lane, not the average one. In a system where one user request fans out to many backend calls, the more backends you touch, the higher the chance at least one hits its slow tail — so the end-to-end p99 gets worse as you add backends. Averaging hides this entirely; only percentiles reveal it.

⚙️ How It Actually Works

Let's make both halves concrete. First the load side — Twitter's famous fan-out problem and how they re-architected around it. Then the performance side — an interactive look at why percentiles, not averages, tell the truth.

Animated architecture: Twitter's fan-out-on-write

The fix for the read-melts-the-database problem was to flip the work to write time. When a user posts a tweet, a fan-out service immediately inserts that tweet into a precomputed home-timeline cache for each follower. Now reading a timeline is dirt cheap — just read your own ready-made cache. Watch one tweet fan out:

One write, many deliveries. The cost moved from read time (paid on every timeline load, which is constant) to write time (paid once per tweet). Since reads vastly outnumber writes on Twitter, this is a huge win — until a celebrity with 30 million followers tweets, and that single write explodes into 30 million cache inserts. We'll see the fix for that next.

Step-through: the two fan-out strategies (and the hybrid)

Step 1 of 4 · Approach 1 — fan-out on read
The naïve design. A tweet is just one inserted row. But loading a home timeline means querying every followed account, merging all their recent tweets, and sorting by time — on every single load. Writes are cheap; reads are brutally expensive. With timeline reads outnumbering posts ~100:1, this is the design that summoned the fail whale.

The math: why moving work to write-time wins (usually)

The whole decision turns on the read:write ratio and the fan-out. Twitter measured ~300k timeline reads/sec but only ~4.6k tweet-writes/sec — reads dominate by ~65×. Paying the fan-out cost once per write is far cheaper than paying it on every read:

Let R = timeline reads/sec, W = tweet writes/sec, F = avg fan-out (followers) Fan-out on READ: cheap writes (1 insert), expensive reads (merge F feeds) total work ≈ R × F + W × 1 Fan-out on WRITE: expensive writes (F inserts), cheap reads (1 lookup) total work ≈ R × 1 + W × F Twitter (R ≈ 300k, W ≈ 4.6k, F ≈ a few hundred): on READ ≈ 300k × F ← huge, paid constantly on WRITE ≈ 4.6k × F ← ~65× cheaper ⇒ Fan-out on WRITE wins... until F is a celebrity's 30,000,000. Then ONE write = 30M inserts. Solution: HYBRID.

⚖️ The Trade-offs

The fan-out decision is the cleanest trade-off in the chapter: you're choosing when to pay for delivery — at read time or at write time — and the right answer flips depending on your workload. Play with it.

And the performance trade-off: which percentile do you optimize?

Chasing higher percentiles has sharply diminishing returns and rising cost. Going from p99 to p999 might mean optimizing the 1-in-1000 request — and Amazon decided p999 was worth it (those are the highest-value customers) but p9999 was not (the cost outweighed the benefit). You choose your SLO target by asking how much is this percentile worth to the business?

🏢 In the Wild

Twitter — the canonical fan-out case study

Exactly the story above. Twitter started with fan-out-on-read, hit the fail whale, and switched to fan-out-on-write with precomputed home-timeline caches. When that broke for celebrities, they went hybrid: tweets from users with enormous followings are not fanned out; instead they're fetched and merged into the timeline at read time. The load parameter that drove the entire architecture was the distribution of followers per user — not raw request volume.

Amazon — the p999 rule and "every 100ms costs 1% of sales"

Amazon describes internal-service response-time requirements in terms of the 99.9th percentile, even though it affects only 1 in 1000 requests — because those slow requests hit customers with the most data (the most orders), who are the most valuable. They also famously measured that every 100ms of added latency cost ~1% in sales. Performance here is revenue, not vanity.

Google — tail latency & "The Tail at Scale"

Google's research crystallized tail-latency amplification: a single search query fans out to thousands of leaf servers, and the user waits for the slowest. To fight it they use tricks like hedged requests (send the same request to two replicas, take whichever answers first) so one slow node can't dominate the end-to-end p99.

Monitoring tools — percentiles everywhere

Look at any modern observability stack — Prometheus/Grafana, Datadog, AWS CloudWatch — and SLO dashboards are defined in p50/p95/p99/p999, not averages. Computing percentiles efficiently over streaming data uses algorithms like forward decay, t-digest, and HdrHistogram (you can't just keep every sample). DDIA warns: don't average percentiles across machines — that's mathematically meaningless; you must aggregate the underlying histograms.

CDNs & load testing (k6, Gatling, wrk)

When you load-test, the tool generates requests at a target rate and reports the latency distribution. A subtle trap DDIA flags is head-of-line blocking: if your test client waits for each response before sending the next, a few slow responses artificially throttle the offered load and hide queueing delays — masking the very tail you're trying to measure (this is the "coordinated omission" problem).

🔍 The SDE 2 Lens

Where this shows up in your day job

You touch load and performance vocabulary constantly — you just may not have named it. Here's the translation.

  • "It's fast on my machine / in staging." Of course it is — load = 1, fan-out = 0, no queueing. Performance only means something under a described load. The senior reflex: "fast at what request rate and what data size?"
  • Your Grafana/Datadog dashboard showing p50, p95, p99. Now you know why the panel isn't "average latency": the average is the metric that lets a bad p99 hide. The alert that fires on "p99 > 500ms" is protecting your unhappiest users.
  • That one endpoint that's "usually fine but sometimes hangs." Classic tail latency — a GC pause, a lock, a cold cache, a slow downstream. Averaging makes it invisible; you have to look at the tail to even see it exists.
  • The N+1 query you got dinged for in review. That's a fan-out-on-read in miniature: one logical read explodes into N database round-trips. The fix (batch / join / precompute / denormalize) is literally Twitter's fan-out decision at function scale.
  • A microservice call that aggregates 8 downstream services. Tail-latency amplification in your own codebase: your endpoint's p99 is roughly the worst of the 8, not the average. Adding a 9th dependency makes your tail worse, not just your average.
  • Caching, read-replicas, denormalized tables, materialized views. Every one of these is "move work to write-time so reads are cheap" — the exact pattern behind fan-out-on-write.
💡 Ohhh, THAT'S why

...your SRE keeps saying "we're SLO-green on the average but burning error budget on p99," and why the perf fix that "worked" in the benchmark did nothing in prod. The benchmark measured throughput at low concurrency; prod has queueing, fan-out, and a fat tail. Different load → different performance. They're not contradicting each other; they measured two different load points.

🎯 System Design Interview Playbook

When this comes up

This is the back-of-the-envelope estimation and "how does it scale?" dimension of every design round. It's front-and-center in "Design Twitter / a news feed," "Design Instagram," "Design a URL shortener at scale," and any time the interviewer says "now we have 100M users." The fan-out feed problem is one of the most-asked questions in the entire interview canon.

What to say (the checklist)

  • Define load parameters first. "Let me estimate: DAU, requests/sec, read:write ratio, and the data-size and fan-out distribution. Those drive the design." Stating QPS and read:write up front is an instant senior signal.
  • Reach for the fan-out framing on any feed/timeline. "Reads dominate, so I'd fan out on write — precompute each user's feed — and fall back to fan-out-on-read for high-follower accounts (hybrid), because the follower distribution is heavily skewed."
  • Talk performance in percentiles, never averages. "I'd set an SLO like p99 < 200ms, because the average hides the tail, and the tail is often our most valuable users."
  • Name tail-latency amplification on fan-out reads. "Since this request hits N services, end-to-end p99 ≈ the worst of N. I'd reduce dependencies on the hot path, parallelize, and consider hedged requests."
  • Separate throughput from latency. "Adding machines raises throughput, but a single slow request's latency won't improve just by adding capacity — that needs queue/fan-out fixes."

The trade-off to articulate

"Fan-out-on-write trades cheap reads for expensive, spiky writes; fan-out-on-read does the reverse. With a read-heavy, skewed-follower workload, a hybrid is optimal. Separately, chasing ever-higher percentiles has diminishing returns — I'd pick the SLO percentile by its business value, like Amazon optimizing p999 but not p9999."

⚠️ Common candidate mistake

Saying "we'll just add more servers / it'll auto-scale" without naming a load parameter, and quoting average latency. Both scream junior. Adding servers is the coping-with-load answer (tomorrow), but it's meaningless until you've described the load you're coping with — and "average latency of 50ms" tells the interviewer nothing about your unhappy users.

⭐ Power move

Do the fan-out math out loud and pivot to the hybrid: "300k reads/sec vs 5k writes/sec means I fan out on write — except a celebrity's 30M-follower tweet would be a 30M-write storm, so those I fan out on read and merge at query time." Then close on performance: "I'll measure p99, watch for tail amplification across my fan-out, and pick the SLO percentile by business value." That arc — load parameters → fan-out math → hybrid → percentile SLO → tail amplification — is a textbook staff-level answer.

✓ Truth

✗ Myth

"Latency and response time are the same thing."

✓ Truth

Response time is what the client sees end-to-end (processing + network + queueing). Latency is the duration a request spends waiting to be serviced. Queueing delay often dominates the tail — and is why a server near capacity has a brutal p99 even if its CPU work is fast.

✗ Myth

"To average p99 across our 10 servers, just average their p99 values."

✓ Truth

🤔 Pause & Think #1 — Why did Twitter pick fan-out-on-write when writes are clearly more expensive that way?

It seems backwards to make the operation that touches more rows the "cheap path." Why is it right?

Answer: Because you don't pay for work in the abstract — you pay for it multiplied by how often it happens. The load parameters were lopsided: ~300k timeline reads/sec versus ~4.6k tweet-writes/sec, a ~65:1 read:write ratio. Total work = reads × read-cost + writes × write-cost. Fan-out-on-read makes every one of those 300k reads expensive; fan-out-on-write makes only the 4.6k writes expensive. Even though a single write now does far more work, you do far fewer of them, so total system work plummets — and, just as importantly, the expensive work happens off the user's critical read path, so the latency the user feels (timeline load) drops to a single cache read. The lesson: optimize the operation that's on the hot path and happens most often, even if it means making the rarer operation heavier.

🤔 Pause & Think #2 — Your service's p50 is 20ms but its p99 is 900ms. The CPU is at 40%. What's going on, and does adding servers fix it?

Low median, ugly tail, plenty of headroom. Diagnose it.

Answer: A big p50→p99 gap with spare CPU is the classic signature of tail effects, not raw compute load. Likely culprits: (1) queueing delay — requests arrive in bursts; even at 40% average utilization, momentary spikes form a queue and the unlucky requests at the back wait (head-of-line blocking, especially with a few slow requests hogging connections); (2) GC pauses / lock contention / cold caches hitting a small fraction of requests; (3) a slow downstream dependency on the tail; (4) tail-latency amplification if the request fans out. Does adding servers fix it? Partially and indirectly — more servers lower per-node utilization, shrinking queueing delay, which can help the tail. But it won't fix a per-request GC pause, a slow dependency, or amplification — those need targeted fixes (tune GC, batch/parallelize, hedge requests, isolate slow tenants). The deeper point: latency and throughput are different axes. "Add capacity" is a throughput lever; a fat tail at low utilization is usually a latency/queueing problem.

⚡ Quick Check #1

A request to your API fans out to 6 independent backend services in parallel; each has a p99 of 100ms (1% chance a call exceeds 100ms). Roughly what's the chance your end-to-end response exceeds 100ms — and what does that do to your p99?

You wait for the slowest of the 6, so you're slow if any of them is slow. P(at least one slow) = 1 − P(all fast) = 1 − (0.99)⁶ ≈ 1 − 0.941 ≈ ~5.9%. So a per-service 1-in-100 tail becomes a roughly 1-in-17 tail end-to-end — your composite p99 is now worse than any single backend's p99. This is tail-latency amplification: the more backends a single request touches, the more your tail degrades. Fixes: reduce fan-out on the hot path, hedge requests (send to 2 replicas, take the first), or set tight per-call timeouts with fallbacks.

⚡ Quick Check #2

Your boss says "make the system scalable." What's the most useful thing you can say back?

Turn the boolean into parameters: "Scalable along which dimension? Are we expecting growth in requests/sec, in total data size, in concurrent users, or in fan-out? And what performance must we hold — say, p99 under 200ms — as that grows?" Only after defining the load parameter and the performance target (as a percentile) can you reason about coping strategies. "Make it scalable" with no load dimension and no SLO is an unanswerable request — and naming that is the senior move.

✅ Key Takeaways

"Is it scalable?" is the wrong question. Scalability isn't binary. Ask: "if load grows along this parameter, what are our options for coping?" Always name the load dimension first.
Describe load with parameters. Requests/sec, read:write ratio, active users, cache hit rate, fan-out distribution — pick the ones that actually stress your architecture. Twitter's was the follower distribution.
Response time is a distribution, not a number — use percentiles. The mean hides the tail. Report p50/p95/p99/p999; the tail is where your heaviest, most valuable users live.
Fan-out: choose where to pay. Read-heavy → fan-out on write (cheap reads). Huge/skewed fan-out → hybrid. It's the same "move work to write time" instinct behind caches, replicas, and denormalization.
Beware tail-latency amplification. When one request fans out to N backends, end-to-end p99 ≈ the worst of N. More dependencies = worse tail. And never average percentiles — aggregate histograms.
