Scalability — When "It Works" Isn't Enough

Chapter 1 · Reliable, Scalable, Maintainable Applications · Describing Load & Performance
Course progress4 / 109 (3.7%)

🌩️ The Problem: The Tweet That Took Twitter Down

It's 2010. A Twitter engineer is staring at a graph that won't stop climbing. The product is simple: people post 140-character messages, and when you open the app you see a merged feed of everyone you follow. Easy, right? The first version did exactly what you'd write on a whiteboard — when you load your home timeline, run a query: "give me all tweets from everyone I follow, sorted by time."

SELECT tweets.*, users.* FROM tweets JOIN users ON tweets.sender_id = users.id JOIN follows ON follows.followee_id = users.id WHERE follows.follower_id = current_user;

It worked beautifully... on day one. But Twitter wasn't being hit by one kind of load. It was being hit by two, and they grew in different shapes. People posted ~4,600 tweets per second on average. But people opened their timelines ~300,000 times per second — sixty-five times more often. And every single one of those 300k requests fired that monster join across a firehose of tweets. The database melted. Adding servers barely helped. The "fail whale" became a meme.

💡 Why this should grab you

Nothing here was broken. No bug, no crash, no human typo (that was yesterday). The code was correct. It just didn't scale — it worked at 1,000 users and fell over at 100 million. Reliability asks "does it work?". Scalability asks a sharper question: "when the load grows 10×, what's our plan?" And here's the kicker — you can't even answer that question until you can describe load and performance with real numbers. That's today's entire mission.

📖 What Is It? Describing Load & Performance

Scalability is a system's ability to cope with increased load. But notice: "scalability" is not a one-dimensional label you stamp on a system. Saying "X is scalable" or "Y doesn't scale" is almost meaningless. The real question Kleppmann insists you ask is:

💡 The two questions that define scalability

1. "If the load grows in this particular way, what are our options for coping with it?"
2. "If we add resources, how can we keep performance steady?"

You can't reason about either until you can put numbers on two things: load and performance. Today is about learning that vocabulary precisely — because vague words ("a lot of traffic", "it's slow sometimes") are how engineers fool themselves.

Step 1 — Describe the load with load parameters

A load parameter is a number that captures what's stressing your system. The right parameter depends entirely on your architecture. Examples from the book:

  • Requests per second to a web server (throughput of an online service)
  • Ratio of reads to writes in a database
  • Number of simultaneously active users in a chat room
  • Hit rate on a cache
  • For Twitter specifically: the distribution of followers per user — this turned out to be the load parameter that mattered most

The skill is picking the parameter that actually drives your bottleneck. For Twitter the headline number (tweets/sec) was a red herring; the fan-out — how many timelines each tweet must reach — was the real beast.

Step 2 — Describe performance: throughput vs response time

Two different lenses, for two different kinds of systems:

MetricWhat it measuresUsed for
ThroughputRecords processed per second, or total time to run a job over a datasetBatch systems (e.g. Hadoop, Day 80)
Response timeThe time between a client sending a request and receiving the response — what the user actually feelsOnline systems (web, APIs)
💡 Response time ≠ latency

People use these interchangeably; DDIA does not. Response time is what the client sees: service time + network delays + queueing delay. Latency is the duration a request is waiting to be handled — latent, awaiting service. The queueing part is huge and sneaky: a request can be fast to process but slow to respond simply because it sat in a queue behind a slow neighbor.

Step 3 — The big idea: don't use the average. Use percentiles.

Response time is not a single number — it's a distribution. Run the same request a thousand times and you'll get a thousand different answers, because of context switches, network hiccups, garbage collection pauses, a TCP retransmit, a page fault. So how do you summarize it?

The average (mean) is the wrong tool — it tells you nothing about how many users had a bad experience, and a single 10-second outlier drags it around. Instead, sort your response times and use percentiles:

  • p50 (median): half your requests are faster, half slower. The "typical" experience.
  • p95 / p99 / p999: the tail. p99 = 99% of requests are faster than this; 1 in 100 is slower. These are your worst experiences — and often your most valuable customers, because the people with the most data (most friends, most orders, most history) are the ones whose requests are slowest.

Amazon famously tracks p99.9 (the slowest 1 in 1,000) because those customers often have the most purchases. They've observed that a 100ms increase in response time drops sales by ~1%. The tail isn't a rounding error — it's revenue.

🧠 The Analogy: The Coffee Shop & the Airport Security Line

Percentiles → the morning coffee queue

Imagine you run a coffee shop and want to brag about speed. You could say "the average wait is 90 seconds." But that average hides everything. Most customers order a drip coffee and are out in 30 seconds (your p50). But every so often someone orders six custom oat-milk caramel macchiatos, and everyone behind them waits five minutes (your p99). If you only watch the average, you'll never see those furious customers at the back — yet they're the ones writing the one-star reviews.

Mapping: each customer = a request; the drip-coffee crowd = your fast common case (p50); the six-macchiato order = a heavy request (a user with millions of followers); the people stuck behind them = head-of-line blocking; the one-star reviews = lost revenue from tail latency. The fix isn't a faster barista on average — it's making sure one giant order can't freeze the whole line.

Tail latency amplification → the group flight

You and seven friends are flying out together, and you've agreed: nobody boards until everyone clears airport security. Seven of you breeze through in 3 minutes. But one friend gets randomly pulled for extra screening and takes 20 minutes. How long until your group is ready? 20 minutes. The group is only as fast as its slowest member.

Mapping: your group's wait = one user-facing request that internally fans out to 8 backend services in parallel; each friend = one backend call; the random extra screening = a normal-but-occasional slow response. Even if each backend is slow only 1% of the time, with 8 parallel calls the chance that at least one is slow is ~8%. This is tail latency amplification: the more services a single request touches, the more often it hits somebody's tail — so the p99 of the whole request is far worse than the p99 of any single backend.

💡 The aha

Both analogies smash the same illusion: averages lie, and the tail is where the pain lives. A system can have a great average and still make a meaningful slice of users miserable — and in a microservices world, the more pieces a request touches, the more the tail dominates the experience.

⚙️ How It Actually Works

Let's make this concrete with Twitter's actual fix, then drill into how percentiles are computed. Twitter's insight: don't do expensive work at read time (300k/s) when you can do cheaper work at write time (4.6k/s). This is fan-out on write — precompute every user's home timeline as a cache, so reading it is a single cheap lookup.

Animated architecture: fan-out on write

When a user posts a tweet, the system looks up all their followers and pushes the tweet into each follower's pre-computed timeline cache (amber = the write fanning out). Later, when a follower opens the app, the read (green) is a single cheap lookup — no join, no merge. We've moved the cost from the hot read path to the cooler write path.

Amber = the write fanning out to every follower's cache (expensive, but only 4.6k/s). Green = the read, now a single lookup (cheap, and it happens 300k/s). The trade-off: a tweet by someone with 30M followers triggers 30M cache writes — the celebrity problem, which we'll explore in the trade-off explorer below.

The math of tail latency amplification

This is the formula every staff engineer keeps in their back pocket. If a single backend serves p99 = "1% of calls are slow," and one user request fans out to N parallel backend calls (and waits for all of them), the probability the whole request hits at least one slow call is:

P(request is slow) = 1 − (P one call is fast)^N = 1 − (0.99)^N N = 1 backend: 1 − 0.99^1 ≈ 1.0% ← p99 of one service N = 10 backends: 1 − 0.99^10 ≈ 9.6% N = 100 backends: 1 − 0.99^100 ≈ 63.4% ← now MOST requests are slow! Lesson: the more services one request touches, the more the tail dominates. A "rare" 1% slowdown becomes the common case once you fan out wide. This is why p99 of the whole > p99 of the parts.

⚖️ The Trade-offs

Twitter's two designs sit at opposite ends of a spectrum, and the "right" answer flips depending on a single load parameter: how many followers users have. Fan-out on write precomputes timelines (cheap reads, expensive writes). Fan-out on read does the join at query time (cheap writes, expensive reads). Neither wins universally — and the real Twitter uses a hybrid.

Side-by-side: the two designs

DimensionFan-out on write (precompute)Fan-out on read (query-time join)
Write costHigh — one write per followerLow — single insert
Read costLow — single cache lookupHigh — join across all followees

💡 The deeper lesson

This is the universal trade-off of precompute vs compute-on-demand — you'll meet it again in materialized views (Day 97), denormalization, caching, and CQRS. The question is always: do I pay the cost when data changes, or when it's read? The answer depends on your read/write ratio and the shape of your data.

🏢 In the Wild

Twitter — the canonical hybrid fan-out

Twitter (now X) uses fan-out on write for the vast majority of users, but maintains an exception list: accounts with millions of followers (celebrities) are not fanned out. When you load your timeline, the system reads your precomputed cache and separately fetches recent tweets from the handful of celebrities you follow, then merges them at read time. Best of both worlds, driven by the follower-count distribution being their key load parameter.

Amazon — p99.9 and "every 100ms costs 1% of sales"

Amazon optimizes for high percentiles (p99.9) rather than averages, precisely because their highest-value customers tend to have the most account activity — and therefore the slowest requests. They quantified that a 100ms latency increase reduced sales by about 1%, which is why tail latency is treated as a revenue metric, not an engineering vanity number.

Google & the "Tail at Scale"

Google's famous paper The Tail at Scale formalized tail-latency amplification and the fixes: hedged requests (send the same request to two replicas, take whichever returns first, cancel the other) and tied requests. When a single search touches hundreds of leaf servers, you will hit someone's slow tail on nearly every query — so you design around it instead of pretending it's rare.

Monitoring stacks — Prometheus, HdrHistogram, t-digest

Computing percentiles correctly at scale is non-trivial — you can't store every sample. Libraries like HdrHistogram and the t-digest algorithm approximate percentiles cheaply with bounded memory, and they're built into tools like Prometheus, Datadog, and Envoy. Crucial rule they all respect: you cannot average percentiles together (more on that in the misconceptions).

SLOs / SLAs — percentiles as contracts

Service Level Objectives are written in percentiles, not averages: e.g. "p99 latency < 200ms and 99.9% availability over 30 days." Cloud providers' SLAs (AWS, GCP) and every serious internal service define their reliability targets this way — the percentile vocabulary you learned today is literally the language of production contracts.

🔍 The SDE 2 Lens

Where this shows up in your day job

You've been swimming in this vocabulary without naming it. Once you see it, your dashboards and incident reviews will read completely differently.

  • That Grafana/Datadog dashboard showing "p95 latency." Now you know why it's p95 and not "avg latency" — the average hides the users having a bad time. When someone asks "is the service healthy?", the right answer cites a percentile and a time window, not a single mean.
  • The alert that fired even though "average looks fine." Classic. Your p50 is flat but your p99 doubled — a subset of requests (the big customers, the cold cache, the GC pauses) got slow. Averages would never have caught it.
  • Your microservice that calls 6 other services to render one response. That's tail-latency amplification in your own codebase. If each dependency is p99 = 50ms, your endpoint's p99 is worse than 50ms — and it gets worse with every dependency you add. This is the hidden cost of "just add another service call."
  • The cache you added to speed up a hot read path. You instinctively did fan-out-on-write: precompute the answer so reads are cheap. Materialized views, denormalized tables, Redis caches — all the same move. Now you can articulate the trade-off (stale data, write amplification) instead of just "it's faster."
  • "It's slow but I can't reproduce it." Tail latency. It's the 1-in-100 request — a queueing delay, a noisy neighbor, a lock contention spike. You reproduce it by load-testing and looking at the distribution, not by hitting it once and seeing a fast response.
💡 Ohhh, THAT'S why

...your SRE team rejects "average response time" in design docs and insists on "p99 under expected peak load." And why "add a read replica" is the reflexive fix for a read-heavy service but useless for a write-heavy one. You're not memorizing rules — you're reading the load parameters.

🎯 System Design Interview Playbook

When this comes up

Constantly — it's the backbone of every design interview. "Design Twitter / a news feed / Instagram" is the literal example. But the load-and-performance framing applies to "Design a URL shortener," "Design a rate limiter," "Design a notification system" — any question where the interviewer says "now it has 100 million users."

What to say (the checklist)

  • Quantify the load first. "Let me estimate: ~X writes/sec, ~Y reads/sec, read:write ratio of Z, with this distribution of object sizes / follower counts." Naming load parameters up front is a senior signal.
  • Identify the dominant load parameter. "The bottleneck here isn't total tweets — it's fan-out, driven by the follower distribution." Show you can find the one number that drives the architecture.
  • Talk performance in percentiles. "I'd target p99 < 200ms at peak load, not average — and I'd watch p999 for the heavy-hitter users." Never say "fast." Say a percentile and a target.
  • Pick precompute vs on-demand deliberately. "Reads dominate 65:1, so I'll fan out on write and precompute timelines, with a hybrid exception for high-fan-out accounts."
  • Name tail amplification when fanning out. "Since one request touches N services, I'll use timeouts, hedged requests, and limit fan-out depth to control the p99."

The trade-off to articulate

"It's precompute-vs-compute-on-read. Fan-out on write makes reads cheap but writes expensive and storage larger; fan-out on read is the reverse. I'd choose based on the read/write ratio and the fan-out distribution, and go hybrid at the extremes — exactly how real Twitter handles celebrities."

⚠️ Common candidate mistake

Optimizing the average and ignoring the tail — or worse, never quantifying load at all and jumping straight to boxes. Also: forgetting that "add more servers" doesn't help a correlated hotspot like a celebrity post; that's a fan-out problem, not a capacity problem. And quoting a "p99" you got by averaging the p99s of two machines (mathematically meaningless).

⭐ Power move

Drop tail-latency amplification with the math: "Because this request fans out to ~20 services, even 1%-slow backends mean ~18% of requests hit a slow tail — so I'll add hedged requests and a fan-out budget." Then tie performance to money: "Per Amazon, +100ms ≈ −1% sales, so the tail is a revenue metric." Connecting percentiles → architecture → business impact is a staff-level signal almost nobody gives.

✗ Myth

"Average response time tells me how my system is performing."

✓ Truth

The average (mean) hides the tail and gets dragged by outliers. It tells you nothing about how many users had a bad experience. Use the median (p50) for the typical case and p95/p99/p999 for the tail. A great average can coexist with thousands of furious users.

✗ Myth

"A system is either 'scalable' or it isn't — it's a property you can label."

✓ Truth

Scalability is meaningless as a one-word label. The real question is "if load grows in this specific way, what are our options?" An architecture that scales beautifully for reads may collapse under writes. Always tie scalability to a specific load parameter and growth pattern.

✗ Myth

"To get the p99 of my whole fleet, I average the p99 of each server."

✓ Truth

You cannot average percentiles — it's mathematically wrong. To aggregate, you must combine the underlying histograms (e.g. HdrHistogram / t-digest) and recompute the percentile from the merged distribution. Averaging p99s produces a number that means nothing.

✗ Myth

"My backends are each fast (p99 = 50ms), so my endpoint that calls all of them is also ~50ms."

✓ Truth

Tail-latency amplification: if you wait for N parallel calls, the chance of hitting at least one slow tail grows fast. With 20 backends at p99=50ms, ~18% of your requests are slow. The whole is far worse than its parts — design with timeouts and hedged requests.

For decades the answer to load was a more powerful single machine (scaling up). Simple, but you hit a ceiling — and one box is a single point of failure. Still valid: many systems scale up surprisingly far before needing distribution.

Dean & Barroso formalized tail-latency amplification and the techniques to fight it (hedged/tied requests, request reissue). It cemented percentiles — not averages — as the language of large-scale performance.
🤔 Pause & Think #1 — Why does optimizing the average response time often make the tail worse?

It feels like making the average better should help everyone. Why might it hurt your slowest users?

Answer: Because the cheapest way to lower the average is to make your already-fast common-case requests slightly faster — there are millions of them, so the mean drops nicely. But the slow tail is caused by fundamentally different things: queueing behind heavy requests, GC pauses, cold caches, users with huge data sets, lock contention. None of those are touched by shaving microseconds off the fast path. Worse, some "average" optimizations actively harm the tail — e.g. batching requests together raises throughput (better average) but adds queueing delay that inflates p99. The senior move is to optimize the tail specifically: isolate heavy requests so they can't block light ones (no head-of-line blocking), set timeouts, use hedged requests, and shard hot keys. You manage the average and the tail as separate goals, because they have separate causes.

🤔 Pause & Think #2 — Twitter's average is 4.6k tweets/sec. Why is that number almost useless for capacity planning?

If the average write rate is 4.6k/s, why can't you just provision for ~5k/s and call it done?

Answer: Three reasons, all about distribution. (1) Peak vs average: the average smears across quiet 3 AM hours and live-event spikes. Twitter saw bursts to ~12k/s during big events; you must provision for the peak, not the mean — or you fall over exactly when you're most visible. (2) Fan-out amplification: 4.6k tweets/sec isn't 4.6k units of work — under fan-out-on-write, each tweet multiplies by the poster's follower count. A normal user's tweet might be 200 cache writes; a celebrity's is 30 million. The real write load is the tweet rate × the follower distribution. (3) The distribution's shape matters more than its center: a few accounts with astronomical follower counts dominate the tail of work. This is why Kleppmann says the key load parameter for Twitter is the distribution of followers per user — a single average throws away exactly the information that determines whether your architecture survives.

⚡ Quick Check #1

Your service's average latency is 40ms and looks healthy on the dashboard, but the support queue is full of "the app is slow" complaints. What single metric change would most likely reveal the problem, and why?

Switch from average to high percentiles (p95/p99/p999). The average being 40ms is fully consistent with, say, p99 = 3,000ms — meaning 1 in 100 requests takes three seconds. Those are real users having a terrible time, but they're statistically invisible in the mean. Plot the latency distribution over time and you'll likely see a fat tail (often correlated with peak load, GC pauses, a slow dependency, or your biggest customers). Bonus: segment percentiles by customer/endpoint to find who is in the tail.

⚡ Quick Check #2

A read-heavy news feed (50 reads per write) is slow on reads. A teammate says "let's fan out on read with a join." Another says "precompute the feed on write." Who's right, and what's the one case that breaks their answer?

Precompute on write is right for a read-heavy workload: you do the expensive merge once per write (rare) instead of on every read (frequent), turning reads into cheap single lookups. The case that breaks it: a celebrity / very high fan-out author. One post by an account with millions of followers triggers millions of cache writes — a write storm. The production answer is a hybrid: precompute for normal authors, but exclude high-fan-out accounts and merge their posts in at read time. The right design depends on the follower distribution, not just the average read/write ratio.

✅ Key Takeaways

"Scalable" is not a label — it's a question. Always ask "if load grows in this specific way, what are our options?" Tie scalability to a concrete load parameter and growth pattern.
Pick the load parameter that drives your bottleneck. For Twitter it wasn't tweet rate — it was the distribution of followers per user. The headline number is often a red herring.
Throughput for batch, response time for online. And response time ≠ latency: response time is what the client feels, including queueing delay behind slow neighbors.
Use percentiles, never the average. p50 for the typical case, p95/p99/p999 for the tail. The tail is where your most valuable users live — and you can't average percentiles together.
Fan-out is precompute vs compute-on-read. Read-heavy → fan out on write (cheap reads). Huge fan-out (celebrities) → fan out on read. Real systems go hybrid. And beware tail-latency amplification when one request touches many services.
