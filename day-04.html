Scalability — Describing Load & Performance

Chapter 1 · Reliable, Scalable, Maintainable Applications · Load Parameters, Throughput & Percentiles
Course progress4 / 109 (3.7%)

🌩️ The Problem: Your Dashboard Says "All Green" While Users Rage-Quit

It's launch day. Your team ships a new feed feature. The on-call dashboard glows reassuringly: average response time: 120 ms. CPU is fine, error rate is near zero. Everyone exhales.

Then support lights up. "The app is unusable." "It hangs for five seconds." "I tapped refresh and nothing happened." You stare at the dashboard. Average is still 120 ms. How can both be true?

Here's the trap: your average is being computed across millions of fast requests and a few thousand catastrophically slow ones. The slow ones get drowned out arithmetically — but they belong to real people. And it gets worse: the users hitting those slow requests are often your most valuable ones — the power users with the most data, the most followers, the fullest carts. The single number on your dashboard is lying to you, and it's lying about the people who matter most.

💡 Why this should bother you

"Is the system fast?" and "Can the system handle more load?" are not vibes — they're measurements, and the way you choose to measure them determines whether you can even see your problem. Pick the wrong metric (like the average) and you'll confidently optimize a system that's quietly failing your best customers. Today is about learning to describe load and performance precisely — because you can't scale what you can't name.

📖 What Is It? Scalability as a Question, Not a Label

Over the last three days we covered reliability — keeping the system working correctly even when things go wrong (hardware, software, human faults). Today we turn to the second of DDIA's three concerns: scalability.

Kleppmann is emphatic about one thing: scalability is not a one-dimensional label you slap on a system. Saying "X is scalable" or "Y doesn't scale" is meaningless. Scalability is the system's ability to cope with increased load, and to discuss it sensibly you must answer a specific question:

💡 The framing question

"If the system grows in a particular way, what are our options for coping with the growth?" and "How can we add computing resources to handle the additional load?"

To even ask that question, you need two ingredients, and today's lesson is precisely these two:

  1. A way to describe load — what exactly is increasing? (Today)
  2. A way to describe performance — what happens to the system when load increases? (Today)

Tomorrow (Day 5) we'll use these two tools to talk about coping with load — scaling up vs. out, stateless vs. stateful, and elastic systems.

1. Describing load: load parameters

Load is described with a few numbers Kleppmann calls load parameters. The right choice depends entirely on your system's architecture:

  • Requests per second to a web server (throughput of an HTTP service)
  • Ratio of reads to writes in a database
  • Number of simultaneously active users
  • Hit rate on a cache
  • ...or, in Twitter's famous case, the distribution of followers per user (we'll get to why this is the killer parameter)

Sometimes the average case drives your design; sometimes a handful of extreme outliers are the bottleneck. Choosing the right load parameter is itself a senior-level skill — it's where you decide what "growth" even means for your product.

2. Describing performance: throughput vs. response time

Once you can describe load, you ask: when load goes up, what happens? There are two ways to phrase this:

  • Keep resources fixed, increase load → how is performance affected?
  • Keep performance fixed → how many more resources do you need?

Two key performance numbers:

MetricWhat it measuresWhose number is it?
ThroughputRecords processed per second, or total job time on a dataset. The system's capacity.Batch systems (e.g. Hadoop) care most about this.
Response timeTime between a client sending a request and receiving the response — what the user actually feels.Online services care most about this.
💡 Latency ≠ response time (a precise distinction interviewers love)

Response time is what the client sees: it includes network delays, queueing delays, and the actual service time. Latency is the duration a request is waiting to be handled — latent, awaiting service. They're related but not synonyms. Saying "response time" when you mean the full round-trip, and reserving "latency" for the waiting portion, marks you as someone who's read the fine print.

3. The big idea: response time is a distribution, not a number

This is the heart of today. Even for identical requests, response time varies on every call — a random extra context switch, a GC pause, a TCP retransmit, a cold cache. So you should think of response time not as a single value but as a distribution of values you can measure. And the right way to summarize a distribution for performance is percentiles — not the average.

🧠 The Analogy: The Coffee Shop & the Group Photo

Percentiles → the morning coffee queue

Imagine a coffee shop measuring how long customers wait. The manager brags: "average wait is 3 minutes!" But that average hides the story. Most people wait 2 minutes (p50, the median — half wait less, half wait more). But the customer who arrived right as a tour bus emptied out waited 11 minutes (p99 — worse than 99% of all visits). And the one whose order broke the espresso machine waited 25 minutes (p99.9).

The average (3 min) describes nobody's actual experience and completely hides the 11- and 25-minute disasters. If you want to know "how bad is a bad day for a customer," you ask for the tail — the high percentiles — not the mean.

Mapping: each customer = one request; wait time = response time; the median = your typical user's experience; p99/p99.9 = your unluckiest (and often most loyal/highest-value) users; the average = the comforting lie on your dashboard.

Tail latency amplification → the group photo

You're organizing a group photo of 100 people. You can't take the shot until everyone is ready. 99 people are ready in 2 seconds. One person is fumbling with their phone for 20 seconds. How long until the photo? 20 seconds. The single slowest person sets the time for the whole group.

Now picture a single web page that, to render, makes 100 parallel backend calls (one per widget). Even if each backend is fast 99% of the time, the chance that at least one of the 100 calls hits its slow p99 is enormous. So a request that touches many backends is slow if any of them is slow — the rare slow case becomes the common case at the page level. This is tail latency amplification, and it's why high percentiles dominate user experience as systems grow.

💡 The aha

The average tells you about the system's arithmetic. The tail tells you about your users' experience. And when one request fans out to many services, the tail isn't a rare edge case — it's the default. Senior engineers obsess over p99 and p99.9 precisely because that's where real users live.

⚙️ How It Actually Works

Let's ground this in the canonical DDIA example: Twitter's home timeline. It's the perfect case study because it shows how choosing the right load parameter completely changes your architecture.

Animated architecture: fan-out on write

When a user posts a tweet, the naive approach is to look it up at read time. But Twitter's key insight was that reads vastly outnumber writes (people scroll far more than they post). So they do the work at write time: when you post, the tweet is fanned out — inserted into a precomputed home-timeline cache for every one of your followers. Reads then become a cheap lookup. Watch a write fan out to followers below.

One write, but the work scales with the number of followers. For an average user (~75 followers) that's fine. But here's the catch DDIA highlights: the distribution of followers per user is wildly skewed. A celebrity with 30 million followers means one tweet triggers 30 million cache writes — and Twitter wanted timelines to update within seconds. That distribution, not raw tweet volume, is the load parameter that breaks the design. (Twitter's real solution: a hybrid — fan-out on write for normal users, fan-out on read for celebrities. We explore the trade-off below.)

💡 SLO / SLA — turning percentiles into promises

Percentiles are how you write contracts. A Service Level Objective (SLO) might say "p50 under 200 ms and p99 under 1 s," and a Service Level Agreement (SLA) makes it a legal guarantee with refunds if you miss. Note you'd never write an SLA on the average — it's meaningless. And percentiles like p99.9 cost real money to defend: optimizing the last 0.1% may require attacking random events (GC, network) that are hard and expensive to control, which is why few teams chase p99.99.

🔗 A measurement gotcha: where you measure matters

Measure response times on the client side, not just the server. Head-of-line blocking means a few slow requests can hold up everything queued behind them — the server might report a fast service time while the client waits ages in the queue. Also beware averaging percentiles across machines (you can't just average two p99s) — you must aggregate the underlying histograms.

🌍 In the Wild

Twitter — the load-parameter masterclass

As covered above, Twitter moved from fan-out-on-read to a hybrid: fan-out-on-write for the ~99.9% of users with normal follower counts, and fan-out-on-read for a small list of celebrities. When you load your timeline, normal-followee tweets come pre-baked from your cache, and the handful of celebrities you follow get merged in at read time. The deciding load parameter wasn't tweets/sec — it was the follower distribution.

Amazon — the p99.9 doctrine

Amazon describes internal response-time requirements in terms of the 99.9th percentile, even though it affects only 1 in 1,000 requests. Why? Because the customers with the slowest requests often have the most data on their accounts — i.e., they're the most valuable customers (they've bought the most). Protecting the tail is protecting revenue. (This logic comes straight from the Dynamo lineage.)

Google & the "tail at scale"

Google's engineers literally wrote the paper "The Tail at Scale" documenting tail-latency amplification — and ship mitigations like hedged requests (send a duplicate request to a second replica if the first is slow, take whichever returns first) and tied requests. This is the group-photo problem solved in production.

Monitoring stacks — Prometheus, Datadog, Grafana

Modern observability tools expose percentile latency (p50/p90/p95/p99) on dashboards by default and aggregate them using histogram data structures (e.g. HdrHistogram, t-digest) so percentiles can be computed cheaply and merged across machines without storing every sample. If your team's dashboards still show only averages, that's a red flag you can now spot.

🔬 The SDE 2 Lens

Where this hits your day job

You've almost certainly seen these and now you'll understand why:

  • "It's fast for me but a customer says it's slow." You were the median; they were the p99. The bug isn't unreproducible — it lives in the tail. Pull the p99 trace, not the average.
  • That Grafana panel labeled "avg latency." Now you know it can hide a fire. Add a p99 panel. The gap between p50 and p99 is one of the most informative numbers about your service's health.
  • The N+1 query / fan-out endpoint. When your service calls 20 downstream services to build one response, you're living the group-photo problem. Each dependency's tail compounds. This is why "just add a microservice" silently degrades latency.
  • Load testing. A good load test generates requests independently of responses (open-loop). If your test waits for each response before sending the next (closed-loop), slow responses artificially throttle your offered load and hide the queueing — you'll measure a rosy picture that production won't match.
  • Choosing a load parameter for capacity planning. Before your next launch, ask: what's the one number that, if it doubled, would hurt us most? Requests/sec? Active users? Data per user? That's your load parameter — and it's rarely the obvious one.
💡 The mental upgrade

Junior framing: "make it faster." SDE 2 framing: "tighten p99 for the timeline read path under 2× current peak load." The second sentence is measurable, falsifiable, and scoped — that's the difference a chapter like this buys you.

🎯 System Design Interview Playbook

When this comes up

Practically every design question: "Design Twitter / a news feed," "Design a URL shortener at scale," "Design a rate limiter," "How would you measure if this is fast enough?" The moment the interviewer says "at scale," they're inviting this vocabulary.

What to say (the 4-beat move)

  1. Name the load parameter first. "Before I design, let me define load: I'll assume X reads/sec, a 100:1 read/write ratio, and a skewed follower distribution. Which dimension should I optimize for?" — This instantly signals seniority.
  2. State performance in percentiles. "My target is p99 read latency under 200 ms, p99.9 under 1 s — not the average, because the tail is what users feel."
  3. Connect load + performance to architecture. "Because reads dominate, I'll precompute (fan-out on write); because of the celebrity outliers, I'll go hybrid." — This is the payoff: the metric drove the design.
  4. Plan to measure. "I'd track p50/p99/p99.9 client-side with histograms, set an SLO, and alert on tail regression."

The trade-off to articulate

"Fan-out on write makes reads cheap but writes expensive and unbounded for celebrities; fan-out on read makes writes cheap but reads expensive and bursty. The read/write ratio and the follower distribution decide which dominates — so I'll split traffic by that distribution."

⚠️ Common candidate mistake

Optimizing for average latency, or saying "I'll make it scalable" without naming what scales. Also: forgetting that a fan-out/microservice request's latency is governed by its slowest dependency, then promising a tight p99 you can't possibly hit.

🚀 Power move

Bring up tail latency amplification unprompted: "Since the timeline page fans out to ~N services, even a 1% per-service tail means most pages hit at least one slow call — so I'd add hedged requests and a strict per-call timeout budget." Interviewers light up, because that's a real Google/Amazon production concern most candidates have never heard of.

Myth

Truth

Scalability is always relative to a specific kind of growth. A system that scales beautifully for more reads may collapse under more writes, or under skewed keys. Always ask: "scale in what dimension?" There's no context-free "scalable."

Myth

"Average response time is a good summary of performance."

Truth

The mean hides the tail and describes no real user. Use percentiles (p50/p95/p99/p99.9). The median tells you the typical experience; the high percentiles tell you how bad the bad cases are — and those bad cases hit your most valuable users.

Myth

"p99 = 200 ms means 99% of users have a great time."

Truth

It means 99% of requests are under 200 ms — not users. A single user making many requests (or one page fanning out to many calls) will probably hit the slow tail eventually. With fan-out, the p99 of one call becomes the common experience of the page.

🧩 Interactive Zone

🤔 Pause & Think: Why is the average a worse metric than the median for response time, even though both are "middle" numbers?

The average (mean) is sensitive to outliers because it sums every value — a single 30-second request can drag the mean of thousands of fast requests upward (or, more insidiously, be diluted by them so you don't notice it at all). It mixes the typical case and the disaster case into one misleading number.

The median (p50) is just "the middle value when sorted" — exactly half of requests are faster, half slower. It's robust to outliers: it tells you the typical experience honestly. But the median alone isn't enough either — it says nothing about the tail. That's why you report the median and high percentiles together: p50 for "typical," p99/p99.9 for "worst-case for real users." The average gives you neither cleanly.

🤔 Pause & Think: A page makes 50 parallel backend calls. Each backend has a p99 of 100 ms (1% of calls take ≥100 ms). Roughly what fraction of pages will have at least one slow call?

If each call is independently slow 1% of the time, the chance a single call is fast is 99% = 0.99. The chance all 50 are fast is 0.9950 ≈ 0.605. So the chance at least one is slow is 1 − 0.605 ≈ 39%.

Read that again: a "1-in-100" slow event at the backend becomes a "nearly 2-in-5" slow event at the page level. This is tail latency amplification in one calculation. It's why fan-out architectures must (a) minimize the number of dependencies on the critical path, (b) set tight per-call timeouts, and (c) use tricks like hedged requests. The tail isn't an edge case at scale — it's the main event.

⚡ Quick Check
You're building a Hadoop job that crunches a day's logs overnight. Which performance metric should you primarily optimize, and why?
Throughput — records processed per second / total time to finish the dataset. Batch jobs aren't user-facing and have no interactive request waiting on them, so response-time percentiles are largely irrelevant. What matters is total job runtime and keeping all the worker nodes busy. (Contrast: a user-facing API would care about p99 response time, not throughput-per-se.)
⚡ Quick Check
Your service's p50 is 40 ms but p99 is 3,200 ms. What does that gap most likely indicate, and what's a first thing to investigate?
A huge p50→p99 gap means most requests are fast but a meaningful slice are catastrophically slow — a classic sign of queueing / contention, GC pauses, lock contention, a slow dependency, or head-of-line blocking. First moves: look at the high-percentile traces specifically (not aggregate averages), check for resource saturation (CPU, connection pool, thread pool) and downstream-call timeouts, and verify you're measuring client-side so you catch queue wait. The flat p50 tells you it's not a systemic slowdown — something is intermittently stalling a subset of requests.

🎒 Key Takeaways

1. Scalability is a question, not a label. Always ask "scale in which dimension?" — you can't say a system "is scalable" without naming the kind of growth.
2. Describe load with the right load parameter. Requests/sec, read:write ratio, active users, cache hit rate — or, like Twitter, the follower distribution. The non-obvious one is often the killer.
3. Performance is throughput (batch) or response time (online). Response time is a distribution, not a number — and it ≠ latency (the waiting portion).
4. Use percentiles, never the average. p50 = typical experience; p99/p99.9 = your unluckiest, often most valuable users. SLOs and SLAs are written in percentiles.
5. Tail latency amplifies under fan-out. When one request touches many services, a rare slow call becomes the common case at the page level. Measure client-side; mind head-of-line blocking.
