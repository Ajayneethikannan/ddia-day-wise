Scalability — When "It Works on My Machine" Meets a Million Users

Chapter 1 · Reliable, Scalable, Maintainable Applications · Describing Load & Performance
Course progress4 / 109 (3.7%)

🐳 The Problem: The Fail Whale That Defined an Era

2008. Twitter is exploding. Every time a big event happened — an earthquake, an awards show, a World Cup goal — the site fell over and users were greeted by a cartoon of a whale being lifted out of the ocean by a flock of birds. The Fail Whale became so iconic it got tattoos, T-shirts, and its own fan club. That's how often Twitter was down.

Here's the twist that should bother you: Twitter wasn't failing because of a bug, a dead disk, or a fat-fingered deploy (those were Days 2–3). The code was correct. The servers were healthy. It fell over simply because too many people were using it at once. The architecture that worked beautifully for 100,000 users buckled at 10 million — and no amount of "be more careful" or "buy a better server" could fix it.

And there was a subtler trap hiding in their dashboards. The average response time looked fine — maybe 200ms. Management saw green. But a meaningful slice of requests — often from the most active, most valuable users — were taking 5, 10, 30 seconds, or timing out entirely. The average was a comfortable lie.

💡 Why this should bother you

A system can be perfectly reliable (no faults) and still be useless because it can't handle the load. Growth itself is a kind of failure mode — one you can't patch your way out of after the fact. Today we learn the two skills senior engineers use to get ahead of it: how to describe load precisely, and how to measure performance honestly (spoiler: never with an average).

📖 What Is It? Scalability, Load, and Performance

Day 1 named the three concerns of every data system: Reliability, Scalability, Maintainability. We've spent three days on reliability. Today we open Scalability — and Kleppmann's first move is to puncture a word you've heard in a hundred meetings.

"Is it scalable?" is the wrong question

Scalability is not a one-dimensional label a system either has or lacks. Saying "X is scalable" or "Y doesn't scale" is, in Kleppmann's words, meaningless. Scalability is a system's ability to cope with increased load — and to discuss it concretely you must answer:

  • "If the load grows in this particular way, what are our options for coping?"
  • "How can we add computing resources to handle the additional load?"

To even ask those questions, you need two ingredients, and the rest of today is built on them:

IngredientThe question it answers
1 · Load parametersHow do we describe the load on the system right now? (the numbers that matter for your system)
2 · Performance metricsWhen load increases, what happens to throughput and response time? And how do we measure them honestly?

Load parameters — pick the numbers that matter

Load is described with a handful of numbers Kleppmann calls load parameters. The right ones depend entirely on your architecture:

  • Requests per second to a web server
  • Ratio of reads to writes in a database
  • Number of simultaneously active users
  • Hit rate on a cache

Sometimes the average case drives your design; sometimes a handful of extreme cases are the bottleneck. Twitter's story is the canonical example of the latter — let's dig in.

Twitter's real load parameter: fan-out

In 2012, Twitter's two main operations had these published numbers:

OperationRateThe catch
Post tweet~4,600 req/s average (12k/s peak)Looks tiny — any laptop could absorb this write rate.
Home timeline read~300,000 req/sEach read must assemble tweets from everyone you follow.

The tweet write rate is trivial. The challenge is the fan-out: because each user follows many people and each person is followed by many users, every tweet must somehow reach every follower's home timeline. The load parameter that actually determines Twitter's scalability is not "tweets per second" but the distribution of followers per user — driven by celebrities with tens of millions of followers. We'll see the two architectures for solving this in How It Works.

🧠 The Analogy: The Newspaper Round & the DMV Queue

Fan-out → printing a newspaper vs. running a reading room

Imagine you publish a daily paper and every reader wants a personalized edition containing only the columnists they subscribe to.

  • Fan-out on write = the delivery round. The moment a columnist files a story, you stamp a copy into the mailbox of every subscriber. Lots of work at publish time (a popular columnist means millions of stamps), but when a reader opens their mailbox in the morning, their custom paper is already sitting there — instant.
  • Fan-out on read = the reading room. You store one master copy of each columnist's stories. When a reader shows up, you run around gathering every relevant column and assemble their paper on the spot. Cheap to publish (file once), but every single reader visit is expensive — and readers visit far more often than columnists file.

Mapping: columnist filing a story = posting a tweet (write); reader opening their mailbox = loading the home timeline (read); the mailbox = a per-user precomputed timeline cache. Because reads outnumber writes ~65:1 on Twitter, doing the work at write time usually wins — except for the celebrity columnist with 30 million subscribers, where the delivery round becomes a nightmare. Hold that thought.

Performance → the DMV, and why the average wait is a lie

You walk into the DMV. A sign says "average wait: 8 minutes." Reassured, you take a number — and wait 50 minutes. The sign wasn't lying about the math; the average was genuinely 8 minutes. But a few unlucky people stuck behind a complicated case waited an hour, and you were one of them.

Mapping: each person served = one request; the wait time = response time; the average = the misleading mean everyone quotes; the question that actually matters — "what's the worst wait 95% / 99% of people experience?" — is the percentile. If the DMV promised "95% of people served within 15 minutes," that's a real, honest commitment — that's a Service Level Objective. The average tells you nothing about how bad your bad day is.

💡 The aha

Two ideas, one lesson: where you do the work matters (write-time vs read-time changes everything when the ratio is lopsided), and distributions beat averages (you live in the tail, not the mean). Both are about looking past the comfortable summary number to the shape of the real load.

⚙️ How It Actually Works

Let's make Twitter's fan-out concrete, then turn to measuring performance with percentiles.

Animated architecture: fan-out on write (the hybrid Twitter uses)

A user posts a tweet. A fan-out service looks up their followers and writes the tweet into each follower's precomputed home-timeline cache (their "mailbox"). Reads then become a cheap single lookup. The catch — a celebrity — is handled separately: their tweets are not fanned out but merged in at read time.

Blue dashes = the tweet being pushed into each follower's timeline at write time. Amber path = the celebrity's tweets, which are pulled in and merged only when a reader loads their timeline (avoiding 30M writes per celebrity tweet). Green = the reader's cheap, mostly-precomputed read. This hybrid is what real Twitter settled on.

Describing performance: throughput vs. response time

When you increase load, "performance" splits into two distinct questions:

  • Throughput — records processed per second, or total job time. The metric for batch systems (like the Hadoop jobs of Chapter 10).
  • Response time — the time between a client sending a request and receiving the response. The metric for online systems. (Note: response time = service time + network delays + queueing delays. "Latency" technically means only the time a request is waiting to be handled — people use the words loosely, but they're not identical.)

Response time is a distribution, not a number

Send the same request a thousand times and you'll get a thousand different response times — random extra latency from context switches, TCP retransmits, garbage collection pauses, a page fault, a slow disk seek. So a single number can't describe it. You need the shape:

p50 (median)

p95

p99

p99.9
  • Median (p50) — half your users get a faster response, half slower. The "typical" experience. Use this, never the mean.
  • Tail latencies (p95, p99, p99.9) — "95% of requests are faster than X." These describe how bad your bad requests are.
💡 Why the tail belongs to your best customers

Amazon observed that the customers with the slowest requests are often the ones with the most data in their accounts — because they're the most active, highest-value buyers. So the p99.9 isn't some irrelevant edge case; it's the experience of your whales. Amazon found that a 100ms increase in latency reduced sales by ~1%. That's why they optimize the 99.9th percentile, even though squeezing it costs disproportionately more than improving the median.

Tail latency amplification — the killer multiplier

Here's the trap that bites every microservices architecture. When a single user request fans out to many backend calls, the request isn't done until the slowest backend responds. So even a small fraction of slow backend calls dominates the end-user experience:

P(a single backend call is fast) = 0.99 (99% under target) A user request makes N backend calls in parallel. P(ALL N are fast) = 0.99 ^ N N = 1 → 99.0% of user requests fast N = 10 → 90.4% of user requests fast (1 in 10 hits a slow call) N = 100 → 36.6% of user requests fast (~2 in 3 hit a slow call!) So a backend that is "99% fast" produces a service that is mostly SLOW once a request touches 100 backends. This is why one slow dependency tanks your whole endpoint.

⚖️ The Trade-offs

The fan-out decision is the cleanest trade-off in the chapter: do the work at write time (push) or at read time (pull)? The answer depends on your read/write ratio and — critically — on the distribution of your load (celebrities).

The right load parameter wasn't "tweets/sec"; it was the shape of the follower distribution.

And the second trade-off: which percentile to chase

Improving the median is relatively cheap. Each higher percentile costs disproportionately more — the p99.9 is hostage to events outside your control (GC pauses, network blips, a noisy neighbor). You must decide how far down the tail is worth optimizing, because the cost curve is brutally non-linear.

🏢 In the Wild

Twitter — the textbook hybrid fan-out

Twitter's journey is literally the DDIA case study: they started with fan-out on read (pull), couldn't keep up with the 300k/s home-timeline read load, switched to fan-out on write (push into per-user caches), then hit the celebrity write-storm problem — a single tweet from a megastar meant tens of millions of writes. The final design is the hybrid: push for normal users, pull-and-merge for the few accounts with enormous follower counts.

Amazon — p99.9 SLAs and the 100ms rule

Amazon's internal services are held to 99.9th-percentile response-time targets, not averages — precisely because the slow tail hits their highest-value customers. The DynamoDB paper and Amazon's "100ms of latency costs 1% of sales" finding both come from this lineage. SLAs/SLOs in the industry are almost always written as percentiles (e.g., "p99 < 200ms, 99.9% of the time").

Google — hedged requests & the tail-at-scale paper

Google's famous "The Tail at Scale" paper addresses exactly the amplification math above. Their fix: hedged requests (send the same request to two replicas, take whichever responds first, cancel the other) and tied requests — engineering specifically to tame the p99 when one request touches thousands of servers.

Instagram & Facebook feed

News Feed / home feed systems face the identical fan-out problem and use the same push/pull/hybrid playbook plus heavy ranking. Instagram's feed is largely pull-with-aggressive-caching; the "who do we precompute for" decision is a live, ongoing tuning problem at their scale.

Discord & the "active channel" problem

Discord pushes messages to actively-connected clients (push) but lazily loads history (pull) — another hybrid driven by the read/write shape of a chat workload, where most channels are quiet and a few are firehoses (the celebrity pattern, again).

🔍 The SDE 2 Lens

Where this shows up in your day job

You touch these ideas constantly — usually without naming them. Once you do, your dashboards and design reviews get sharper.

  • Your Grafana/Datadog latency panel shows p50 / p95 / p99 — not "average." Now you know why: the average hides the tail your worst-served users live in. If your team alerts on average latency, that's a bug — switch it to p99.
  • That N+1 query your ORM generates is fan-out on read. Loading a list and then firing one query per item is exactly the "assemble it at read time" pattern — cheap to write, brutal to read at scale. The fix (eager-load / precompute / denormalize a cached view) is fan-out on write.
  • Your caching strategy is a push/pull decision. A write-through cache (update the cache on every write) is fan-out on write. A read-through/lazy cache (compute on miss) is fan-out on read. You're making Twitter's choice every time you design a cache.
  • "Why did our endpoint get slow when only one downstream service degraded?" Tail latency amplification. Your endpoint fans out to N services; the slowest one sets your response time. This is why one flaky dependency tanks an aggregator API.
  • Load testing & capacity planning. When you write a load test, you're choosing load parameters (RPS, read/write mix, payload size). Reporting only the average from a load test is the rookie move — report the percentiles.
  • The "celebrity" / "hot key" problem is everywhere. A viral product page, a hot partition key, one massive tenant in your multi-tenant DB — all the same skewed-distribution problem Twitter hit. (We'll formalize "hot keys" in Partitioning, Chapter 6.)
💡 Ohhh, THAT'S why

...your SRE keeps saying "the average looks fine but p99 is on fire," and why the fix for a slow page was sometimes "precompute it into a materialized view" rather than "add a server." You can't add hardware to fix a fan-out-on-read design that's drowning in reads — you have to move where the work happens.

🎯 System Design Interview Playbook

When this comes up

This is the most common system-design domain. "Design Twitter" (or Instagram feed, Facebook News Feed, a notification system) is fan-out, full stop. And every design eventually gets the question: "How does this perform under load? What's your p99? How do you scale it?" Knowing percentiles and the push/pull trade-off cold is table stakes at FAANG.

What to say (the checklist)

  • State load parameters first: "Before scaling anything, let me quantify load — reads/sec, writes/sec, read:write ratio, and the distribution (are there hot keys / celebrities?)." This signals senior thinking immediately.
  • Reach for the fan-out trade-off by name: "Reads dominate, so I'd fan out on write — precompute each user's timeline into a cache for cheap reads."
  • Pre-empt the celebrity problem: "But fanning out a celebrity's tweet is millions of writes, so I'd go hybrid — pull-and-merge for high-follower accounts." Bringing this up before the interviewer does is a power move.
  • Define SLOs in percentiles: "I'd target p99 < 200ms rather than an average, because the tail is where my most active users live."
  • Name tail amplification for fan-out reads: "Since the request hits many services, I'd worry about tail latency amplification and consider hedged requests / timeouts with fallbacks."

The trade-off to articulate

"It's fundamentally where do I pay the cost — write time or read time? Given reads outnumber writes here, I pay at write time to make reads cheap, and I special-case the skewed tail (celebrities/hot keys) so a single write doesn't become a storm."

⚠️ Common candidate mistake

(1) Saying "I'll use a scalable database like Cassandra" as if "scalable" were a magic property — it isn't, and good interviewers will push back. (2) Quoting average latency. (3) Designing pure fan-out-on-write and never mentioning the celebrity write-storm, then getting cornered when the interviewer asks "what happens when Taylor Swift tweets?"

⭐ Power move

Do the back-of-envelope on the spot: "300k reads/s vs 4.6k writes/s — reads win by ~65×, so I push at write time. But a 30M-follower account = 30M writes per tweet, so those go pull. Hybrid." Then mention p99.9 SLAs and tail latency amplification. Quantifying the load and naming the skewed distribution is exactly the staff-level instinct most candidates skip.

🤔 Pause & Think #1 — Why does optimizing the p99.9 cost so much more than improving the median?

The median is fast — why is squeezing the far tail disproportionately expensive?

Answer: The median is determined by your normal, controllable code path — make the common case efficient and p50 drops. But the far tail (p99.9) is dominated by events that are rare, random, and largely outside your direct control: a garbage-collection pause, a TCP retransmit, a context switch, a brief network blip, a slow disk seek, a noisy neighbor on shared hardware, a cold cache. To shave the tail you must fight each of these independently — tune GC, add hedged/redundant requests, isolate resources, pre-warm caches, add timeouts and retries. Each fix attacks one rare cause for a sliver of improvement, so the cost curve is steeply non-linear. That's exactly why you must decide how far down the tail is worth chasing — chasing p99.999 for a non-critical internal tool is wasted money; chasing it for a checkout flow may be worth millions.

🤔 Pause & Think #2 — If fan-out on write makes reads cheap and reads dominate, why not use it for everyone, celebrities included?

Reads outnumber writes 65×, so writing more to save on reads seems like a clear win. Where does it break?

Answer: It breaks on the distribution. For a normal user with 200 followers, one tweet = 200 cheap writes — totally fine, and it buys you 200 cheap reads later. But fan-out cost scales with follower count. A celebrity with 30M followers means a single tweet triggers 30M writes — a sudden, enormous "write storm" that spikes load, delays the tweet's delivery, and wastes huge storage duplicating that tweet 30M times. Worse, many of those followers may not even read their timeline soon (or ever). So the work is both gigantic and often wasted. The hybrid fix: keep fan-out-on-write for the long tail of normal accounts (where it's cheap and reads dominate), but for the handful of mega-accounts, switch to fan-out-on-read — store the celebrity's tweets once and merge them in at read time. You're matching the strategy to the shape of the load, which is the whole lesson of describing load with the right parameter.

⚡ Quick Check #1

Your service's p50 is 40ms and p99 is 40ms, but your monitoring shows the average is 95ms. What does that tell you, and is your service healthy?

If p50 and p99 are both 40ms but the mean is 95ms, then a tiny fraction beyond the 99th percentile (the p99.9 / p100 region) must be enormously slow — slow enough to drag the average up by ~55ms despite 99% of requests sitting at 40ms. The service is mostly healthy, but it has a nasty far tail: maybe 0.1–1% of requests taking seconds (think GC pauses, timeouts, a slow shard). This is the perfect illustration of why the mean is misleading — it's the only one of the three numbers that even hinted at the problem, yet it can't tell you whether it's "everything slightly slow" or "a few requests catastrophically slow." You need the high percentiles (p99.9) to see it clearly.

⚡ Quick Check #2

An aggregation endpoint calls 50 microservices in parallel and returns when all reply. Each service meets its p95 (95% of calls under 50ms). Roughly what fraction of user requests will be "fast" (all 50 under 50ms)?

P(all 50 fast) = 0.9550 ≈ 0.077, or about 8%. So even though every individual service hits its p95, only ~8% of user requests are fast — the other ~92% wait on at least one slow backend. That's tail latency amplification. The takeaways: (1) per-service p95 is nowhere near good enough when you fan out widely — you may need p99 or p99.9 on the backends; (2) consider hedged requests (race two replicas, take the first), tighter timeouts with partial-result fallbacks, or reducing the fan-out width. One slow dependency, multiplied across a wide fan-out, defines the user's experience.

✅ Key Takeaways

"Scalable" is not a property. Always ask "if load grows this way, what are our options to cope?" Describe load with the parameters that matter for your system.
Find the load parameter that actually hurts. For Twitter it wasn't tweets/sec — it was the skewed follower distribution. The average case and the extreme case can need totally different designs.
Pay at write time or read time — pick based on the ratio and the tail. Reads ≫ writes → fan-out on write (cheap reads). Huge fan-out (celebrities) → fan-out on read. Real systems go hybrid.
Never trust the average. Use percentiles. p50 for the typical user, p99/p99.9 for the worst — which is where your most valuable customers live. Amazon: 100ms ≈ 1% of sales.
Beware tail latency amplification. Fan a request out to N backends and the slowest one wins: 0.99100 ≈ 37% fast. One slow dependency can define your whole endpoint.
