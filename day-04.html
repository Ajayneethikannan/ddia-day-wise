Scalability — Averages Lie, Percentiles Don't

Chapter 1 · Reliable, Scalable, Maintainable Applications · Describing Load & Performance
Course progress4 / 109 (3.7%)

🌩️ The Problem: The Dashboard Says 50ms. Your Best Customer Is Screaming.

You're on call. Your service dashboard is a wall of green. The headline number — average response time: 50ms — has been flat for weeks. Leadership is happy. Then the account manager for your single largest enterprise customer pings you: "The app is unusably slow. Pages take seconds. We're considering churning."

You check their requests. They're not lying — many of their calls are taking 2–3 seconds. But your average is 50ms. How can both be true? Because the average is a liar by design. Out of a million requests, 999,000 are blazing fast and 1,000 are catastrophically slow. The average drowns the slow ones in a sea of fast ones — and the slow ones are landing on the people who matter most.

Here's the cruel twist Amazon discovered: the customers experiencing the worst latency are often the customers with the most data — the ones who've made the most purchases, have the biggest carts, the longest history. In other words, your most valuable customers reliably get your worst experience, and a single average number hides it perfectly.

💡 Why this should rewire your brain

For three days we asked "is the system up?" (Reliability). Today we ask a sharper question: "is the system good — and will it stay good as it grows?" (Scalability). And step one is brutal: you cannot manage what you describe with a single number. Load and performance are distributions, not scalars. The senior engineer's first move isn't to fix the latency — it's to measure it honestly, with percentiles, before anyone can hide behind an average.

📖 What Is It? Load, Performance & the Discipline of Describing Them

Day 1 named Scalability as the second of the three concerns. Kleppmann is careful here: scalability is not a one-dimensional label. You don't say "X is scalable" or "Y doesn't scale." You ask: "If the system grows in a particular way, what are our options for coping?" To even ask that, you need two precise vocabularies — one for load, one for performance.

1 · Describing load with load parameters

A load parameter is a number that captures the shape of the demand on your system. Which number matters depends entirely on your architecture:

  • Requests per second to a web server (throughput)
  • Ratio of reads to writes in a database
  • Number of simultaneously active users
  • Hit rate on a cache
  • ...or something subtler, like the distribution of how many followers each user has

The right parameter is sometimes the average case, sometimes a handful of extreme outliers that dominate everything. Picking the wrong load parameter means you optimize for a world that doesn't exist.

The canonical example: Twitter's home timeline

DDIA uses Twitter's (circa 2012) numbers. Two operations dominate:

OperationScale
Post a tweet~4,600 req/s on average, ~12,000 req/s at peak
Read home timeline~300,000 req/s

Reads outnumber writes by ~65×. So far this looks like a simple read-heavy system. But the killer detail isn't the request rate — it's fan-out: each user follows many people, and each user is followed by many people. The load parameter that actually decides your architecture is the distribution of followers per user. We'll watch that distribution wreck a naive design in the How It Works section.

2 · Describing performance

Once load is defined, ask two questions:

  • When you increase a load parameter and keep resources fixed — how is performance affected? (It gets worse.)
  • When you increase a load parameter — how much do you need to increase resources to keep performance unchanged?

Two performance metrics matter, and they're not the same thing:

MetricDefinitionWhose world
ThroughputRecords/requests processed per unit timeBatch systems (Hadoop, ETL)
Response timeTime between client sending a request and receiving the responseOnline systems (your API)

Response time ≠ latency (a precise distinction)

People use these interchangeably; Kleppmann doesn't. Response time is what the client sees: the full round trip = actual service time + network delays + queueing delays. Latency is the duration a request is waiting to be handled — latent, awaiting service. The response time you measure includes latency you may not realize is there.

The headline idea: response time is a distribution

Even identical requests take different amounts of time — a background GC pause, a TCP retransmit, a cold cache, a noisy neighbor on the box. So response time is not a number; it's a distribution with a long right tail. That's why the average is dangerous and why we measure with percentiles:

  • p50 (median): half of requests are faster, half slower. The "typical" experience.
  • p95 / p99 / p99.9: the tail latencies. p99 = "99% of requests are faster than this; 1% are slower." These outliers are the experience your power users actually feel.
💡 The reframe

Stop reporting "average latency." Report p50 / p95 / p99. The mean is pulled around by a single 10-second outlier and tells you almost nothing about what a real user feels. The median tells you the typical case; the tail tells you the worst case that still happens often enough to matter (1% of a million requests is 10,000 unhappy users).

🧠 The Analogy: The Coffee Shop & Bill Gates Walking Into a Bar

Percentiles → the morning coffee queue

Imagine your favourite café. Most mornings you're served in 90 seconds (that's the median — p50). But occasionally the person ahead of you orders eleven custom drinks for their office, and you wait 8 minutes (that's a p99 morning). If the café advertised its "average wait," it would average your great days with the occasional disaster and quote something meaningless like "2 minutes 10 seconds" — a number you personally never actually experience. What you care about is: "How bad is a bad day, and how often does it happen?" That's exactly p95/p99.

Mapping: a single order = a request; serving time = response time; the eleven-drink order = a tail event (GC pause, cold cache); the median = your typical morning; p99 = your worst plausible morning. The barista pulling one giant order blocks everyone behind them — that's head-of-line blocking, which we'll meet shortly.

Why the average lies → Bill Gates walks into a bar

Ten people are in a bar; the average net worth is modest. Bill Gates walks in. Now the average net worth is over a billion dollars — yet not one person in that bar feels a billion dollars richer. The median barely moved; the mean exploded. One extreme outlier hijacked the average and made it describe a reality nobody is living.

Mapping: Bill Gates = one 10-second outlier request; the bar patrons = your ordinary fast requests; "average net worth" = average latency (useless); "median net worth" = p50 (honest). This is precisely why a 50ms average can coexist with a customer experiencing 3-second pages.

Fan-out → the wedding invitation problem

You can address invitations one of two ways. (A) Keep one master guest book and, when someone asks "who's invited?", read the whole book and filter — cheap to write, expensive to read. (B) Pre-stuff a personalized envelope for every guest the moment you decide on the list — expensive up front, but each guest instantly sees their invite. Now imagine one "guest" is a celebrity with 30 million friends who all want to know if they're invited. Pre-stuffing 30 million envelopes for one decision is insane. That asymmetry — most users cheap, a few users catastrophic — is the Twitter fan-out problem in a nutshell.

💡 The aha

Both halves of today's lesson share one shape: a small number of extreme cases dominate the system's behaviour. A few slow requests define your tail latency; a few mega-followed accounts define your fan-out cost. Describing a system by its average hides exactly the cases that will hurt you.

⚙️ How It Actually Works

Two mechanisms to internalize today: (1) how fan-out turns a follower-distribution load parameter into an architecture decision, and (2) how percentiles are actually computed from a stream of response times — and why the mean betrays you.

Animated architecture: Twitter fan-out-on-write (with the celebrity escape hatch)

Twitter's winning design pre-computes each user's home timeline. When you post a tweet, a fan-out service writes a copy into every follower's cached timeline — so reads (300k/s) become dirt cheap. But a celebrity with millions of followers would trigger millions of writes per tweet, so those accounts are excluded from fan-out and merged in at read time instead. Watch the ordinary write fan out (blue) while the celebrity tweet takes the slow read-time path (purple).

Blue = fan-out-on-write: one ordinary tweet → ~75 cheap cache writes, so the 300k/s reads are instant. Purple = celebrity path: their tweets are not fanned out (that'd be 30M writes); instead the reader merges a small number of celebrity tweets into their cached timeline at read time. This hybrid is the real Twitter answer — the follower distribution decides which path each account takes.

Step-through: from a stream of response times to honest percentiles

Twelve requests came back with these response times (ms): 20, 22, 25, 28, 30, 32, 35, 40, 55, 90, 180, 400. Step through how we turn them into p50, p95, p99 — and watch the mean get hijacked by the tail.

Step 1 of 5 · Collect & sort the samples
Every request's response time is recorded, then sorted ascending. Percentiles are positions in this sorted list — so sorting is the whole trick. Notice the shape: a cluster of fast requests on the left, then a long tail stretching right. This long right tail is the signature of real-world latency.

Tail latency amplification: why the tail gets WORSE at the user level

Here's the trap that ambushes microservice architectures. Suppose one user-facing page makes parallel calls to 10 backend services and can't render until all have returned. Even if each backend has a great p99 of just 1%, the chance that at least one of the 10 is slow is much higher: 1 − (0.99)^10 ≈ 9.6%. So nearly 1 in 10 page loads hits a tail. The more services a request fans out to, the more often the user feels a slow one. This is tail latency amplification.

P(user sees a slow page) = 1 − (P(one backend is fast))^N N = 1 backend, p99 each: 1 − 0.99^1 = 1.0% ← user p99 ≈ backend p99 N = 10 backends, p99 each: 1 − 0.99^10 = 9.6% ← user "p99" is now p90-ish! N = 100 backends,p99 each: 1 − 0.99^100 = 63% ← most pages hit a tail Lesson: in a fan-out architecture, the TAIL is the typical experience. Optimize p99 of every backend, or the slowest one defines your product.
💡 Where the tail comes from

Tail latency is usually queueing delay, not slow code. A server handles only a few requests in parallel; one slow request (a big query, a GC pause) holds up the queue behind it — head-of-line blocking. Because even a small number of slow requests holds up many others, you must measure response times on the client side, and beware: a load generator that waits for each response before sending the next will hide queueing and lie to you (that's coordinated omission).

⚖️ The Trade-offs

The fan-out decision is the cleanest trade-off in Chapter 1: do the work at write time (pre-compute timelines) or at read time (merge on demand)? It hinges entirely on your load parameters — the read/write ratio and the follower distribution.

🏢 In the Wild

Amazon — the p99.9 obsession

Amazon describes internal service requirements in terms of p99.9 (the slowest 1 in 1,000), not the average. Why so extreme? Because the slowest requests often belong to the customers with the most data — and therefore the most valuable accounts. Amazon also found that a 100ms increase in latency cut sales by ~1%, and Google found an extra 500ms in search dropped traffic ~20%. Latency is revenue. They optimize the tail because the tail is where the money is. (They stop short of p99.99 — reducing the absolute worst-case costs too much for too little gain.)

Twitter — fan-out, exactly as in the book

Twitter's home-timeline system is the textbook example because it is the textbook example. They moved from read-time merge to write-time fan-out (precomputed timeline caches in Redis), then to a hybrid where high-follower accounts skip fan-out and are merged at read time. The load parameter that drove every redesign was the distribution of followers per user, not the raw request rate.

Monitoring stacks — percentiles everywhere

Modern observability is built around percentiles. Prometheus histograms and summaries, Datadog, Grafana, and AWS CloudWatch all expose p50/p90/p95/p99 because everyone learned the average lesson the hard way. Computing exact percentiles over a stream is expensive, so production systems use approximation algorithms like t-digest and HdrHistogram to estimate them cheaply. (Beware naïvely averaging percentiles across machines — you can't average p99s and get a meaningful p99.)

SLOs / SLAs — contracts written in percentiles

Service-level agreements are expressed as tail guarantees: "p99 response time under 200ms, 99.9% availability, measured over rolling 1-minute windows." If you promise an average, a provider can game it by being lightning fast on cheap requests and terrible on expensive ones. Percentile SLOs close that loophole. This connects directly to Day 3's error budgets.

CDNs & edge caching

Cloudflare and Akamai are, in a sense, giant tail-latency machines: by serving content from a POP near the user, they cut the network-delay component of response time and shrink the variance that creates long tails for far-away users.

🔍 The SDE 2 Lens

Where this shows up in your day job

You touch this every sprint — you just may not have had the vocabulary for it. Here's where today's ideas hide in plain sight.

  • That Grafana panel you glance at. If your team alerts on average latency, you're flying blind — switch the alert to p99. The 3 AM page you should get is "p99 crossed 500ms," not "mean crossed 500ms" (which only fires after things are catastrophic).
  • The "it's fast on my machine / in staging" bug. Your laptop has one user and a warm cache — you're measuring p50 with no queueing. Production tail latency comes from concurrency and queueing you can't reproduce locally. That's why a feature "tested fine" but pages users in prod.
  • Your N+1 query and chatty microservice calls. Every backend hop you add multiplies tail-latency-amplification risk: 1 − 0.99^N. The page that calls 12 services to render is structurally going to feel slow ~11% of the time, no matter how good each service is. This is the real cost of "just one more service call."
  • The cache you added. Cache hit rate is a load parameter. A 99% hit rate sounds great until you realize the 1% misses are your tail — and a cache stampede on expiry is a head-of-line-blocking event waiting to happen.
  • Pre-compute vs compute-on-read. Materialized views, denormalized read models, and "build the feed when they post vs when they scroll" are all the fan-out trade-off. Now you can reason about it with read/write ratios instead of vibes.
  • Load tests that look too good. If your load generator sends one request, waits, then sends the next, it removes queueing and reports a fantasy tail (coordinated omission). Real load tests fire at a fixed rate regardless of responses.
💡 Ohhh, THAT'S why

...the same endpoint that's "20ms in the metrics" gets bug reports about being slow. You were looking at p50; the complainers are living at p99. And it's why your biggest customer — the one with 10× the data — is always the one who churns over performance. They're not unlucky; they're structurally on your tail.

🎯 System Design Interview Playbook

When this comes up

This is the numbers phase of nearly every system design interview: "Design Twitter / a news feed," "Design a URL shortener — what's the read/write ratio?", "How do you measure if this is fast enough?", and any time the interviewer says "now it's 10× the traffic." Showing you think in load parameters + percentiles is an instant signal you've operated real systems.

What to say (the checklist)

  • Define load parameters first: "Before I design, what's the read/write ratio, requests/sec at peak, and the distribution — e.g., are there celebrity/hot keys?" Estimate them out loud.
  • Pick the right performance metric: "This is an online service, so I care about response-time percentiles, not just throughput. I'll target p99, not the mean."
  • State a percentile SLO: "Let's say p99 < 200ms and p99.9 < 1s. That makes 'fast enough' measurable."
  • Reason about fan-out: "Reads dominate 65:1, so I'd pre-compute the feed on write (fan-out-on-write) — except for celebrities, who I'll merge at read time to avoid write amplification." (A hybrid is the senior answer.)
  • Name tail-latency amplification: "Since the page fans out to N services, the user-perceived p99 is worse than any single backend's p99, so I'll watch the slowest dependency and add timeouts/hedged requests."

The trade-off to articulate

"Fan-out is a write-cost vs read-cost trade governed by the read/write ratio and the follower distribution. Read-heavy + modest fan-out → pre-compute on write. Extreme fan-out (celebrities) → that single account would dominate write cost, so I special-case it to read-time merge. The right answer is load-dependent, not absolute."

⚠️ Common candidate mistake

Saying "the system should be fast" or quoting an average latency target. Also: declaring a design "scalable" as if it's a binary property. Senior engineers never call something "scalable" unconditionally — they say "it scales along this dimension; here's where it breaks and what we'd do next." And quoting throughput when the interviewer cares about per-request latency (or vice-versa).

⭐ Power move

Drop the tail-amplification math unprompted: "Because the page calls ~10 services in parallel, a 1% backend p99 becomes a ~10% chance the user hits a slow path — so I'd add per-call timeouts, hedged requests, and budget the tail across dependencies." Then connect it to revenue: "Amazon measured ~1% sales lost per 100ms." Talking percentiles + fan-out + business impact in one breath is a clean staff-level signal.

✗ Myth

"System X is scalable and system Y isn't — scalability is a property you have or don't."

✓ Truth

Scalability is never absolute. It's always "scales with respect to a particular load parameter." A design that scales beautifully for reads may collapse on writes. The real question is "if load grows this way, what are our options?"

✗ Myth

"If every backend service has a great p99, the overall user experience has a great p99 too."

✓ Truth

Tail-latency amplification: a request fanning out to N services hits a tail if any one is slow. With 10 services at 1% p99, ~9.6% of pages are slow. The user's tail is far worse than any single backend's tail.

"Response time and latency are the same thing."

✓ Truth

📜 How Twitter's timeline architecture evolved (tap each to expand)

v1 · Fan-out on read
One global tweet collection, merge at read time
Posting is trivial (one insert). But every home-timeline view must look up everyone you follow and merge-sort their recent tweets. At 300k reads/s this read path became the bottleneck — too much work repeated on every refresh.
v2 · Fan-out on write
Pre-compute a cached timeline per user
When you post, write a copy into every follower's cached timeline (in Redis). Reads become a single cheap lookup. Trade: a tweet now costs ~75 writes on average — fine, because reads vastly outnumber writes and reads are now instant.
v3 · The celebrity problem
One tweet → 30 million writes
A user with tens of millions of followers makes fan-out-on-write explode: a single post triggers tens of millions of cache writes, and a burst of celebrity tweets can't keep timelines fresh in time. The average follower count hid this — the distribution's tail revealed it.
v4 · Hybrid (today)
Fan-out for the masses, read-merge for celebrities
Most accounts use fan-out-on-write; a small number of high-follower accounts are excluded and merged in at read time. The follower distribution — not the request rate — is the load parameter that defines the architecture.
🤔 Pause & Think #1 — Your p50 is 30ms, p99 is 40ms, but a few users report 5-second loads. Where are those 5 seconds hiding?

The percentiles look fantastic — so what's going on?

Answer: A p99 of 40ms means 1% of requests are slower than 40ms — but it says nothing about how slow that 1% gets. The 5-second loads are above p99, living in the p99.9 / p99.99 / max region you're not measuring. Two things to do: (1) measure higher percentiles (p99.9, p99.99, and the max) — the tail beyond p99 can be enormous. (2) Remember tail events compound: if the slow user's page fans out to several services, tail-latency amplification means they hit a slow backend far more often than 1%. Also consider whether you're measuring on the server side and missing client-side queueing/network. The lesson: p99 is not "the worst case" — there's a whole distribution beyond it, and that's exactly where angry users live.

🤔 Pause & Think #2 — A read-heavy social feed at 65:1 reads:writes. Fan-out on write or on read? Now a Justin-Bieber account joins. Does your answer change?

Reason it out with load parameters, not gut feel.

Answer: For a normal account, the 65:1 read dominance screams fan-out on write: pay a modest write cost (~75 cache writes per tweet) so that the 65× more frequent reads become a single cheap lookup. The total work is far lower than re-merging on every read. But the celebrity breaks the math: their fan-out cost scales with follower count, so one tweet could mean tens of millions of writes — and worse, those writes spike unpredictably. For that account, fan-out on read (merge their handful of tweets in when a follower loads their feed) is cheaper and smoother. So the senior answer is a hybrid: the load parameter that flips the decision isn't the global read/write ratio — it's the per-author follower count. Same system, two strategies, chosen per account by where they sit in the distribution.

⚡ Quick Check #1

A page renders by calling 20 independent backend services in parallel and waits for all of them. Each service has a p99 of 1% (99% of calls are "fast"). Roughly what fraction of page loads will feel slow?

About 18%. The page is fast only if all 20 calls are fast: 0.99^20 ≈ 0.818, so 1 − 0.818 ≈ 0.182 → ~18% of loads hit at least one slow backend. That "1% p99" per service became a ~1-in-5 bad experience for users. This is tail-latency amplification, and it's why fan-out architectures must obsess over every dependency's tail (and use timeouts, hedged requests, and fewer synchronous hops).

⚡ Quick Check #2

Your manager says "let's set our SLA to a 100ms average response time." Why should you push back, and what should you propose instead?

An average SLA is gameable and meaningless: a service can be 5ms on a million cheap requests and 10 seconds on the thousand requests that matter, and still "pass." The average hides the tail where real users suffer. Propose a percentile SLO: e.g., "p99 < 200ms and p99.9 < 1s, measured client-side over rolling windows." This guarantees the worst common case, not just the bulk. Bonus: tie it to an error budget so you can trade velocity against tail-latency regressions explicitly.

✅ Key Takeaways

Load and performance are distributions, not numbers. Describe load with explicit load parameters (req/s, read:write ratio, follower distribution), and performance with the full response-time distribution.
The average lies; percentiles tell the truth. Use the median (p50) for the typical case and the tail (p95/p99/p99.9) for the worst case your power users actually feel. Outliers hijack the mean.
Tail-latency amplification is real. A request fanning out to N services hits a tail if any one is slow: 1 − pfast^N. In fan-out systems, the tail becomes the typical experience.
Fan-out is a write-cost vs read-cost trade. Read-heavy → pre-compute on write; extreme fan-out (celebrities) → merge on read. The follower distribution, not the request rate, drives the design.
"Scalable" is never absolute. Always ask "scales with respect to which load parameter, and where does it break?" Define percentile SLOs so "fast enough" is measurable.
