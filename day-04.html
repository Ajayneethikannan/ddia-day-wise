Scalability — Describing Load & Performance

Chapter 1 · Reliable, Scalable, Maintainable Applications · Describing Load and Performance
Course progress4 / 109 (3.7%)

🌩️ The Problem: Posting Is Easy. Reading Is What Kills You.

It's November 2012. Twitter is exploding. The engineering team stares at two numbers that don't seem related but are about to define their entire architecture:

Posting a tweet ........ 4,600 writes/sec (average), 12,000/sec (peak) Reading home timeline .. 300,000 reads/sec

Twelve thousand writes a second is nothing. A single beefy database can swallow that without breaking a sweat. So the team relaxes. The hard part is solved, right?

Wrong. The killer is that 300,000 reads/sec. Every time someone opens the app, Twitter has to assemble a personalized home timeline — the latest tweets from every single account that person follows, merged and sorted by time. Do that naively, and each of those 300k requests becomes a giant database query joining millions of rows. The database melts.

And it gets worse. The work isn't evenly spread. When an ordinary user with 75 followers posts a tweet, that's a tiny ripple. When Lady Gaga, with 30+ million followers, posts — the same single action triggers a tsunami of work somewhere in the system. One tweet. Tens of millions of timelines to update. Within seconds.

💡 Why this should grab you

"Will it scale?" is the wrong question — it has no answer. The right question is: "if our number of users grows 10×, or our power-users get 100× more followers, which specific part breaks first, and what are our options?" Scalability isn't a property you have or lack. It's a conversation about which dimension of load grows, and how performance degrades when it does. Today you learn to have that conversation precisely — the way staff engineers and FAANG interviewers do.

📖 What Is It? Load, Performance, and the Word "Scalable"

Over Day 1 (the three concerns) and Days 2–3 (reliability), we built the foundation. Today is the second of the three concerns: scalability — a system's ability to cope with increased load.

First, kill the word "scalable"

Kleppmann is emphatic: calling a system "scalable" (or "unscalable") as a one-dimensional label is meaningless. Scalability is always relative to a specific kind of growth. So we make it concrete with two precise tools:

  1. Describe the load — with numbers called load parameters.
  2. Describe the performance — with a distribution, not a single number.

Then scalability becomes a real, answerable question: "When this load parameter goes up, what happens to this performance number?"

Tool 1 — Load parameters

A load parameter is the number that best captures the stress on your system. There's no universal one — you pick what matters for your architecture:

SystemThe load parameter that matters
Web serverRequests per second (throughput)
DatabaseRatio of reads to writes
Chat / live appNumber of simultaneously active users
CacheHit rate
Twitter timelineDistribution of followers per user (the fan-out)

That last one is the whole ballgame for Twitter. The average might be 75 followers, but the distribution has a monstrous tail — celebrities with tens of millions. The average lies; the tail is where the system lives or dies. (Notice this is the second time today the tail of a distribution is the real story — hold that thought.)

Tool 2 — Performance: throughput vs response time

Two flavors, depending on the system:

  • Throughput — records processed per second, or total time to run a job. The metric for batch systems (Hadoop, ETL — we'll meet these around Day 80).
  • Response time — the time between a client sending a request and receiving the response. The metric for online systems. This is what your users actually feel.
💡 Latency ≠ Response time (the distinction interviewers love)

Response time is what the client sees: the full round trip = actual service time plus network delays plus queueing delays. Latency is narrower — the duration a request is latent, waiting to be handled, before it's even being serviced. People use them interchangeably; DDIA does not. The practical upshot: most of a bad response time is often not the work itself — it's the request sitting in a queue.

Why response time is a distribution, not a number

Send the exact same request a thousand times and you'll get a thousand different response times. Why? Random, unavoidable noise: a garbage-collection pause, a context switch to another process, a page fault forcing a disk read, a TCP packet retransmit, a network hiccup. So you must think of response time as a probability distribution — and summarize it with percentiles, never the average.

  • p50 (median) — the "typical" experience. Half of requests are faster, half slower.
  • p95 / p99 / p999 — the tail latencies. p999 means 1 in 1,000 requests is at least this slow.

Why obsess over the tail? Because the slowest requests often belong to your most valuable users — they have the most data (most tweets, most orders, most friends), so their requests do the most work. Amazon found that a 100ms increase at the high percentiles measurably reduced sales. The customer who waited the longest is frequently the one with the fullest shopping cart.

🧠 The Analogy: The Dinner Rush & the Mailroom

Percentiles → the restaurant on a Friday night

A restaurant boasts "average wait time: 10 minutes." Sounds great. But averages hide horror stories. On Friday at 8pm, most tables are seated in 5 minutes (p50), but one party of twelve waited an hour (p999) — and they were the celebrating-an-anniversary regulars who spend the most and tell the most friends. The average of "lots of fast + a few catastrophic" looks fine while your best customers walk out furious.

Mapping: diners = requests; seating time = response time; "average 10 min" = the misleading mean; the party that waited an hour = your p999 tail latency; the fact that they're high-value regulars = the slowest requests hitting your most valuable users. The kitchen's throughput (covers served per hour) is a different metric from any one table's response time — just like batch vs online systems.

And head-of-line blocking? If the kitchen has only four burners and one order is a slow 40-minute soufflé, every order queued behind it is late too — even the 2-minute salads. One slow request makes many fast ones appear slow. That's why you measure the wait from the customer's seat, not the chef's station.

Push vs pull fan-out → the apartment mailroom

Imagine an apartment building newsletter. Two ways to deliver it:

  • Pull (work at read time): nobody delivers anything. When you want your mail, you walk to the central archive and personally dig through every publisher's pile to find the items addressed to you. Cheap to publish, brutally expensive to read — and everyone reads constantly.
  • Push (work at write time): the moment a newsletter is published, a clerk looks up its subscriber list and drops a copy into each subscriber's personal mailbox. Reading is now trivial — you just open your own mailbox. But publishing a popular newsletter means the clerk runs around stuffing 30 million mailboxes.

Mapping: publishing = posting a tweet; reading your mail = loading your home timeline; the central archive dig = the read-time JOIN; your personal mailbox = your precomputed home-timeline cache; the clerk stuffing mailboxes = the fan-out-on-write service; the 30-million-subscriber newsletter = the celebrity problem.

💡 The aha

You can't make work disappear — you can only move it between write-time and read-time. Twitter's genius was realizing reads outnumber writes ~65:1, so paying at write time (push) is usually the better trade — except for celebrities, where you fall back to pull. The whole art of scalability is choosing when to pay, based on your load parameters.

Notice the mean sits to the right of the median — dragged there by the tail — yet it's nowhere near p99. If you alert on "average latency < 200ms," you can be totally green on the dashboard while 1% of users (your whales) are timing out. Measure percentiles, not means.

The tail-latency trap: amplification

One more gotcha. A single user-facing request often fans out into many backend calls (the timeline page needs tweets, profiles, ads, who-to-follow…). The end-user only sees the response once all of them return — so they wait for the slowest one. If each backend has a 1% chance of being slow, and the page makes 100 calls, the chance that at least one is slow is 1 − 0.99¹⁰⁰ ≈ 63%. A 1% backend problem becomes a 63% user problem. This is tail latency amplification, and it's why p99 of your services can quietly become p50 of your page.

💡 The real-world answer

Twitter started with pull, hit a wall, switched to push, then hit the celebrity wall. The final design is hybrid: ordinary users are fanned out on write into followers' caches; a handful of celebrities are excluded from fan-out, and their tweets are pulled and merged into each timeline at read time. Most timelines are still cheap cache reads; only the rare celebrity-follow path pays a small read-time cost. The right answer wasn't pull or push — it was knowing the load parameter (follower distribution) well enough to route each tweet down the cheaper path.

🌍 In the Wild

  • Twitter / X — the canonical hybrid fan-out. Home timelines are precomputed caches (Redis), celebrities are pulled at read time and merged. The follower distribution is the load parameter that drove the entire redesign.
  • Instagram & Facebook feed — same fan-out dilemma at even larger scale. Both lean on push-to-cache for most users with pull-based exceptions and aggressive ranking layers on top, mixing precomputation with read-time work.
  • Amazon — popularized SLOs on the p999, not the mean, because their highest-percentile requests came from their highest-value customers (most data → slowest queries). They measured response time at the client to catch head-of-line blocking and tail amplification.
  • Netflix & Google — fight tail latency with request hedging: fire a duplicate request to a second replica if the first is slow, and take whichever returns first. A direct countermeasure to tail latency amplification across many backend calls.
  • Observability stacks — Prometheus histograms, Datadog/Grafana p95/p99 panels, and libraries like HdrHistogram and t-digest exist precisely so you can compute high percentiles cheaply over rolling windows — because you cannot average percentiles.

🔍 The SDE 2 Lens

Where this hits your day job

You've already lived every concept in this lesson — you just may not have named it:

  • That Grafana panel showing "avg response time: 120ms" is lying to you. Add a p99 panel and you'll often find it's 10× the mean. The on-call alert that should page you is on the p99, not the average. Now you know why.
  • The "one slow endpoint takes down the service" incident. A single slow query holds a worker thread; your fixed-size thread pool fills; fast requests queue behind it. That's head-of-line blocking. The fix is often a separate pool / bulkhead, or a timeout — not "make the slow query faster."
  • Your load test passed but prod fell over. Your synthetic test sent uniform traffic and never simulated a "celebrity" — the one account, tenant, or partition with 1000× the data. Real load is skewed; your load parameter has a fat tail you didn't model.
  • The caching decision you make every sprint — "compute on read vs precompute and store" — is the push/pull fan-out trade-off. Denormalizing into a materialized view is fan-out-on-write. Now you can reason about it instead of guessing.
  • Closed-loop load generators undercount. Many tools wait for a response before sending the next request (closed loop), which artificially smooths the tail by not piling up requests the way real open-loop traffic does. Knowing this saves you from "but the load test was fine!"

🎯 System Design Interview Playbook

When this comes up

"Design Twitter / Instagram / a news feed," "Design a notification or activity-feed system," "Design a URL shortener / rate limiter" (load params + percentiles), and the scaling portion of any design round.

What to say (your script)

  • Name the load parameters first. "Reads vs writes? Active users? What's the fan-out — the follower distribution?" This signals seniority instantly.
  • Estimate the read/write ratio. "Reads dominate ~65:1, so I'll pay at write time — fan out on write into per-user timeline caches."
  • Pre-empt the celebrity problem yourself. "Pure push breaks for accounts with millions of followers, so I'll go hybrid: push for normal users, pull-and-merge for celebrities."
  • Talk performance in percentiles. "SLO: p99 timeline load < 200ms," never "average latency." Mention tail latency amplification across backend calls.
  • State the SLO/SLA explicitly with a percentile and a target, and say how you'd measure it (client-side, rolling window, histograms).
⚠️ Common candidate mistake

Quoting average latency and declaring victory — while ignoring the tail and head-of-line blocking. Equally common: proposing a pure read-time JOIN for a feed ("just query it") without realizing reads dominate and it won't survive the read load.

🚀 Power move

Do a back-of-envelope on write amplification: "12k tweets/sec × ~75 avg followers ≈ 900k cache writes/sec at fan-out — fine. But one celebrity tweet = 30M writes; pushing that within our few-second SLA is infeasible, so celebrities must be pulled." Then mention request hedging as a tail-latency mitigation. That trio — load parameter → write amplification math → hybrid + hedging — is staff-level reasoning.

🃏 Common Misconceptions

The myth

"Latency and response time are the same thing."

click to flip ↻
The truth

Response time is the full round trip the client sees (service + network + queueing). Latency is just the time a request spends waiting to be serviced. Most of a bad response time is queueing, not work — which is why you fix queues, not just code.

The myth

"Average response time tells me how my system is doing."

click to flip ↻
The truth

The mean hides the tail and is skewed by outliers. Your worst-served 1% (p99) are often your most valuable users. Use percentiles — and never average percentiles across machines; that's mathematically meaningless. Aggregate histograms instead.

The myth

"A system is either 'scalable' or it isn't."

click to flip ↻
The truth

Scalability is never a binary label. It's always "scalable with respect to which load parameter?" A design that scales to more users may collapse when one user gets a huge fan-out. Always pin the growth dimension before answering.

🧩 Interactive Zone

🤔 Pause & Think: Twitter handles 12k writes/sec and 300k reads/sec. Why is the bigger architectural headache caused by the smaller number?

Because the cost isn't symmetric. A write (posting) is a single insert — cheap, and there are "only" 12k/sec. But each read must assemble a personalized timeline from everyone you follow. Done naively (pull), each of the 300k reads/sec becomes a heavy multi-way JOIN. The read volume is 65× higher and each read unit of work is far heavier. That's why the system's center of gravity is the read path — and why moving work to write time (push) is the winning trade: you convert 300k expensive reads into cheap cache lookups, at the cost of making the rarer writes do more.

🤔 Pause & Think: Your service's p99 is 50ms. A page makes 50 independent calls to it. Roughly what fraction of page loads hit at least one slow (≥p99) backend?

"≥p99" means each call has a 1% chance of being slow. The chance that none of 50 calls is slow is 0.99⁵⁰ ≈ 0.605, so the chance that at least one is slow ≈ 1 − 0.605 ≈ 39.5%. Almost 40% of your page loads inherit a tail-latency event from a backend whose individual slow rate is only 1%. This is tail latency amplification: the more backend calls per page, the more your end-user latency is governed by your services' high percentiles, not their medians. Mitigations: fewer calls, parallelism with timeouts, and request hedging.

🤔 Pause & Think: Why should you measure response time on the client, not just on the server?

Two reasons. First, head-of-line blocking: the server may finish each request quickly, but if a few slow requests hog the limited worker threads, later requests sit in a queue. The server's own timer starts when it picks up the request — it never sees the queueing wait. The client does. Second, the client also experiences network delay and TCP retransmits. Server-side metrics can look healthy while users suffer. Always close the loop at the edge the user actually sits at.

⚡ Quick Check 1
You're defining an SLA for a checkout API. Which is the more meaningful target: "average response time < 200ms" or "p99 < 1s"?
p99 < 1s (and ideally a p50 target too). The average can be dragged around by outliers and hides the experience of your worst-served — often highest-value — customers. Percentile-based SLOs bound the tail that users actually feel. A good SLA usually specifies several percentiles, e.g. "median < 200ms AND p99 < 1s."
⚡ Quick Check 2
Your app is read-heavy with modest, evenly-distributed fan-out and no celebrity accounts. Push, pull, or hybrid?
Push (fan-out on write). Reads dominate, so paying at write time turns every read into a cheap cache lookup. With no celebrities and modest fan-out, the write amplification stays bounded, so you never hit the case that forces a hybrid. Hybrid only earns its complexity once a fat tail in the follower distribution appears.

🎒 Key Takeaways

1. "Scalable" is meaningless alone. Always ask: scalable with respect to which load parameter? Pick the number that captures stress on your architecture (read/write ratio, active users, fan-out).
2. Response time is a distribution, not a number. Summarize with percentiles (p50/p95/p99/p999), never the mean — and watch the tail, because it's your most valuable users.
3. Latency ≠ response time. Response time is the full round trip the client sees; latency is just the wait before service. Most bad response times are queueing, not work.
4. Scaling is moving work between write-time and read-time. Twitter's fan-out: pull (cheap write, costly read) vs push (costly write, cheap read), resolved by a hybrid driven by the follower distribution.
5. Beware tail latency amplification and head-of-line blocking: a 1% slow backend across many calls becomes a majority-slow page; measure client-side, and mitigate with bulkheads, timeouts, and request hedging.
