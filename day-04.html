Scalability — Describing Load & Performance

Chapter 1 · Reliable, Scalable, Maintainable Applications · Describing Load and Performance
Course progress4 / 109 (3.7%)

🐦 The Problem: The Tweet That Took 5 Seconds to Show Up

It's 2012. You open Twitter and pull down to refresh your home timeline. Most days it snaps in instantly. But during a major live event — an election, a World Cup goal, an awards show — the same refresh sometimes hangs. Two seconds. Five seconds. The little spinner mocks you while the most-talked-about moment of the year scrolls past everyone else.

Here's the twist that breaks intuition: Twitter's hard problem was never tweeting. Posting a tweet ran at a sleepy ~4,600 requests/second. The thing that nearly melted the system was the boring act of reading — assembling each user's home timeline ran at 300,000 requests/second, roughly 65× heavier. The same feature, viewed from two directions, had wildly different costs.

💡 Why this should change how you think

You cannot make a system "scalable" in the abstract. Scalability is always a question, not a property: "If load grows in this specific way, what are our options for coping?" And to even ask that question, you first need two things most engineers hand-wave: a precise way to describe load, and a precise way to describe performance. Get those wrong and every capacity decision after is a guess. Today we make both precise — using the exact case study Kleppmann uses: Twitter's timeline.

📖 What Is It? Load Parameters and Performance Numbers

Over Day 1 we named the three concerns; Days 2–3 finished Reliability (is it up?). Now we open Scalability — a system's ability to cope with increased load. Kleppmann insists scalability is not a one-dimensional label you slap on a system ("X scales!"). It's a structured conversation with two halves:

Half 1 — Describe the load with load parameters

A load parameter is the number that best captures the pressure on your system. The right choice depends on the architecture. Examples:

  • Requests per second to a web server (throughput of incoming work)
  • Ratio of reads to writes in a database
  • Number of simultaneously active users
  • Hit rate on a cache
  • For Twitter specifically: the distribution of followers per user — because that, not raw tweet volume, is what determines the fan-out work.

The art is picking the parameter that actually drives cost. For Twitter, "tweets per second" looks like the load — but the real driver is "how many timelines does one tweet need to be written into," which is governed by the follower distribution. Choosing the wrong load parameter is how teams get blindsided.

Half 2 — Describe performance with the right numbers

Once load is defined, ask two questions:

  • When you increase a load parameter and keep resources fixed, how does performance degrade?
  • When you increase a load parameter, how much do you need to grow resources to keep performance unchanged?

For batch systems we usually care about throughput (records processed per second). For online systems we care about response time — the time between a client sending a request and receiving the response.

💡 Latency ≠ response time (the distinction interviewers love)

Response time is what the client sees: service time + network delays + queueing delays. Latency is the duration a request is waiting to be handled — latent, awaiting service. They get used interchangeably, but Kleppmann keeps them separate: response time is the whole round-trip the user feels; latency is one component of it. Say it precisely and you sound senior.

The big idea: performance is a distribution, not a number

Even identical requests take different amounts of time — random extra latency from a context switch, a TCP retransmit, a GC pause, a cold cache, page faults. So "the" response time doesn't exist. You have a distribution of values, and you must describe it with percentiles, never a single average. We'll build the intuition for why averages lie in the next sections.

🧠 The Analogy: The Coffee Shop & the Group Photo

Load & throughput → the coffee shop at rush hour

Picture a café. Throughput is how many drinks the baristas complete per minute. Response time is how long you personally wait from ordering to sipping. Notice these are different: the shop can have great throughput (200 drinks/hour) while you wait 15 minutes because there's a queue. Add one customer who orders 12 custom lattes (a "celebrity tweet") and everyone behind them waits — a single heavy request inflates the response time of every request stuck behind it. That's head-of-line blocking, and it's why one slow request poisons many.

Mapping: baristas = servers/CPU; drinks/min = throughput; your personal wait = response time; the queue = queueing delay; the 12-latte order = a heavy request causing tail latency for everyone behind it.

Percentiles → grading a group photo, not the average face

Imagine you run a photo booth and want to know "how happy are people?" If you take the average smile, one ecstatic person and one miserable person cancel out to "meh" — the average hides both. The honest way: line everyone up worst-to-best and report "the 95th-worst-off person out of 100 still rated it a 7." That's a percentile. The p95 response time means 95% of requests were faster than this value and 5% were slower. The p99 and p999 (99.9th) describe your tail — the unlucky requests. Averages bury the tail; percentiles expose it.

💡 The aha

The customers stuck in your tail latency are often your most valuable ones. At Amazon, the slowest requests tend to come from accounts with the most data — the heaviest shoppers, who've bought the most. So the 1% experiencing the worst latency are precisely the people you least want to annoy. That's why Amazon optimizes for p999, not the median. An average would have told you everything was fine while your best customers quietly suffered.

⚙️ How It Actually Works

Let's make it concrete with Twitter's two approaches to building a home timeline — the canonical "describe the load, then choose the architecture" example. Then we'll nail down percentiles with a live distribution.

Animated architecture: fan-out on write (the "mailbox" model)

The naive read-time approach (Approach 1) is a SQL JOIN: when you open Twitter, query everyone you follow, merge and sort their recent tweets. Simple — but at 300k timeline reads/sec, that join is brutally expensive. So Twitter flipped it (Approach 2): do the work at write time. When you post a tweet, immediately push it into a per-follower home-timeline cache (a mailbox). Reads then become a cheap lookup of an already-assembled list.

One write fans out into N cache inserts (green = the amplified writes). Average user has ~75 followers, so 4.6k tweets/sec → ~345k cache writes/sec. That's a fine trade: writes are cheaper and rarer than the 300k/sec reads, and reads become trivially fast. But watch what happens when a celebrity posts…

Why the tail matters more than you'd think: tail latency amplification

Suppose serving one user page requires fan-out to multiple backend services in parallel, and you wait for all of them. Even if each backend is slow only 1% of the time (p99), the chance that at least one of them is in its slow tail rises fast with the number of calls. Call 100 backends and roughly 1 − 0.99¹⁰⁰ ≈ 63% of your page renders touch someone's slow tail. The end-user p99 is dominated by backend tails. This is tail latency amplification — and it's why a single slow node is everyone's problem.

Per-backend p99 = "1 in 100 calls is slow" Page calls 1 backend → P(hit a slow tail) = 1 − 0.99¹ ≈ 1% Page calls 10 backends → P(hit a slow tail) = 1 − 0.99¹⁰ ≈ 10% Page calls 100 backends → P(hit a slow tail) = 1 − 0.99¹⁰⁰ ≈ 63% Lesson: the more services a request fans out to, the more the SLOWEST backend defines the user's experience. Optimize tails, not means.

⚖️ The Trade-offs

The Twitter timeline forces the classic read-heavy vs write-heavy decision. Fan-out on read (a join at read time) keeps writes cheap but makes the hot path — reads — slow. Fan-out on write (the mailbox) makes reads trivial but pays a write-amplification tax that explodes for users with millions of followers. The right answer depends entirely on your load parameters: read/write ratio and follower distribution.

And the scaling axis: scale up vs scale out

Coping with load (tomorrow's topic) splits into vertical scaling (scale up — a bigger machine, simple but capped and pricey) and horizontal scaling (scale out — distribute across many machines, the "shared-nothing" approach, elastic but complex). Real systems mix both pragmatically. We'll go deep tomorrow.

🏢 In the Wild

Twitter — the textbook hybrid fan-out

Exactly as DDIA describes: most timelines are precomputed on write (mailbox model), but tweets from users with enormous follower counts are excluded from fan-out and merged in at read time. The load parameter that drove this entire architecture wasn't tweets/sec — it was the follower-count distribution. Measuring the distribution, not the average, is what made the design work.

Amazon — SLAs written on the 99.9th percentile

Amazon famously specifies response-time requirements in terms of p999, having found that the slowest 0.1% of requests hit customers with the most data — their best customers. They also quantified that a 100ms increase in latency cut sales by ~1%. Optimizing the mean would have been optimizing for the wrong customer.

Google & "The Tail at Scale"

Google's research paper formalized tail latency amplification and countermeasures like hedged requests (send the same request to two replicas, take the first to answer) and tied requests. When a single search fans out to thousands of leaf servers, the slowest leaf sets the response time — so the whole infrastructure is engineered around the tail.

Prometheus / Grafana / Datadog — percentiles as a first-class metric

Every modern observability stack reports histogram_quantile / p50/p95/p99 on dashboards and alerts on them, not on averages. The industry has fully internalized "averages lie." Computing percentiles efficiently at scale uses approximation algorithms like t-digest and HdrHistogram — you can't store every request, so you summarize the distribution.

CDNs & load balancers — head-of-line blocking is real

HTTP/1.1 suffered head-of-line blocking (one slow response stalls the connection); HTTP/2 multiplexing and then HTTP/3 (QUIC) were designed largely to kill it. The coffee-shop queue problem shows up at every layer of the stack.

🔍 The SDE 2 Lens

Where this shows up in your day job

You touch these ideas constantly — usually without naming them. Naming them is what turns "I made a dashboard" into "I reason about performance distributions."

  • Your latency dashboard already has p50/p95/p99 lines. Now you know why the p99 line is the one your SRE cares about and why "average latency is fine" in a postmortem is a red flag. The mean is hiding the customers who are actually suffering.
  • That alert that fires on "p99 > 500ms for 5 min." That's an SLO defined on a percentile — straight out of today. You'd never alert on the mean, because the mean can look healthy while the tail is on fire.
  • The "denormalize for read performance" decision. Precomputing a feed, materializing a view, maintaining a counter cache — that's fan-out-on-write thinking. You're moving work from the hot read path to the rarer write path. Same trade-off as Twitter's mailbox.
  • The one slow microservice that tanks your page's p99. That's tail latency amplification. When your endpoint awaits 8 downstream calls, your p99 is roughly the worst of their p99s — so chasing your own service's mean is useless if a dependency has a fat tail.
  • "It's fast on my machine / in the load test." Synthetic tests often miss the tail because they don't reproduce real queueing, GC pauses, and cache-cold paths. Production p99 is where truth lives.
💡 Ohhh, THAT'S why

…your SRE rejected the graph where you proudly showed "average response time dropped." They asked for p99 instead — because a deploy can lower the average while making the tail worse (e.g., a cache that helps most requests but adds a slow fallback for misses). The distribution moved in two directions at once, and only percentiles showed it.

🎯 System Design Interview Playbook

When this comes up

This is the back-of-the-envelope & SLO dimension of essentially every design round. It's the heart of "Design Twitter / a news feed / Instagram," and it shows up as the follow-up in any design: "What's your read/write ratio? How do you keep p99 low at 10× traffic? What happens to a celebrity user?"

What to say (the checklist)

  • State the load parameters first: "Let me define load — reads/sec, writes/sec, the read:write ratio, and the fan-out distribution (followers per user). Those drive the design."
  • Reason about the hot path: "Reads dominate ~65:1, so I'll optimize reads — precompute timelines with fan-out on write so reads are O(1)."
  • Handle the skew explicitly: "Celebrities break fan-out-on-write, so I'll go hybrid: fan out ordinary users on write, fetch mega-accounts at read time and merge."
  • Specify performance as percentiles + SLO: "Target p99 < 200ms, not an average. I'd alert on p99/p999 because the tail hits our most valuable users."
  • Name tail latency amplification: "Since the page fans out to N services, I'll watch the worst-case tail and use techniques like hedged requests to tame it."

The trade-off to articulate

"Fan-out-on-write trades cheap reads for expensive, amplified writes; fan-out-on-read does the reverse. The choice is dictated by the read/write ratio and the fan-out distribution — so I'd measure those before committing, and likely go hybrid to handle the skew."

⚠️ Common candidate mistake

Quoting average latency, and ignoring follower skew. Saying "the average user has 75 followers so fan-out-on-write is fine" — then having no answer when the interviewer says "Justin Bieber just tweeted to 100 million people." Averages and happy-path thinking are exactly what this chapter is warning you against.

⭐ Power move

Compute the write amplification out loud: "4.6k tweets/sec × ~75 avg followers ≈ 345k timeline writes/sec — acceptable. But a 30M-follower account means one tweet = 30M writes, so those go on the read path." Then drop tail latency amplification and hedged requests. Showing you separate describing load from coping with it — and that you reason in distributions, not averages — is a clear senior/staff signal.

✗ Myth

"A system either 'is scalable' or it isn't — it's a property you design in once."

✓ Truth

Scalability is always relative to a specific way load grows. "Scales with users" and "scales with data size" can demand opposite designs. You must name the load parameter first; there's no context-free "scalable."

✗ Myth

"Average (mean) response time is a good summary of performance."

✓ Truth

The mean hides the tail and isn't even "typical." Use the median (p50) for typical, and p95/p99/p999 for the tail — where your most valuable, data-heavy users often live. A few outliers can drag the mean far from anyone's real experience.

✗ Myth

"High throughput means every individual request is fast."

✓ Truth

Throughput and response time are different axes. A batching system can have huge throughput while any single request waits in a long queue (high response time). The café finishes many drinks/hour while you wait 15 minutes.

✗ Myth

"If each backend is 99% fast, the overall page will be ~99% fast too."

✓ Truth

🤔 Pause & Think #1 — Why does Twitter's hard problem live on the read side, not the write side?

Posting feels like "the action." Why is reading the timeline the expensive part?

Answer: Two reasons compound. First, raw volume: ~300k home-timeline reads/sec vs ~4.6k tweet-posts/sec — reads outnumber writes ~65:1, so the read path is the hot path by sheer frequency. Second, structural cost: assembling a timeline naively means joining everyone you follow, merging and sorting their recent tweets at request time — an expensive operation to run 300k times a second. The senior insight is that you should move work off the hot path. Since writes are rare, do the assembly work once at write time (push the tweet into each follower's mailbox) so the frequent reads become a trivial lookup. You trade a small amount of extra, rarer write work for a massive reduction in frequent read work. The only catch is skew — celebrities — which is why the real system is hybrid.

🤔 Pause & Think #2 — Your service's mean latency improved after a deploy, but users complain it feels worse. How?

The average went down. How can the experience get worse?

Answer: Because the mean is a single number summarizing a whole distribution, and a change can move different parts of the distribution in opposite directions. Classic case: you add a cache. Cache hits get much faster, pulling the mean (and p50) down. But cache misses now pay an extra lookup-then-fallback penalty, so the tail (p99/p999) gets worse. If your most valuable, data-heavy users tend to hit cold/uncached paths (they have the most unique data), they feel the regression even as your average looks better. This is exactly why you report percentiles, alert on p99, and never declare victory on the mean alone. The fix: look at the full distribution before and after — p50 down is nice, but check that p99/p999 didn't blow out.

⚡ Quick Check #1

Your API page makes 5 independent parallel calls and waits for all. Each backend has a p99 of "1 in 100 calls is slow." Roughly what fraction of page loads will hit at least one slow tail?

About 5%. P(no backend slow) = 0.99⁵ ≈ 0.951, so P(at least one slow) = 1 − 0.951 ≈ 4.9%. So your page's effective "slow rate" is ~5×worse than any single backend's 1% — this is tail latency amplification. Scale that to 100 calls and it's ~63%. The takeaway: when you fan out, the slowest dependency dominates your user-facing p99, so you must attack tails (timeouts, hedged requests, reducing fan-out), not just averages.

⚡ Quick Check #2

A teammate says "our app scales fine — we benchmarked 50k requests/sec." Why is that statement incomplete?

It conflates a single throughput number with "scalability," and omits everything that makes the claim meaningful. Missing pieces: (1) at what response-time percentiles? 50k/sec at a p99 of 5 seconds is not "fine." (2) what load parameter and mix? 50k cheap reads ≠ 50k heavy writes or 50k celebrity fan-outs. (3) how does it degrade past 50k, and how much must resources grow to handle 2×? Scalability is a relationship between load, resources, and a performance distribution — not a single peak number from a synthetic test that may not reproduce real queueing and tails.

✅ Key Takeaways

Scalability is a question, not a label. Always "if load grows this way, how do we cope?" Step one is naming the right load parameter — the number that actually drives cost (for Twitter: follower distribution, not tweets/sec).
Throughput ≠ response time; latency ≠ response time. Throughput = work/sec (batch). Response time = the full round-trip the client feels. Latency = the waiting portion of it. Keep them distinct.
Performance is a distribution — use percentiles, not averages. p50 for typical, p95/p99/p999 for the tail. Averages hide the tail, and the tail is often your most valuable users (Amazon optimizes p999).
Tail latency amplifies under fan-out. When a request waits on many backends, the slowest one defines the user's p99. Optimize and tame tails (timeouts, hedged requests), not just means.
Move work off the hot path. Fan-out-on-write makes frequent reads cheap by paying a rarer write tax; fan-out-on-read does the reverse. Choose by read/write ratio + skew — and go hybrid for the long tail (celebrities).
