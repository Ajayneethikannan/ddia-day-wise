Scalability — Averages Lie, Percentiles Tell the Truth

🌩️ The Problem: "Our API Is Fast" — Said the Dashboard That Lied

It's a launch week. Your team's service is humming. The big dashboard on the wall shows the headline number everyone trusts: average response time: 92 ms. Green. Beautiful. Leadership is happy.

Then support tickets start trickling in. Then flooding in. "The app froze." "It took 5 seconds to load my cart." "I clicked checkout twice because nothing happened." You stare at the dashboard. 92 ms. It's right there. How can it be both fast and unbearably slow?

Here's the brutal truth hiding inside that average: most requests really were ~90 ms. But 1 in 100 requests took 4 seconds — and those slow requests landed disproportionately on your biggest, most active, most valuable customers, because they have the most data and trigger the most backend calls. Your average was a comforting lie. The customers who matter most were living in the tail you couldn't see.

💡 Why this should change how you think

You cannot manage what you measure badly. Day 3 closed out Reliability — keeping the system up. Today begins Scalability, which starts with a deceptively hard question: how do you even describe load and performance precisely? Get the vocabulary wrong — say "average" when you mean "p99" — and every scaling decision you make afterward is built on sand.

📖 What Is It? Load, Performance, and the Right Numbers

Scalability is a system's ability to cope with increased load. But Kleppmann is emphatic: scalability is not a one-dimensional label you slap on a system ("X is scalable, Y isn't"). It's a question: "If the load grows in a particular way, what are our options for coping with it?" To even ask that question well, you need two precise vocabularies.

1. Describing load — the load parameters

You can't reason about growth until you name what is growing. The right numbers depend on your architecture — these are your load parameters:

  • Requests per second to a web server (throughput)
  • Ratio of reads to writes in a database
  • Number of simultaneously active users
  • Hit rate on a cache
  • Fan-out: how many downstream requests one user action triggers (the Twitter example, coming up)

The load parameter that actually stresses your system is often non-obvious. Sometimes the average case matters; sometimes a handful of extreme cases (a celebrity, a giant tenant) are the bottleneck.

2. Describing performance — and the response-time distribution

Once load is defined, ask: "When load increases (and resources stay fixed), how does performance suffer?" and "To keep performance unchanged, how much do we need to grow resources?" Two performance metrics matter:

  • Throughput — records/second or requests/second the system can handle. The headline metric for batch systems.
  • Response time — the time between a client sending a request and receiving the response. The headline metric for online systems.
💡 The distinction that trips up most engineers

Response time ≠ latency. Response time is what the client sees: the actual service time plus network delays plus queueing delays. Latency is the duration a request is waiting to be handled — latent, awaiting service. They're related but not the same, and conflating them hides where your time actually goes.

The core insight: response time is a distribution, not a number

Even for identical requests, response time varies on every call — a context switch, a TCP retransmit, a garbage-collection pause, a page fault, a slow disk seek. So you must think in distributions, not single values. And the right summary is percentiles, not the mean:

  • p50 (median) — half your users are faster, half slower. The "typical" experience.
  • p95 / p99 / p99.9 (tail latencies) — the slow requests. p99 = "99% of requests are faster than this; the worst 1% are slower."

The mean (average) is a poor summary: it doesn't tell you how many users actually experienced that delay, and a few extreme outliers can drag it around. The median answers "how long does a typical user wait?" — and the tail answers "how bad is it for my unluckiest (often most important) users?"

🧠 The Analogy: The Coffee Shop & the Group Photo

Percentiles → the morning coffee queue

Picture your favorite coffee shop. On average, you get your latte in 3 minutes. Sounds great. But the average is built from 95 quick customers and 5 people who ordered a tray of 12 custom drinks and waited 20 minutes. If you are one of those 5 — and the regulars who buy the most always seem to be — your "average" experience is irrelevant. What you feel is your wait, which lives in the tail.

Mapping: each customer = a request; service time = response time; the 5 huge orders = your power users with the most data; the 3-minute average = the lying dashboard; "how long the 99th unluckiest customer waits" = p99. The shop owner who only watches the average will never understand why their best customers are quietly leaving.

And here's the kicker — queueing. A barista can only make one drink at a time. If one customer ahead of you has a giant order, everyone behind them waits, even people with a simple order. This is head-of-line blocking: a slow request at the front inflates the response time of fast requests stuck behind it. It's why a small number of slow requests poisons the whole queue.

Tail-latency amplification → the group photo

You're organizing a group photo of 100 people. You can't take it until everyone is ready — and there's always that one person fixing their hair, that other one who blinked, that third one who wandered off. The photo waits for the slowest person, every time. With 100 people, the chance that at least one is slow is nearly certain.

Mapping: the photo = a single user-facing request; each person = one backend call that request fans out to; "wait for everyone" = the request isn't done until all backends respond. So if each backend is slow 1% of the time, a request touching 100 backends is slow almost every time. The rare tail of one service becomes the common case for the user. This is tail-latency amplification.

💡 The aha

Averages describe a crowd; you live as an individual in the tail. And when one user request depends on many backends, the tail isn't a rare edge case — it becomes the typical experience. That's why serious shops obsess over p99 and p99.9, not the mean.

⚙️ How It Actually Works

Kleppmann's signature example is Twitter. It perfectly shows how a load parameter — fan-out — drives an entire architecture decision. Twitter has two main operations:

  • Post tweet: ~4.6k requests/sec on average, ~12k/sec at peak. (A write.)
  • Home timeline: ~300k requests/sec — users reading the tweets of people they follow. (A read, ~65× more frequent than writes.)

The challenge isn't the raw tweet volume — it's the fan-out: each user follows many people, and each user is followed by many people. There are two ways to build the home timeline.

Animated architecture: fan-out on write (Twitter's approach)

When you post a tweet, Twitter doesn't compute timelines at read time. Instead it fans the write out: it looks up all your followers and inserts the tweet into a precomputed home-timeline cache (a "mailbox") for each one. Reads then become dirt cheap — just fetch your prebuilt timeline. Watch one tweet fan out to three followers' caches (green = write delivery), and a reader pulling instantly (blue = read).

Green = the write fanning out to each follower's cache (work done once, at post time). Blue = a reader fetching a fully-prebuilt timeline (work done never at read time). Since reads outnumber writes ~65:1, paying the cost at write time is the winning trade — until a celebrity shows up.

Illustrative M/M/1-style queueing model (response time ≈ exponential, mean = service / (1−ρ)). The takeaway is the shape: percentiles grow far faster than the mean as you approach saturation. This is why you should never run a latency-sensitive system near 100% utilization.

Why the tail explodes near saturation

This isn't bad luck — it's queueing theory. A server with limited parallelism can only handle a few requests at once. It takes a small number of slow requests to back up the queue (head-of-line blocking), and a small number of backed-up requests to make everything behind them slow. As utilization ρ approaches 1, waiting time grows roughly as 1/(1−ρ) — a hyperbola that shoots to infinity:

Response time ≈ service_time / (1 − ρ) (ρ = utilization) ρ = 0.50 → 2.0× service time (comfortable) ρ = 0.80 → 5.0× service time (getting warm) ρ = 0.90 → 10× service time (tail is screaming) ρ = 0.95 → 20× service time (danger) ρ = 0.99 → 100× service time (meltdown) Lesson: latency is not linear in load. The last 10% of utilization can cost you more than the first 80%.

⚖️ The Trade-offs

The Twitter fan-out story is fundamentally a trade-off about when you do the work: at write time (fan-out on write, precompute timelines) or at read time (fan-out on read, compute the timeline by querying on demand). The right answer depends entirely on your load parameters — especially the read/write ratio and the fan-out per user.

Interactive: fan-out on write vs. fan-out on read

Pick a user type and slide their follower count. Watch the cost shift. A normal user with a few hundred followers is cheap to fan out on write. A celebrity with tens of millions turns a single tweet into a tens-of-millions-write storm — so Twitter went hybrid: fan out on write for normal users, and fetch-on-read for the handful of celebrities, merging the two at read time.

💡 The senior reframe

There is no universally "scalable" design — only a design matched to a load profile. Twitter's brilliance wasn't picking write-fan-out or read-fan-out; it was recognizing that different users have different load parameters and routing each to the cheaper strategy. That hybrid instinct shows up everywhere in real systems.

🏢 In the Wild

Amazon — percentiles over averages, because the tail is your best customer

Amazon describes internal response-time requirements in terms of the 99.9th percentile, even though it affects only 1 in 1,000 requests. Why bother? Because the customers with the slowest requests are often those with the most data in their accounts — i.e., they've made the most purchases. They're the most valuable customers, and you absolutely do not want them to have a slow experience. (Amazon also found that even 100ms of extra latency measurably reduces sales — performance is revenue.)

Google's Dapper & "The Tail at Scale"

Google's famous paper The Tail at Scale formalizes tail-latency amplification: when a single user request fans out to thousands of servers, you're guaranteed to hit some server's slow tail. Their countermeasures — hedged requests (send to two replicas, take the first to answer) and tied requests — are now standard playbook for taming p99 in large fan-out systems.

Twitter — the hybrid timeline we just walked through

Twitter genuinely ran into the celebrity write-storm and built the hybrid: precomputed timelines for the masses, on-the-fly merge of celebrity tweets at read time. It's the canonical interview example of "scalability is choosing the right strategy per load parameter."

Prometheus / Grafana / Datadog — histograms, not averages

Modern observability tools record latency as histograms (buckets), precisely so you can compute p50/p95/p99 cheaply and accurately. Prometheus's histogram_quantile() exists because everyone eventually learns that avg(latency) is the metric that lies. (And you cannot average percentiles across machines — more on that in the SDE 2 lens.)

SLOs everywhere (Google SRE)

Service Level Objectives are almost always phrased as percentiles over a window: "p99 latency < 300ms, 99.9% of the time over 28 days." This is the direct operational descendant of today's lesson — turning "how fast?" into a precise, measurable, contractual number.

🔍 The SDE 2 Lens

Where this shows up in your day job

This lesson is the difference between an engineer who says "it's fast" and one who can prove — or disprove — it with the right number.

  • Your latency dashboards. If your team alerts on average latency, you have a blind spot the size of your tail. Switch alerts to p95/p99. The mean can look perfect while a tenth of users are suffering.
  • "Why is the service slow only sometimes?" That's the tail and queueing in action — GC pauses, a slow downstream call, a noisy-neighbor tenant, a request stuck behind a fat one (head-of-line blocking). It's usually not random; it's the distribution you're not measuring.
  • The cache hit-rate metric you tune. That's a load parameter. A drop from 95%→90% hit rate can double the load hitting your database — load parameters interact non-linearly.
  • Capacity planning & autoscaling thresholds. The 1/(1−ρ) curve is why you scale out at ~70–80% utilization, not 95%. The last slice of capacity buys runaway latency, not throughput.
  • Load testing. Beware the coordinated omission trap: naive load generators wait for slow responses before sending the next request, which accidentally hides the tail you're trying to measure. Drive open-loop load and report percentiles.
  • Designing a feed / notifications / activity stream. The Twitter fan-out decision is your decision the moment you build any "show me updates from things I follow" feature. Precompute or query-on-demand? It depends on your read/write ratio and fan-out — exactly like Twitter.
💡 Ohhh, THAT'S why

...you can't just average the p99 from each of your 10 servers to get the fleet p99. Percentiles don't average. The correct way is to aggregate the raw histograms and compute the percentile from the combined data. Averaging percentiles is one of the most common — and silently wrong — things engineers do with metrics.

🎯 System Design Interview Playbook

When this comes up

This is the "let's talk about scale and performance" phase of nearly every design — "Design Twitter / a news feed / Instagram," "How do you measure if this is fast enough?", and the follow-up to any architecture: "What happens to latency as load grows?" The fan-out problem and percentiles are two of the most reusable tools you can carry into the room.

What to say

  • Quantify the load first. "Let me estimate the load parameters: reads/sec, writes/sec, the read:write ratio, and the fan-out per action." Numbers before architecture signals seniority.
  • Talk in percentiles, never averages. "I'd target p99 latency, not the mean — the tail is where real users suffer, and the slow requests often hit our most valuable users."
  • Use fan-out on write vs read explicitly. "For the feed I'd precompute timelines (fan-out on write) since reads dominate, but go hybrid for high-follower accounts to avoid write storms."
  • Name tail-latency amplification for any high-fan-out design, and reach for hedged requests / timeouts / backups to tame it.
  • Tie utilization to latency: "I'd scale out around 70–80% utilization because response time grows like 1/(1−ρ) near saturation."

The trade-off to articulate

"It's work-at-write-time vs. work-at-read-time. Precomputing makes reads cheap but writes expensive and storage heavier; computing on read is the reverse. I choose based on the read/write ratio and fan-out — and I'll happily go hybrid when the load profile isn't uniform across users."

⚠️ Common candidate mistake

Saying "make it scalable" or "it should be fast" without a single number. Equally bad: optimizing the average. Interviewers immediately downgrade candidates who can't separate throughput from response time, or who think one design is universally "scalable" regardless of load profile.

⭐ Power move

Drop the line: "There's no such thing as a scalable architecture in the abstract — only one matched to a load profile. So let me pin down the load parameters." Then estimate them, choose fan-out-on-write with a hybrid celebrity path, and specify SLOs in percentiles (p99 < 300ms). Mentioning tail-latency amplification and hedged requests by name is a staff-level signal most candidates never reach.

v2 · Fan-out on write

v3 · The celebrity problem
One tweet → 30+ million writes
A user with tens of millions of followers turns a single post into a write storm, and a flurry of celebrity tweets must all be delivered within a tight window. Pure fan-out-on-write buckles on these outliers.
v4 · Hybrid
Fan-out for most, merge celebrities on read
Normal users: fan-out on write. Celebrities: skip the fan-out and fetch their tweets at read time, merging them into the requesting user's precomputed timeline. Each load profile gets the cheaper strategy. The canonical scalability answer.
🤔 Pause & Think #1 — Your p50 is 40ms but p99 is 2.5s. What's the most likely cause, and where do you look?

A small fraction of requests are wildly slower than typical — classic tail behavior. What produces a tail like this?

Answer: The huge gap between median and p99 points to occasional, not systematic, slowness — the work itself is usually fast (40ms), so the extra 2.46s is mostly waiting, not computing. Prime suspects: (1) Queueing / head-of-line blocking — you're running near saturation, or a few fat requests are stalling the queue behind them. (2) GC / runtime pauses — a stop-the-world pause freezes whatever request is in flight. (3) A slow dependency on the tail — one downstream call (DB, cache miss → DB, a lock) occasionally spikes, and if your request fans out, tail-latency amplification makes it likely. (4) Noisy neighbors — another tenant/process stealing CPU or I/O. Where to look: per-endpoint and per-dependency percentile breakdowns, utilization (is ρ near 1?), GC logs, and request size distribution. The fix menu: shed/limit load to lower ρ, separate big requests from small ones, add timeouts + hedged requests for fan-out, and right-size the runtime. Note what won't help much: optimizing the median path — that's already fast.

🤔 Pause & Think #2 — You're designing Instagram. Would you fan out on write or read for the home feed? What about for "likes"?

Apply the read/write ratio + fan-out reasoning to two different features.

Answer: For the home feed, reads vastly outnumber posts, so default to fan-out on write (precompute each user's feed) — but go hybrid for high-follower accounts to dodge write storms, exactly like Twitter. For likes, the shape is different: likes are extremely high-write, and what you usually need is an aggregate (the count) plus "did I like this?". You wouldn't fan a like out to millions of viewers' precomputed state; you'd keep a counter (often approximate/eventually-consistent) and check the viewer's own like status on read. The lesson: different features within the same app have different load parameters and deserve different strategies. Naming that explicitly is the senior move.

⚡ Quick Check #1

Your service sits at 95% CPU utilization and p99 latency is awful. A teammate says "we're at 95%, only 5% headroom — barely need to scale." Why are they dangerously wrong?

Because latency vs. utilization is not linear — it grows like 1/(1−ρ). At ρ=0.95, response time is already ~20× the raw service time, and the curve goes vertical from here: nudging to 0.97 or 0.99 doesn't cost "a little more," it causes a meltdown. "5% headroom" in utilization is actually negative headroom in latency. You should run latency-sensitive systems around 70–80% and scale out well before the cliff. The remaining capacity isn't usable throughput — it's a latency landmine.

⚡ Quick Check #2

A single user request to your service fans out to 50 backend microservices, and it can't return until all 50 reply. Each backend is slow (>1s) just 1% of the time. Roughly how often is the user's request slow — and what's this called?

The request is fast only if all 50 backends are fast: P(all fast) = 0.99⁵⁰ ≈ 0.605. So the request is slow about 1 − 0.605 ≈ 40% of the time — even though each individual backend is slow only 1%! A rare tail at the component level becomes the common case at the user level. This is tail-latency amplification (the "group photo" problem). Mitigations: hedged/tied requests (ask two replicas, take the first), aggressive timeouts with fallbacks, and reducing fan-out where possible.

✅ Key Takeaways

Scalability is a question, not a label. Ask "if load grows this way, what are our options?" — never "is it scalable?" in the abstract. Always name the load parameter.
Describe load with load parameters. Requests/sec, read:write ratio, active users, cache hit rate, fan-out. The parameter that stresses your system is often non-obvious — and outliers (celebrities) can dominate.
Response time is a distribution; use percentiles. The mean lies. Median = typical experience; p95/p99/p99.9 = the unlucky, often most valuable users. And response time = work + network + queueing, which is not the same as latency.
The tail explodes near saturation. Response time grows like 1/(1−ρ); run latency-sensitive systems at ~70–80%, not 95%. And tail-latency amplification makes rare component tails the common user experience under high fan-out.
Match strategy to load profile. Fan-out on write (cheap reads) vs on read (cheap writes) — pick by read/write ratio and fan-out, and go hybrid when users differ. There is no one scalable design.
