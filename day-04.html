Scalability — Describing Load & Performance

Chapter 1 · Reliable, Scalable, Maintainable Applications · Describing Load and Performance
Course progress4 / 109 (3.7%)

🌩️ The Problem: The System That Worked Perfectly — Until 6 Million People Showed Up

Your app is flawless. It passed code review, it has tests, it has canaries and rollback (all the Day 3 guardrails). On your laptop, a home-page request returns in 40ms. In staging with 100 fake users, still 40ms. You ship it. For weeks, life is good.

Then your app gets featured. Traffic goes from 1,000 users to 6,000,000 users in an afternoon. Nothing crashed — every server is up, every health check is green. But page loads that took 40ms now take 9 seconds. The database that hummed along is at 100% CPU. Users see spinners, rage-tap, and leave. Your perfectly reliable system is failing — not because anything broke, but because it can't handle the load.

Here's the trap: someone in the war room says "is our system scalable?" — and that question is meaningless. There is no checkbox that makes a system "scalable." Worse, your monitoring dashboard shows "average response time: 280ms" and everyone relaxes... while 1% of your users — 60,000 people, including your biggest customers — are staring at 9-second loads. The average lied to you.

💡 Why this should change how you think

"Scalable" is not a label you attach to a system; it's a question you investigate: "If load grows in this specific way, what are our options?" To even ask it properly you need two precise vocabularies — one to describe load, and one to describe performance. Today we build both. And we'll learn why the single most common metric engineers quote — the average — is the one you should trust least.

📖 What Is It? Load Parameters & Performance Numbers

Over Day 1–Day 3 we tackled Reliability — keeping the system working correctly even when things go wrong. Today we pivot to the second of the three big concerns: Scalability — a system's ability to cope with increased load. But Kleppmann is emphatic: scalability is not a one-dimensional label ("X is scalable, Y is not"). It's about asking, "If the system grows in a particular way, what are our options for coping?"

Step 1 — Describe the load (load parameters)

You can't reason about growth until you can describe it with numbers. The right numbers depend on your system's architecture. These are called load parameters:

  • Requests per second to a web server (and the read/write split)
  • Ratio of reads to writes in a database
  • Number of simultaneously active users
  • Hit rate on a cache
  • Something domain-specific — e.g. for Twitter, the distribution of followers per user

Choosing the right load parameter is the whole game. For Twitter, "tweets per second" is almost a red herring — the parameter that actually breaks the system is the fan-out: how many followers each user has. We'll see exactly why in How It Works.

Step 2 — Describe the performance

Once load is defined, ask: "When I increase a load parameter and keep resources fixed, how is performance affected?" and "How much do I need to increase resources to keep performance unchanged?" To answer, you need performance metrics. Two dominate:

MetricDefinitionWho cares
ThroughputNumber of records processed per second, or total time to run a job on a dataset.Batch systems (Hadoop, ETL) — "how much work per hour?"
Response timeThe time between a client sending a request and receiving the response — what the user actually experiences.Online systems (web, APIs) — "how long does the user wait?"
💡 Response time ≠ latency (a subtlety interviewers love)

Kleppmann distinguishes them: response time is what the client sees — it includes network delays and queueing delays on top of the actual service time. Latency is the duration a request is waiting to be handled — latent, awaiting service. People use them interchangeably, but the precise version: response time = service time + latency (queueing/network). The queueing part is exactly where tail latencies are born.

Step 3 — Stop quoting averages. Use percentiles.

Response time is not a single number — it's a distribution. The same endpoint can return in 30ms a thousand times and 5s once (a GC pause, a TCP retransmit, a cold cache, a context switch). Reporting the average (mean) hides this completely. Instead, sort your response times and read off percentiles:

  • p50 (median) — half of requests are faster than this. The "typical" experience.
  • p95 / p99 / p99.9 (tail latencies) — the worst 5% / 1% / 0.1%. These are the slow requests that hurt your most valuable users — the ones with the most data, the fullest carts, the longest histories.

Amazon famously talks in terms of p99.9, because the customers experiencing the slowest requests are often the ones who have made the most purchases — i.e., the most valuable. They observed that a 100ms increase in response time reduces sales by ~1%. Optimizing the tail directly protects revenue.

🧠 The Analogy: The Restaurant & the Supermarket Checkout

Load & performance → a busy restaurant

Picture a restaurant. The load parameters aren't just "number of customers" — they're how many tables, the size of each party, how many order at once, the ratio of quick coffees to seven-course meals. A restaurant that breezes through 50 solo coffee-drinkers will collapse if 50 people arrive as one wedding party all ordering simultaneously. Same headcount, completely different load. Choosing the right load parameter is choosing "wedding party" over "headcount."

Throughput vs response time: Throughput is "how many meals the kitchen serves per hour." Response time is "how long YOUR food takes to arrive after you ordered." A kitchen can have great throughput (1,000 meals/hour) while your personal response time is terrible (you waited 50 minutes) — because you were stuck in a queue behind the wedding party. Throughput is the kitchen's view; response time is the diner's view.

Percentiles & tail latency → the supermarket checkout

You're at a supermarket with 20 checkout lanes. On average, checkout takes 3 minutes — sounds great. But you happened to get behind someone doing a price-check on 60 items and paying with coins: you wait 22 minutes. That's your p99. The store manager looking at the average sees a healthy 3 minutes and thinks everything's fine. You, the customer who'll never come back, lived the tail.

Head-of-line blocking: even fast shoppers stuck behind the coin-payer wait 22 minutes too — through no fault of their own order. A few slow requests at the front of a queue make many fast requests slow. That's why a server with a few slow handlers can drag down everyone's response time.

💡 The aha

"Average checkout time" is the metric the manager loves and the customer never feels. Nobody experiences the average. Each real person experiences their own wait — and your unhappiest, most-likely-to-churn customers are living in the tail. Measure p95/p99, not the mean, because that's where you lose people.

⚙️ How It Actually Works: Twitter's Fan-Out Problem

DDIA's signature example. Twitter has two main operations: post a tweet (~4.6k requests/sec average, ~12k at peak) and view your home timeline (~300k requests/sec). Notice: reads dominate writes by ~65×. The naive design — "on home-timeline read, go find everyone this user follows and merge their recent tweets" (fan-out on read) — was crushed by that 300k read rate. So Twitter flipped it: do the expensive work at write time instead.

Animated architecture: fan-out on write

When you post a tweet, Twitter doesn't wait for readers. It looks up your followers and immediately pushes the tweet into a per-user "home timeline" cache for each follower. Reads then become trivially cheap — just read your own precomputed timeline. Watch the amber write fan out to the followers' caches; the green reads are then instant.

Amber = the write fanning out (expensive, but rare). Green = reads served straight from a precomputed cache (cheap, and there are ~65× more of them). Twitter traded expensive reads for expensive writes — a great deal when reads vastly outnumber writes. But watch what happens when one user has 30 million followers...

The math: why fan-out on write usually wins (and when it breaks)

The key load parameter isn't tweet volume — it's the distribution of followers per user. For an average user, fanning out a tweet to 75 caches is trivial. But a celebrity with 30M followers turns one tweet into 30 million cache writes. If that celebrity tweets during a live event, you get a write storm.

Fan-out on WRITE (Twitter's choice for most users): cost(post) = O(followers) ← expensive only when followers is huge cost(read) = O(1) ← read your precomputed timeline. CHEAP. Reads (300k/s) ≫ Writes (4.6k/s) → pay the cost on the rare side. WIN. Fan-out on READ (the naive design): cost(post) = O(1) ← just store the tweet once cost(read) = O(followees) ← merge everyone you follow, every load. EXPENSIVE. The celebrity wall: one tweet × 30,000,000 followers = 30,000,000 writes → 💥 Twitter's real answer: a HYBRID. - Normal users → fan-out on write (push) - Celebrities → fan-out on read (followers merge celeb tweets at read time) - Best of both: bounded write cost, still-cheap reads for the common case.

⚖️ The Trade-offs

The fan-out decision is the canonical "describe the load, then pick the strategy" exercise. The right answer flips entirely depending on your read:write ratio and your follower distribution. There is no universally "scalable" choice — only the right choice for a given load.

And the deeper trade-off behind performance measurement: chasing the tail (p99.9) is expensive — it often means over-provisioning, since the slowest requests are caused by the rarest, hardest-to-predict events. You must decide which percentile is worth the cost. Reducing p99.9 from 2s→1s may need 2× the hardware; reducing the median may be nearly free.

🏢 In the Wild

Twitter — the textbook hybrid fan-out

The exact case from DDIA. Twitter started with fan-out on read, hit a wall at ~300k timeline reads/sec, switched to fan-out on write (push to per-user timeline caches), then hit the celebrity wall. Today they run a hybrid: most users are push, the few accounts with tens of millions of followers are pull, and the two are merged when you load your timeline. A perfect illustration that "scalability" is about matching strategy to load distribution.

Amazon — optimizing for p99.9, not the average

Amazon discovered the customers with the slowest requests are often their best customers — they have the most data (largest order history, most items) and thus the heaviest queries. So Amazon targets p99.9 (the 99.9th percentile) in its internal service SLOs, reasoning that the tail is where their revenue is. They've cited that every 100ms of added latency costs ~1% in sales. Going further to p99.99 was judged not worth the cost — a deliberate trade-off.

Google & the "tail at scale"

Google's research paper "The Tail at Scale" shows why tail latency explodes in distributed systems: if a single user request fans out to 100 backend services and you need all of them to respond, then even a 1-in-100 slow service (p99) means almost every user request waits on at least one slow backend. This is tail latency amplification — the more services you call, the more your effective latency is governed by the tail, not the median. Mitigations: hedged requests (send to two, take the first), tied requests, and request reissues.

Service SLOs/SLAs everywhere (AWS, Stripe, Cloudflare)

Production SLAs are written in percentiles, not averages: "p99 latency < 200ms" or "99.9% of requests succeed within 300ms." Monitoring tools (Datadog, Prometheus + Grafana, Honeycomb) all surface percentile histograms precisely because averages mask the tail. When you see a Grafana panel with p50/p95/p99 lines, that's this lesson made operational.

Facebook / Discord / Instagram feeds

Every "feed" or "timeline" product faces the same fan-out decision. Instagram and Facebook use push-based delivery for most users with pull-based handling for high-fan-out accounts, plus aggressive ranking and caching. Discord, with its massive servers, similarly can't naively fan out a message to millions of members in real time and engineers around the hot-channel problem.

🔍 The SDE 2 Lens

Where this shows up in your day job

You touch load and percentiles constantly — you just may not have named them. Here's the translation.

  • That Grafana/Datadog panel with p50, p95, p99 lines. Now you know why it's not just an average: the average is the number that's fine right up until customers are churning. When you investigate "the API feels slow sometimes," you look at p99, not the mean.
  • "Average response time looks fine but users complain." Classic tell that your tail is bad. A handful of slow requests (a missing index on a big customer's data, a cold cache, a GC pause) live in the p99 and never move the average. Pull the percentile graph.
  • Your load test that "passed." Load tests are only meaningful if they reproduce the real load parameters — the right read:write ratio, payload sizes, concurrency, and the long-tail of big accounts. A test with 100 uniform users tells you nothing about the wedding-party day.
  • The read replica / cache you added. That's "describe the load (read-heavy), then cope" — you moved read work off the primary. Same instinct as Twitter precomputing timelines.
  • That async job / message queue. Often a fan-out-on-write in disguise: do expensive work once at write time (precompute, denormalize, push to a queue) so reads stay cheap.
  • The "noisy neighbor" / one slow endpoint dragging others down. Head-of-line blocking. A few slow handlers occupy your worker pool/threads and queue everyone behind them. This is why you isolate slow endpoints and bound concurrency.
💡 Ohhh, THAT'S why

...your SRE keeps saying "don't optimize the average, optimize the tail," and why your SLO is written as "p99 < 300ms" instead of "average < 300ms." And it's why the same feature can be free for 99% of users but melt the DB for your one whale customer with 10 years of data — that customer is your p99.9.

🎯 System Design Interview Playbook

When this comes up

This is the "how does it scale?" backbone of almost every interview — and it's the explicit topic in "Design Twitter / a news feed," "Design Instagram," "Design a notification system." The fan-out trade-off is one of the single most-asked design discussions at FAANG. The percentile/SLO discussion comes up whenever you're asked about latency or capacity.

What to say (the checklist)

  • Quantify load FIRST, before any architecture: "Let me estimate the load — DAU, read:write ratio, peak QPS, payload size, and the distribution (e.g., followers per user). Numbers drive the design." (This is the back-of-envelope step interviewers wait for.)
  • Identify the right load parameter: "For a feed, raw write volume isn't the killer — it's the fan-out distribution and the read:write ratio. Reads dominate ~65:1, so I'll optimize reads."
  • Propose fan-out on write for the common case: "Precompute each user's timeline on write so reads are O(1) cache hits."
  • Pre-empt the celebrity problem with a hybrid: "Pure push breaks for accounts with millions of followers, so those go pull and get merged at read time." (Bringing this up unprompted is a strong signal.)
  • Specify performance in percentiles + SLOs: "Target p99 < 200ms, not an average. I'd measure p50/p95/p99/p99.9 and set SLOs against the tail."

The trade-off to articulate

"Fan-out on write trades expensive writes for cheap reads — ideal when reads ≫ writes — but it breaks on high-fan-out accounts, so I'd use a hybrid. And on performance: chasing p99.9 costs real money (over-provisioning), so I'd pick the percentile target that matches the business value of the tail — like Amazon optimizing p99.9 because their slowest requests are their richest customers."

⚠️ Common candidate mistake

Saying "I'll just add more servers / it'll auto-scale" without ever quantifying the load or naming a strategy. Also: quoting average latency, or treating "scalable" as a yes/no property. And forgetting the celebrity/hot-key case entirely — interviewers always probe it. Horizontal scaling doesn't save you from a single hot key or a bad fan-out design.

⭐ Power move

Drive the back-of-envelope yourself and reach the fan-out trade-off, then volunteer the hybrid and tail-latency amplification: "Since a feed request fans out to many services, my effective latency is governed by the tail — so I'd use percentile SLOs and consider hedged requests." Naming tail latency amplification and p99.9 with the Amazon reasoning marks you as senior/staff — most candidates never get past 'add a cache.'

🃏 Common Misconceptions (tap a card to flip)

✗ Myth

"Scalability is a property — a system either 'is scalable' or it isn't."

✓ Truth

It's not a label, it's a question: "if load grows in THIS specific way, what are our options to cope?" A system can scale beautifully on one dimension (more users) and fall apart on another (bigger payloads, hotter keys). Always ask "scale along which axis?"

✗ Myth

"Average response time is a good summary of how fast my system is."

tap to reveal truth →
✓ Truth

The average hides the tail and nobody experiences it. One 10s outlier and a thousand 30ms requests can average to a cheery number while real users suffer. Use the median (p50) for "typical" and p95/p99/p99.9 for the pain. Measure the distribution, not the mean.

✗ Myth

"Latency and response time are the same thing."

tap to reveal truth →
✓ Truth

Response time is what the client sees end-to-end (service time + network + queueing). Latency is the time a request spends waiting to be serviced. The queueing component is where tail latency is born — and why a few slow requests block many fast ones (head-of-line blocking).

✗ Myth

"When load grows, just add more machines — horizontal scaling solves everything."

tap to reveal truth →
✓ Truth

More machines don't fix a bad shape of load — a single hot key (the celebrity), a fan-out storm, or a head-of-line-blocking endpoint won't spread across nodes. You first have to describe the load and pick a strategy (hybrid fan-out, partitioning, caching). Hardware is step two, not step one.

🤔 Pause & Think #1 — Your dashboard shows average latency = 120ms and it's flat. Users are furious about slowness. What's going on and what do you check?

The flat, healthy average is a classic false comfort. The likely story: your tail is bad while the bulk is fine. A subset of requests — maybe your largest customers, a slow query path, a cache-miss path, GC pauses, or a degraded replica — are taking seconds, but they're rare enough that they don't move the mean.

What to check: (1) Pull up p95/p99/p99.9, not the average — that's where the pain is. (2) Look at the distribution/histogram; a bimodal shape (a fast hump + a slow hump) screams "two code paths." (3) Segment by customer/endpoint/region — tail latency often concentrates in one big account (the p99.9 = your whale) or one endpoint suffering head-of-line blocking. (4) Correlate the slow requests with payload size or row counts. The fix usually targets those specific slow requests (an index, a cache, request hedging), not the already-fine median.

🤔 Pause & Think #2 — Why does fan-out on write beat fan-out on read for Twitter, even though writes do way more total work?

Because total work isn't what matters — work on the hot path is. Twitter serves ~300k timeline reads/sec but only ~4.6k posts/sec — reads outnumber writes ~65:1. Fan-out on write moves the expensive merge off the read path (which happens constantly) and onto the write path (which is comparatively rare). You "pay" 65× less often.

It's the same instinct as caching, denormalization, or precomputing a materialized view: do the expensive work once, at write time, so the frequent operation stays cheap. The catch is the celebrity — a single write to 30M followers — which is why pure push needs a hybrid escape hatch. The general principle: optimize the operation that dominates your load, and put the cost on the rarer side of the ratio.

⚡ Quick Check #1

A request to your service fans out to 50 independent backends and needs all 50 to reply. Each backend has p99 = 1s and p50 = 10ms. Roughly what does the user's latency look like, and why?

The user is slow most of the time — this is tail latency amplification. Each backend is slow (≥1s) for 1% of calls. With 50 independent calls and needing all of them, the chance that at least one is in its slow tail is 1 − (0.99)^50 ≈ 1 − 0.605 ≈ 39%. So roughly 40% of user requests wait on at least one 1s backend, even though any single backend is fast 99% of the time. The median backend (10ms) is almost irrelevant to the user experience. Mitigations: hedged/tied requests (fire to two replicas, take the first), reduce fan-out, or relax the "need all" requirement. This is exactly Google's "Tail at Scale."

⚡ Quick Check #2

An interviewer asks "Is your design scalable?" What's a senior answer vs. a junior answer?

Junior: "Yes, it's scalable — we can add more servers / it auto-scales." (Treats scalability as a yes/no property and reaches for hardware.)

Senior: "Scalable along which axis? Let me name the load — say DAU, read:write ratio ~65:1, peak QPS, and the follower distribution. Reads dominate, so I'd precompute timelines (fan-out on write) with a hybrid for high-fan-out accounts, partition by user, and set p99 SLOs. If writes or hot keys become the bottleneck instead, the strategy changes." The senior version describes the load, ties strategy to it, names the failure modes (hot keys, tail), and treats hardware as a consequence, not the answer.

✅ Key Takeaways

"Scalable" is a question, not a label. Always ask "scale along which axis?" Describe the load first; the right strategy depends entirely on it.
Pick the right load parameter. For a feed it's the fan-out distribution and read:write ratio, not raw request count. Optimize the operation that dominates your load.
Throughput vs response time. Throughput = work/sec (batch view). Response time = what the user waits (online view). They can be great and terrible at the same time.
Averages lie — use percentiles. Report p50 for typical, p95/p99/p99.9 for the tail. Your unhappiest, most valuable customers live in the tail. Set SLOs there.
Move cost to the rare side. Fan-out on write trades expensive (rare) writes for cheap (frequent) reads — with a hybrid escape hatch for hot keys / celebrities. Beware tail-latency amplification when requests fan out.
