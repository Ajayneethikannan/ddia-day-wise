Scalability — Describing Load & Performance

Chapter 1 · Reliable, Scalable, Maintainable Applications · The Twitter Fan-out Problem & Why Averages Lie
Course progress4 / 109 (3.7%)

🌩️ The Problem: Your P95 Is Fine and Your Users Are Furious

You run the dashboard. Average API latency: 42 ms. The graph is a flat, comforting green line. Every SLO is green. And yet support tickets keep piling up: "the app freezes for seconds," "checkout hangs," "it's unusable at 8pm." Engineering swears there's no problem — the metrics prove it. The metrics are lying.

Here's the twist. That 42 ms average is dominated by the millions of trivial requests that return instantly. Hidden in the tail, 1 in 100 requests takes 2 full seconds. That doesn't sound like much — until you realize a single page load makes 50 backend calls, so the odds that at least one of them lands in that slow 1% is about 40%. Four in ten of your page loads are janky. And the users hitting the slow path aren't random — they're your power users with the most data, the biggest carts, the most followers. The average told you everything was fine while your best customers left.

💡 Why this should unsettle you

Reliability (Days 2–3) asked "is it up?" Today asks a harder question: "as load grows, does it stay fast — for everyone, not just on average?" That's scalability. And the very first skill is deceptively humble: learning to describe load and performance with numbers that don't lie to you. Get the numbers wrong and every capacity decision you make afterward is built on sand.

📖 What Is It? Scalability, Load Parameters & Response Time

Kleppmann is careful to kill a lazy phrase first. People say a system "is scalable" or "doesn't scale" as if scalability were a one-dimensional label you stamp on a box. It isn't. Scalability is a system's ability to cope with increased load — but the only useful way to reason about it is to ask concrete questions: "If the load grows in this specific way, what are our options for coping with the growth?"

To ask that question well you need two vocabularies: one for describing load, and one for describing performance.

1 · Describing load — "load parameters"

You can't discuss growth until you can put a number on the current pressure. Kleppmann calls these load parameters. Which numbers matter depends entirely on your system's architecture:

SystemThe load parameter that actually matters
Web serverRequests per second
DatabaseRatio of reads to writes
Chat / social appNumber of simultaneously active users
CacheHit rate
Twitter timelineDistribution of followers per user (not just user count!)

The last row is the whole game. The right load parameter is often not the obvious "total users" — it's some distribution or ratio that captures where the real cost lives. Picking the wrong load parameter is how teams get blindsided.

2 · Describing performance — throughput vs response time

Once load rises, two questions follow:

  • If you keep resources fixed and increase load, how does performance degrade?
  • If you increase load, how much extra resources do you need to keep performance unchanged?

Two numbers answer these:

  • Throughput — records processed per second, or total time to run a job over a dataset. The headline metric for batch systems (Hadoop, ETL).
  • Response time — the time between a client sending a request and receiving a response. The headline metric for online systems.
💡 A distinction interviewers love: response time ≠ latency

Response time is what the client sees: service time + network delays + queueing delays. Latency is the duration a request is waiting to be handled — latent, awaiting service. They're related but not the same, and the difference (queueing) is where most tail-latency pain hides.

3 · The key mental leap — response time is a distribution, not a number

Even for identical requests, response time varies: a context switch, a TCP retransmit, a garbage-collection pause, a page fault, a busy disk. So a single "average response time" is meaningless. You must think in terms of a distribution — and describe it with percentiles, not the mean.

🧠 The Analogy: The Coffee Shop Queue & the Airport Security Line

Load parameters → the coffee shop's real bottleneck

Imagine a café owner planning for growth. The naive metric is "customers per day." But the number that actually determines whether the line explodes is the distribution of order complexity — a shop of 500 filter-coffee regulars flows fine, while 200 customers each ordering an 8-ingredient oat-milk-half-caff-art-latte will jam the machine and snake the queue out the door. Same "customer count," wildly different load. Mapping: "customers/day" = the tempting-but-wrong load parameter (total users); "order complexity distribution" = the right load parameter (like Twitter's followers-per-user). The senior move is finding the parameter that captures where the cost really concentrates.

Percentiles → the airport security line you actually remember

The airport advertises an "average wait of 10 minutes." Reassuring. But you don't experience the average — you experience your wait. Most days you're through in 8 minutes (that's the p50, the median). But 1 day in 20 (p95) a bag gets flagged and you wait 25 minutes. And 1 day in 100 (p99) you're pulled for a full search and miss your flight — 90 minutes. The average of 10 minutes never once described your actual experience, and the day that ruins your trip lives entirely in the tail. Mapping: average wait = the mean latency that hides pain; p50 = the typical user's experience; p95/p99/p999 = the tail, the increasingly rare but increasingly catastrophic slow requests. Airlines and airports are judged on the tail, and so is your service.

💡 The aha

Averages describe a system; percentiles describe an experience. Your users don't live in the average — each one lives in a single sample, and it's the slow samples they remember, tweet about, and churn over. That's why the whole industry writes SLOs as "p99 < 200 ms," never as "average < 200 ms."

⚙️ How It Actually Works

Kleppmann's running example is Twitter, and it's the single best illustration of "describe the load before you design the system." The load isn't "tweets per second" — it's the fan-out: one tweet must reach every follower's home timeline. How you handle that fan-out is the entire architecture decision.

Twitter's two numbers

Post tweet: ~4,600 requests/sec average (12,000/sec at peak) Home timeline: ~300,000 requests/sec → Reads outnumber writes ~65:1. The load is READ-dominated. → So: is it cheaper to do work when a tweet is WRITTEN, or when a timeline is READ?

Animated architecture: fan-out on write

Twitter's answer (approach 2) is fan-out on write: when you tweet, the system immediately pushes that tweet into a precomputed home-timeline cache for every one of your followers. Reads then become trivial — just fetch your already-assembled timeline. Watch a single tweet fan out to three followers' timeline caches:

One write becomes many writes — one per follower. Reads (300k/sec) get cheap because the timeline is pre-assembled. The cost moved from read-time to write-time. Great... until a celebrity tweets and "many" means 30 million cache writes for a single tap.

Why the mean is worse than useless here

Samples (ms): 12, 14, 16, 19, 23, 28, 35, 51, 89, 1900 Mean (average) = 218.7 ms ← describes NOBODY. Dragged up by one outlier. p50 (median) = 23 ms ← the typical request. Half are faster, half slower. p95 = 89 ms ← 95% of requests beat this. p99 / p99.9 = 1900 ms ← the tail. Your biggest customers live here. The mean is 9x the median. If you report "avg = 219ms" you sound broken. If you report "median = 23ms" you hide the 1900ms tail that's losing you money. Only the FULL percentile picture tells the truth.
💡 Tail latency amplification

In a fan-out architecture (like Twitter, or any microservice that calls many backends to build one response), the slowest backend sets the pace. If each backend has a 1%-slow tail and you call 100 of them in parallel, the chance at least one is slow is 1 − 0.99¹⁰⁰ ≈ 63%. So a 1-in-100 backend problem becomes a majority-of-requests problem at the front door. This is why Google engineers obsess over p99.9, not just p50.

⚖️ The Trade-offs

Back to Twitter's fan-out decision. There is no free lunch — you're choosing when to pay the cost, not whether.

The other big trade-off: scaling up vs scaling out

Coping with load has two broad strategies (we'll go deep tomorrow):

Scale up (vertical)Scale out (horizontal)
WhatA bigger, more powerful machineMany smaller machines (shared-nothing)
ProSimple — no distributionCheap commodity HW, near-limitless
ConCost grows super-linearly; a ceilingDistributed-systems complexity
RealityGood designs pragmatically mix both. Elasticity (auto-scaling on load) vs manual scaling is a further axis.

🏢 In the Wild

Amazon — the $ value of the tail (p99.9, not the mean)

Amazon describes internal response-time requirements in terms of the 99.9th percentile, even though it affects only 1 in 1,000 requests. Why bother? Because the customers with the slowest requests are precisely those with the most data in their accounts — i.e. the most purchases, the most valuable customers. Amazon also found that a 100 ms increase in latency reduced sales by ~1%, and others report a 1-second slowdown cutting customer satisfaction by 16%. The tail isn't an edge case; it's where your revenue is.

Twitter — the canonical fan-out story

Twitter genuinely started with fan-out on read (a global tweet table + query-time join), hit a wall at read scale, and moved to fan-out on write with per-user home-timeline caches. When that broke for celebrities, they adopted the hybrid now described in DDIA. It's the textbook example of "describe the load parameter (follower distribution) before choosing the architecture."

Google — tolerating the tail with hedged requests

Because a single search fans out to thousands of servers, Google lives and dies by tail latency. Their "The Tail at Scale" work introduced tricks like hedged requests (send the same request to two replicas, take whichever answers first, cancel the other) and tied requests to keep p99 in check despite unavoidable per-server jitter.

Monitoring stacks — why percentiles are hard to compute

Computing percentiles cheaply on a firehose of requests is non-trivial (you can't keep every sample). Real systems use approximate streaming algorithms: HdrHistogram, t-digest, and forward-decay methods. Prometheus histograms, Datadog, and Envoy all expose p50/p90/p99 this way. And beware: you cannot average percentiles — averaging the p95 of ten machines does not give you the fleet p95. You must aggregate the histograms.

🔍 The SDE 2 Lens

Where this shows up in your day job

You touch these ideas every sprint — usually without naming them. Naming them is what turns "the API feels slow sometimes" into a diagnosable, fixable problem.

  • That Grafana/Datadog dashboard showing "avg latency." Now you know it's the wrong panel. Switch it to p50/p95/p99. The average that looks healthy is hiding the tail your users actually feel — exactly the hook at the top of this page.
  • The SLO in your team's runbook: "p99 < 300 ms." That's a percentile, not an average, and for good reason. When you're on call, you're being paged because the tail broke, not the mean.
  • Your service that calls 8 downstream microservices to build one response. That's fan-out, and it's tail-latency amplification in the wild: your response is only as fast as your slowest dependency. This is why one flaky downstream tanks your p99 even when its p50 is fine.
  • The "hot key" / "celebrity user" bug you've debugged. One user with 10× the normal data or fan-out is the follower-distribution load parameter biting you. The fix is rarely "more servers" — it's handling the skewed key specially (a hybrid, like Twitter).
  • Load tests that look great then fall over in prod. Usually because the test used uniform synthetic load and missed the real load parameter (the skew, the read/write ratio, the queueing under burst). Test with a realistic distribution, not a flat one.
  • Averaging p95 across pods in a rollup. A subtle, common bug. You can't mean-of-percentiles; you have to merge histograms. If your dashboard does the former, its "fleet p95" is fiction.
💡 Ohhh, THAT'S why

...your latency alert stays quiet while users complain, and why the senior engineer's first question in an incident is "what's the p99, not the average?" and "is this a hot key?" They're not being pedantic — they're refusing to let a lying number set the agenda.

🎯 System Design Interview Playbook

When this comes up

Constantly — it's the back-of-the-envelope phase of every design. "Design Twitter/Instagram/a news feed" is the fan-out question verbatim. "How would you handle 1M requests/sec?" is the scale-out question. And any senior interviewer will probe: "How do you measure that it's fast? What's your latency target?" — that's your cue to say "percentiles."

What to say (the checklist)

  • Estimate load parameters first: "Let me nail the numbers — reads/sec, writes/sec, read:write ratio, and the key distribution (e.g. followers per user). The architecture follows from these."
  • State targets in percentiles: "My latency SLO is p99 < 200 ms, not an average — averages hide the tail that users actually experience."
  • Name the fan-out choice: "For a feed, I'd fan out on write to precompute timelines since reads dominate, but fall back to fan-out on read for celebrity accounts — a hybrid — to dodge write amplification."
  • Address tail-latency amplification: "Since one request fans out to many services, I'll watch p99.9, add timeouts + hedged requests, and keep the fan-out width bounded."
  • Scale out, shared-nothing: "I'd scale horizontally with stateless services behind a load balancer and partition the data, rather than betting on one giant box."

The trade-off to articulate

"Fan-out is a question of when you pay: on write (cheap reads, expensive writes, celebrity problem) or on read (cheap writes, expensive reads). Given a read-heavy feed, I pay on write for most users and on read for the few whose fan-out is enormous."

⚠️ Common candidate mistake

Jumping straight to boxes and databases without ever quantifying the load, then optimizing the wrong thing. The classic tell: designing a write-optimized system for a 65:1 read-heavy workload — or quoting "average latency" as your performance target. Both signal you skipped the "describe the load and performance" step entirely.

⭐ Power move

Compute the tail-amplification probability out loud: "Each shard is 1%-slow at p99; my query hits 50 shards, so 1 − 0.99⁵⁰ ≈ 40% of user requests touch a slow shard. That's why I target p99.9 per shard and add hedged requests." Pairing a load parameter with a percentile target and the amplification math is a staff-level signal almost nobody produces.

✗ Myth

"Our average response time is low, so the system is fast."

✓ Truth

The mean is dominated by the many fast requests and dragged around by outliers — it describes nobody. Users experience individual requests, and the slow ones (the tail) are what they remember. Always report p50/p95/p99, never the average alone.

✗ Myth

"Scalability is a property — a system either 'scales' or it doesn't."

✓ Truth

Scalability is meaningless in the abstract. It's always relative to a specific load growth: "if this parameter grows this way, what are our options?" A system that scales beautifully for reads may collapse on writes. Name the load parameter first.

✗ Myth

"The p99 of my whole fleet is the average of each server's p99."

✓ Truth

You cannot average percentiles. The mean of ten p95 values is not the fleet p95. To combine, you must aggregate the underlying histograms (HdrHistogram, t-digest) and recompute. Averaging percentiles is a real, common dashboard bug.

✗ Myth

"To handle more load, just add more servers — scaling is automatic."

✓ Truth

Adding nodes doesn't help a skewed hot key (the celebrity), and it introduces distributed-systems complexity. Some load (a single mega-follower fan-out) needs an architectural change, not more of the same. There's no magic auto-scale for a bad load model.

🤔 Pause & Think #1 — Reads outnumber writes 65:1 on Twitter. Why does that ratio push you toward fan-out on write?

It feels backwards to do more work on the rarer operation. Why is it right?

Answer: Because you want to make the frequent operation cheap, even at the cost of making the rare one expensive. Reads happen 65× more often than writes, so total system work ≈ (65 × read cost) + (1 × write cost). Fan-out on read makes each of those 65 reads expensive (a join across everyone you follow) — you pay the join 65 times. Fan-out on write makes the single write expensive (one push per follower) but every one of the 65 reads becomes a trivial cache fetch. You've moved the cost off the hot path and onto the cold one, and paid it once instead of 65 times. The catch — and the reason for the hybrid — is that "write cost" scales with follower count, so for a celebrity the single write becomes 30M writes and the arithmetic flips. The load parameter that decides everything isn't the read:write ratio alone; it's the follower distribution.

🤔 Pause & Think #2 — Your dashboard shows p50 = 20 ms, p99 = 25 ms, but users complain of multi-second freezes. What's going on?

The percentiles look tight and healthy. Where's the lie?

Answer: Several usual suspects. (1) You're not measuring the right layer. Server-side p99 excludes queueing before your server, network/TLS, client rendering, and retries — the user's end-to-end response time can be seconds while your service-time p99 is 25 ms. (2) You're averaging percentiles across pods, smoothing away a single bad instance whose real p99 is multi-second. (3) Your sampling window is too coarse — a 5-minute p99 hides a 10-second burst of slowness during a GC pause or a lock storm. (4) The freeze is fan-out amplification: one page = 50 calls, so even a healthy 25 ms p99 per call yields a slow call on ~40% of page loads, and the user's perceived latency is the max, not the per-call p99. The senior instinct: measure end-to-end at the client, don't average percentiles, use fine windows, and reason about the max across a fan-out — not each call in isolation.

⚡ Quick Check #1

A service's response-time samples (ms) are: 10, 11, 12, 13, 900. Your teammate reports "average latency ≈ 189 ms, we have a problem." Is that the right conclusion, and what should they report instead?

The average is technically 189.2 ms but it's a misleading summary — it's dragged up entirely by the single 900 ms outlier and describes none of the five requests. The right framing: p50 = 12 ms (typical request is snappy), but there's a real tail problem — 1 in 5 requests took 900 ms. So the honest report is "median is great at 12 ms, but our p95/p99 tail is ~900 ms and needs investigation." That both avoids crying wolf about the average and surfaces the genuine tail issue the mean accidentally hinted at. Report the distribution, not one number.

⚡ Quick Check #2

You're designing Instagram's feed. Most users have a few hundred followers, but influencers have 50M+. Which fan-out strategy do you choose, and why not just pick one?

Use a hybrid. For the vast majority of users (normal follower counts), fan-out on write — precompute followers' feeds so the read-heavy timeline load is cheap. But detect high-follower accounts and switch them to fan-out on read: don't push an influencer's post to 50M caches (that's 50M writes per post — a write storm and a hot-partition nightmare). Instead, at read time, merge each user's precomputed feed with a fresh pull of the handful of celebrities they follow. Picking only fan-out on write dies on the celebrity write amplification; picking only fan-out on read makes the common 300k reads/sec expensive. The load parameter — the follower distribution — is bimodal, so the architecture is too.

✅ Key Takeaways

Scalability is a question, not a label. Never say "it scales." Say "if this load parameter grows this way, here are our options." Pick the right load parameter first — often a distribution or ratio, not total count.
Response time is a distribution — use percentiles. The mean is dragged by outliers and describes nobody. Report p50/p95/p99/p99.9. Users live in individual samples, and they remember the slow ones.
The tail is where the money is. The slowest requests often belong to your most valuable users. Amazon targets p99.9; a 100 ms slowdown cost ~1% of sales. SLOs are percentiles for a reason.
Fan-out is about when you pay. Fan-out on write = cheap reads, expensive writes, celebrity problem. Fan-out on read = the reverse. Read-heavy feeds favor write-time work, with a hybrid for mega-accounts.
Fan-out amplifies the tail. One request hitting many backends is only as fast as its slowest dependency: 1 − 0.99ⁿ. Watch p99.9, bound fan-out width, and consider hedged requests. And never average percentiles.
