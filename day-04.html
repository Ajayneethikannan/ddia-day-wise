Scalability — Describing Load & Performance

Chapter 1 · Reliable, Scalable, Maintainable Applications · Twitter's Fan-Out Problem & Why Averages Lie
Course progress4 / 109 (3.7%)

🌩️ The Problem: "It Works Fine on My Laptop" (and Then Lady Gaga Tweets)

Your team ships a feature. In dev, with one user, it returns in 20 ms. Beautiful. You launch. For the first thousand users, still snappy. Then a tweet goes viral, traffic 50×'s in an hour, and suddenly the same endpoint takes 8 seconds — or times out entirely. Nothing in the code changed. The only thing that changed was load.

Here's the trap that catches almost everyone: you measured the wrong thing. Your dashboard proudly showed an average response time of 200 ms during the spike, and you reported "we're fine, average is healthy." But behind that comfortable average, 1 in 100 of your most important customers — the ones with the most data, the ones who buy the most — were waiting 2+ seconds and quietly leaving. The average didn't lie exactly. It just hid the people who mattered.

💡 Why this should change how you think

"Scalability" is not a yes/no property you can stamp on a system ("is it scalable?"). That question is meaningless. The real questions are: (1) how do I describe my load precisely? and (2) how do I describe performance honestly — not with an average that flatters me, but with a number that captures the customers I'm actually losing? Today is about learning the vocabulary to ask those two questions like a senior engineer. Get the vocabulary wrong and every capacity decision you make afterward is built on sand.

📖 What Is It? Load Parameters & Performance, Defined

On Day 1 we named the three concerns of a data system; Days 2–3 finished Reliability ("is it up?"). Today we open Scalability — Kleppmann's word for a system's ability to cope with increased load. But before you can cope with load, you have to be able to describe it. So this lesson is the measurement half; tomorrow (Day 5) is the coping half.

Step 1 — Describe load with "load parameters"

A load parameter is whatever number best captures the pressure on your system. There's no universal one — it depends on your architecture. Examples:

  • Requests per second to a web server (throughput of an online service)
  • The ratio of reads to writes in a database
  • The number of simultaneously active users
  • The hit rate on a cache
  • For Twitter (the running example): the distribution of followers per user — and this turns out to be the parameter that breaks everything.

Step 2 — Describe performance two ways

Once you know your load, you ask two questions:

  • When you increase a load parameter and keep resources fixed — what happens to performance? (It degrades — by how much?)
  • When you increase a load parameter — how much do you need to increase resources to keep performance unchanged?

And "performance" itself splits in two, depending on the kind of system:

MetricWho caresWhat it measures
ThroughputBatch systems (e.g. Hadoop)Records processed per second, or total time to run a job over a dataset.
Response timeOnline systems (e.g. a web API)The time between a client sending a request and receiving the response — what the user actually experiences.

Step 3 — The crucial distinction: response time ≠ latency

People use these interchangeably; Kleppmann is careful, and so should you be:

  • Response time = what the client sees end-to-end = service time + network delays + queueing delays.
  • Latency = the duration a request is waiting to be handled — latent, awaiting service.
💡 The mental model to lock in

Response time is not a single number — it's a distribution of values you must measure. Every request is a little different: one hits a warm cache, the next triggers a slow GC pause, another waits behind a fat request in a queue. If you collapse that distribution into a single "average," you've thrown away exactly the information you need. The senior skill is to think in percentiles, not averages — which we'll make visceral in a moment.

🧠 The Analogy: The Post Office & the Airport Security Line

Fan-out → the post office's two delivery strategies

Imagine you run a town's mail. Someone famous writes a newsletter that 10 million townsfolk subscribe to. You have two ways to deliver it:

  • Strategy A (deliver on demand): Keep every newsletter in one big archive. When a resident wants their mail, you run to the archive, find everything from everyone they subscribe to, sort it by date, and hand it over. Cheap to file, brutally expensive to read — every reader does a big merge.
  • Strategy B (pre-sort into mailboxes): The moment a newsletter arrives, you photocopy it 10 million times and stuff one copy into each subscriber's personal mailbox. Now reading is instant — just open your box. But the write is monstrous: one famous newsletter = 10 million copy-and-stuff operations that must finish before everyone "has their mail."

Mapping: the archive = a global tweet collection; Strategy A = compute the home timeline at read time; Strategy B = maintain a per-user timeline cache and fan out writes; the famous newsletter = a celebrity tweet; "stuff 10M mailboxes" = the fan-out write amplification. This is the Twitter problem, and the load parameter that decides which strategy hurts is the distribution of followers.

Percentiles → the airport security line

The TSA brags that the average wait is 5 minutes. But you have a flight to catch. What you actually care about is: "of the last 1,000 travelers, how long did the unluckiest ones wait?" If 1 in 100 waited 45 minutes (got pulled for a bag search, stuck behind a family of eight), then the 99th percentile is 45 minutes — and if that's you, the 5-minute average is cold comfort.

Mapping: a traveler = a request; wait time = response time; the average = the comforting lie; the 99th percentile (p99) = the experience of your unluckiest 1% of requests; the bag-search outlier = a slow GC pause, a cold cache, or a request stuck behind a big one. Amazon famously optimizes for p99.9 because the customers with the slowest requests often have the most data on their account — i.e., they're the best customers. The tail is where your money is.

💡 The aha

Both analogies expose a trade-off the happy path hides. Fan-out trades cheap reads for terrifying writes (or vice versa) — there's no free lunch, only a choice of where the pain lands. And percentiles reveal that "average is fine" can coexist with "we're losing our best customers." Describing load and performance precisely is what lets you see these hidden truths before they bankrupt you.

⚙️ How It Actually Works

Let's use Kleppmann's exact running example: Twitter's home timeline, circa 2012. Two operations dominate:

Post tweet: ~4.6k requests/sec average, ~12k requests/sec at peak Home timeline: ~300k requests/sec (read your feed) The home timeline read is ~65× more frequent than the write. So Twitter's pain is NOT ingesting tweets — it's FANNING THEM OUT to everyone's home timeline fast enough.

Animated architecture: a tweet fanning out to followers' timelines

This is Approach 2 (write-time fan-out). A user posts one tweet; the system looks up that user's followers and inserts the tweet into each follower's home-timeline cache. Watch one write explode into many. The orange (faster) path is a celebrity with millions of followers — the write that hurts.

One write in, N writes out. For an average user (~75 followers) that's fine. The killer is the distribution: a celebrity with 30M followers means a single tweet triggers 30 million cache inserts — and they're supposed to land within a few seconds. That long tail of the follower distribution is the load parameter that makes naive fan-out fall over.

Step-through: how Twitter actually solved it (the hybrid)

Step 1 of 5 · The load parameter
Twitter's challenge isn't the 4.6k tweets/sec written — it's the 300k/sec home-timeline reads. Each read must merge tweets from everyone a user follows, sorted by time. The question: do we pay this cost at read time or write time?

Describing performance: percentiles, not averages

Same dataset, wildly different stories. The mean is dragged around by outliers and tells you about nobody in particular. p50 is "a typical user." p99/p99.9 are your unluckiest 1% / 0.1% — the tail where churn and lost revenue hide. SLOs and SLAs are written in percentiles for exactly this reason.

Why the tail is so fat: tail-latency amplification

Here's the cruel multiplier in backend systems. Suppose one user-facing request fans out to many backend calls (microservices, database shards), and waits for all of them. Even if each backend is fast 99% of the time, the slowest of the batch sets your response time. The more calls you fan out to, the more likely at least one is slow:

P(a single call is slow) = 1% (i.e. above p99) P(at least one of N is slow) = 1 − (0.99)^N N = 1 call → 1.0% of user requests are slow N = 10 calls → 9.6% of user requests are slow N = 100 calls → 63.4% of user requests are slow ← !! A request that touches 100 backends is slow MOST of the time, even though every backend is "99% fast." This is tail-latency amplification — and it's why p99 of your backends becomes p50 (or worse) of your user-facing latency.

⚖️ The Trade-offs

The core trade-off in the fan-out problem: where do you pay the cost — at read time or write time? Since Twitter reads ~65× more than it writes, paying at write time is usually the win… until a celebrity makes the write cost explode. Drag the sliders to feel it.

🏢 In the Wild

Twitter — the hybrid timeline, exactly as DDIA describes

Twitter started with read-time merge (Approach 1), hit a wall, switched to fan-out-on-write (Approach 2) for fast reads, then hit the celebrity problem and landed on the hybrid: tweets from users with huge follower counts are not fanned out — they're fetched and merged into the timeline at read time, while everyone else's tweets are fanned out on write. The load parameter that drove every one of these decisions was the distribution of followers per user.

Amazon — optimizing for p99.9, not the mean

Amazon describes internal response-time requirements in terms of the 99.9th percentile, even though it affects only 1 in 1,000 requests. Why bother? Because the customers with the slowest requests are often those with the most data on their accounts — i.e., they've made the most purchases. They're the most valuable customers, and a slow experience loses them. Amazon also found that a 100 ms increase in latency reduced sales by ~1%; others report a 1-second slowdown cutting customer satisfaction metrics meaningfully.

Google & the "tail at scale"

Google's research on The Tail at Scale formalized tail-latency amplification: when a single search query fans out to thousands of leaf servers and waits for all, the p99 of the leaves becomes the typical user latency. Their mitigations — hedged requests (send a duplicate to a second replica if the first is slow) and tied requests — are now standard tools for taming the tail.

Percentile-based SLOs everywhere (Prometheus, Datadog, k8s)

Modern monitoring is built around this lesson. You'll see histograms and histogram_quantile() in Prometheus, p50/p95/p99 latency panels in Datadog/Grafana, and SLOs phrased as "99% of requests under 300 ms over 28 days." Nobody serious alerts on average latency anymore — they alert on the tail.

CDNs & cache hit rate as a load parameter

For read-heavy services (news, media, e-commerce), the single most important load parameter is often the cache hit rate. A 95% → 99% hit-rate improvement isn't a 4% win — it can 5× reduce the load that reaches your origin (misses drop from 5% to 1%). Cloudflare/Fastly/Akamai exist to move that parameter.

🔍 The SDE 2 Lens

Where this shows up in your day job

You touch these concepts constantly — usually without naming them. Naming them is what turns "the dashboard looks fine" into "I know exactly which customers we're losing."

  • Your Grafana/Datadog latency panel. When you set up a dashboard, do you graph average latency or p50/p95/p99? If it's the average, you're flying blind to the tail. Switch to percentiles — that's today's lesson made literal.
  • The "denormalize for read performance" debate. Every time you precompute a feed, a counter, or a materialized view instead of computing it on each read, you're choosing write-time fan-out over read-time merge. The Twitter problem is the same shape as your "should I store this aggregate or compute it live?" decision.
  • That one slow endpoint that's "fine on average." If your p99 is 10× your p50, you have a tail problem — probably a slow query path, a cold cache, lock contention, or GC pauses. The average will never reveal it; the percentile will.
  • Fan-out in microservices. Your API gateway calls 8 services to render one page and waits for all of them. That's tail-latency amplification: each service being "usually fast" is not enough. This is why you add timeouts, fallbacks, and parallelism limits.
  • Load testing & capacity planning. "Can we handle Black Friday?" is unanswerable until you've defined the load parameter (peak RPS? concurrent carts? cache hit rate?) and the performance target (p99 < 500 ms). Today gives you the vocabulary to make that conversation precise.
💡 Ohhh, THAT'S why

...your SRE keeps saying "average latency is meaningless" and rewrites your alert to fire on p99. And why the senior engineer in design review always asks "what's your read:write ratio?" before suggesting an architecture. They're not being pedantic — they know that the load parameter determines the right design, and the percentile determines whether you're actually meeting your users' needs.

🎯 System Design Interview Playbook

When this comes up

This is the back-of-the-envelope / requirements phase of every design interview, and the explicit topic of "Design Twitter," "Design a news feed," "Design Instagram." The fan-out question is one of the most-asked design problems at FAANG. Percentiles show up the moment you discuss SLAs or "how do you know it's fast enough?"

What to say (the checklist)

  • Define the load parameter first: "Before I design, let me estimate load — read:write ratio, peak RPS, and crucially the distribution of followers, because that skew drives the architecture."
  • State the fan-out trade-off explicitly: "I can compute the feed at read time (cheap writes, expensive reads) or fan out on write (cheap reads, expensive writes). Since reads dominate ~65:1, I'll fan out on write."
  • Pre-empt the celebrity problem: "Pure fan-out breaks for celebrities — one tweet = millions of writes. So I'll go hybrid: fan out for normal users, merge celebrity tweets at read time."
  • Use percentiles for targets: "I'll target p99 < 200 ms, not average — the average hides the tail, and the tail is where we lose engaged users."
  • Name tail amplification: "Because the timeline fans out to many shards, I need to watch tail-latency amplification and consider hedged requests."

The trade-off to articulate

"Read-time vs write-time cost is a fundamental trade-off, and the right answer depends on the read:write ratio and the skew of the workload. There's no universally scalable design — only one matched to a specific load profile. That's why I estimate the load parameters before committing to an architecture."

⚠️ Common candidate mistake

Jumping straight to boxes-and-arrows without ever estimating load, then proposing pure fan-out-on-write and getting blindsided when the interviewer says "okay, now Justin Bieber tweets." The other classic miss: quoting average latency as your performance target. Both signal you haven't internalized that load shape and the tail drive everything.

⭐ Power move

Volunteer the hybrid before being prompted: "Pure fan-out is elegant until you hit the follower-count power law, so I'd special-case high-follower accounts with read-time merge." Then tie performance back to business: "I'd hold p99.9, not the mean — Amazon does this because the slowest requests belong to the highest-value customers." Connecting an architectural choice to a load parameter and a revenue consequence is a staff-level signal.

🃏 Common Misconceptions (tap a card to flip)

✗ Myth

"Our average response time is 200 ms, so the system is fast."

tap to reveal truth →
✓ Truth

The mean is dragged by outliers and describes nobody. You can have a 200 ms average while your p99 is 2 seconds — meaning 1% of requests (often your best customers) have a terrible experience. Always report and target percentiles.

✗ Myth

"Scalability is a property — a system either is or isn't scalable."

tap to reveal truth →
✓ Truth

"Is X scalable?" is meaningless. Scalability is relative to a specific load parameter: scalable along which axis — more users? more writes? bigger follower counts? A design that scales reads may collapse on writes. Always ask "scale what?"

✗ Myth

"Latency and response time are the same thing."

tap to reveal truth →
✓ Truth

Response time is the full client-observed time = service time + network + queueing. Latency is the time a request spends waiting to be handled. Queueing delay often dominates the tail: a few slow requests block everything behind them (head-of-line blocking).

✗ Myth

"If each backend is 99% fast, the overall request is 99% fast too."

tap to reveal truth →
✓ Truth

If a request waits on N backends, P(at least one slow) = 1 − 0.99^N. At N=100 that's 63%. Tail-latency amplification means the more you fan out, the more often the slowest call dominates — backend p99 becomes user-facing p50.

🧩 Interactive Zone

🤔 Pause & Think #1 — Twitter reads 65× more than it writes. Why didn't they just keep the simple read-time merge and throw more database replicas at it?

It seems like "add replicas" should solve a read-heavy problem. Why doesn't it here?

Answer: Read replicas help when reads are cheap and independent — you spread identical lookups across more machines. But Twitter's read-time merge isn't a cheap lookup; each home-timeline read is an expensive computation: fetch the tweets of everyone you follow (could be thousands of accounts), merge them, and sort by time. At 300k reads/sec, that's 300k expensive merges per second. Replicas multiply your read capacity, but each replica still pays the full merge cost per request, and they all hammer the same underlying tweet data. The smarter move is to change the shape of the work: do the expensive merge once at write time (fan-out) and turn every read into a trivial "read my prebuilt cache." You're trading a small number of expensive reads-many for a precomputed write — exactly because the read:write ratio makes that trade pay off. This is the same instinct as a materialized view: precompute the expensive part when data changes, so reads are cheap.

🤔 Pause & Think #2 — Why does Amazon care about p99.9 (1 in 1,000 requests) when it costs real engineering effort for such a tiny slice?

Optimizing the 99.9th percentile is hard and expensive. Why not stop at p95?

Answer: Two reasons, one technical and one commercial. Technically, the slowest requests are usually not random — they correlate with users who have the most data (the most orders, the largest carts, the longest history), because more data means more work per request. Commercially, those high-data users are frequently your most valuable customers — the ones who buy the most. So the tail isn't a random unlucky 0.1%; it's disproportionately your best customers having the worst experience. Add that Amazon measured ~1% sales drop per 100 ms of added latency, and improving p99.9 has a direct revenue case. Beyond p99.9 there are diminishing returns (a single request might be slow due to a rare unavoidable event), which is why they draw the line there rather than chasing p99.99.

🤔 Pause & Think #3 — A request sits in a queue for 400 ms, then is served in 10 ms. What's the response time, and what does this teach about load?

Where did the time go, and what's the lesson for a busy server?

Answer: The response time is ~410 ms — service time (10 ms) plus queueing delay (400 ms). The lesson: at high load, queueing delay dominates response time, and it does so non-linearly. A server can only process a few requests in parallel; once a few slow requests are in flight, everything behind them waits (head-of-line blocking), so even fast requests get slow response times. This is why tail latency rises sharply as you approach capacity, and why a single slow request poisons the experience of many fast ones. Practical consequence: when you measure response time, measure it client-side (the queueing happens before your server-side timer even starts), and beware coordinated omission — load generators that pause when the server is slow systematically under-measure exactly the slow requests you care about.

⚡ Quick Check #1

A user-facing request fans out to 50 backend calls and waits for all of them. Each backend is below its p99 latency 99% of the time. Roughly what fraction of user requests will be "slow" (hit at least one backend's tail)?

P(at least one slow) = 1 − 0.9950 ≈ 1 − 0.605 = ~39.5%. So nearly 2 in 5 user requests are slow, even though every single backend is "99% fast." That's tail-latency amplification: the more calls you fan out to and wait on, the more the slowest one dominates. Mitigations: reduce fan-out, parallelize with timeouts, use hedged/tied requests, or relax the "wait for all" requirement (return partial results).

⚡ Quick Check #2

You're designing Instagram's feed. Reads are ~100× writes, but a few celebrity accounts have 400M followers. Pure fan-out-on-write or pure read-time merge — or something else? Justify with the load parameter.

Hybrid. The 100:1 read:write ratio strongly favors fan-out-on-write for the vast majority of users — precompute the feed so the hot read path is a cheap cache lookup. But the load parameter that breaks pure fan-out is the follower-count distribution: a 400M-follower celebrity posting would trigger 400M cache writes per post, which is infeasible at posting cadence. So special-case high-follower accounts: do not fan their posts out; instead fetch their recent posts at read time and merge them into each viewer's precomputed feed. You pay a tiny read-time merge for the handful of celebs a user follows, while keeping cheap reads for everyone else. This is exactly Twitter's real-world solution.

✅ Key Takeaways

"Is it scalable?" is the wrong question. Scalability is always relative to a load parameter: scale along which axis? Pick the parameter that captures pressure on your system (RPS, read:write ratio, follower distribution, cache hit rate).
Averages lie; percentiles tell the truth. Report p50/p95/p99/p99.9, never the mean. The tail (p99+) is where you lose your most valuable users — Amazon optimizes p99.9 for exactly this reason.
Fan-out is a read-time vs write-time choice. Read-heavy workloads favor precomputing on write (fan-out); but the follower power-law forces a hybrid — fan out for normal users, merge celebrity content at read time.
Response time ≠ latency. Response time is the full client-observed duration including queueing; latency is waiting time. At high load, queueing delay and head-of-line blocking dominate the tail.
Tail-latency amplification is brutal. Fan out to N backends and wait for all, and P(slow) = 1 − 0.99^N — 100 calls means slow ~63% of the time. Fanning out multiplies your tail.
