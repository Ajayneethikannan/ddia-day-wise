Scalability — Describing Load & Performance

Chapter 1 · Reliable, Scalable, Maintainable Applications · Describing Load & Performance
Course progress4 / 109 (3.7%)

🔥 The Problem

Your side project blew up on Hacker News. On Monday it served 200 users a day off a single box, and everything felt instant. On Friday, after the front-page hit, it's serving 2 million requests an hour — and it's on fire. Pages that returned in 40 ms now hang for 8 seconds. The database CPU is pinned at 100%. Some users still get snappy responses; others stare at spinners and rage-tweet at you.

Here's the trap that eats junior engineers alive: your manager asks "Can our system scale?" and it feels like a yes/no question. It isn't. "Can it scale?" is a meaningless question until you answer three others:

  • Scale along which axis? More users? More data? More writes? A viral single tweet? Each stresses a totally different part of your system.
  • Scale is not a number. "It handles 10,000 requests/sec" tells you almost nothing — 10k tiny cache hits is trivial; 10k analytical joins will melt any single server.
  • Fast for whom? "Average response time is 100 ms" can hide the fact that your most valuable customers are waiting 3 seconds — and averages will actively lie to you about it.

Before you can make a system scalable, you have to describe load and performance in numbers precise enough to reason about. That vocabulary — load parameters and latency percentiles — is today's lesson. Get it wrong and you'll optimize the thing nobody was waiting on.

🧩 What Is It?

Scalability is not a label you slap on a system ("this system is scalable" ✓/✗). In DDIA's framing it's a question:

Definition

Scalability = a system's ability to cope with increased load. It is used to discuss the question: "If the system grows in a particular way, what are our options for coping with that growth?"

To even ask that question sensibly, you need two measuring sticks:

1. Describing load — load parameters

You pick a few numbers that best capture what stresses your system. These are load parameters. The right choice depends entirely on your architecture:

  • Requests per second to a web server (throughput)
  • Ratio of reads to writes in a database
  • Number of simultaneously active users
  • Hit rate on a cache
  • ...or, for Twitter, the distribution of followers per user — which turns out to matter far more than raw request counts (we'll see why).

The art is choosing the parameter that actually predicts pain. Everything else is noise.

2. Describing performance

Once you can describe load, you ask: when load goes up, what happens to performance? There are two flavours:

Throughput

Records processed per second, or total time to run a job over a dataset. This is the metric for batch systems (think a nightly Hadoop job).

Response time

The time between a client sending a request and receiving the response — what the user actually feels. This is the metric for online systems. It's the one we'll obsess over today.

Sharp distinction most people blur

Response time is what the client sees: service time plus network delays plus queueing delays. Latency is the duration a request is waiting to be handled — latent, awaiting service. They are not synonyms, and the queueing part is exactly where things go bad under load.

The single most important idea in this whole lesson: response time is not a single number — it's a distribution. The same endpoint, hit a thousand times, returns a thousand slightly different times. Report it as an average and you've thrown away the story. Report it with percentiles and the truth comes out.

🎭 The Analogy

Analogy 1 — The coffee shop (load & percentiles)

Picture a café. Load parameters are the things that describe how busy it is: customers per minute, ratio of "just a black coffee" to "oat-milk half-caf 6-pump caramel macchiato," how many are ordering at once. Notice that customers per minute alone doesn't tell you if the barista is drowning — 30 black coffees is easy; 30 complicated orders is chaos. That's why you pick the load parameter that captures real stress.

Response time is how long you personally wait from ordering to drink-in-hand. Now — the manager brags "average wait is 3 minutes." But you came in behind a catering order for 40 lattes and waited 20 minutes. The average hid you completely. The honest metric is: "95% of customers wait under 4 minutes; 99% wait under 9; but 1-in-1000 waits 25 minutes." Those tail cases — p99, p999 — are the customers who leave one-star reviews. And ironically they're often your biggest orders (most valuable customers).

Analogy 2 — The post office (fan-out / load parameter that matters)

You mail one letter (post a tweet). Two ways the post office can work:

  • Deliver-on-read: nobody delivers anything. Every recipient, whenever they want mail, walks to the sorting office and asks "any letters for me from anyone I know?" Cheap to send (you just drop your letter in a bin), brutal to receive (huge lookup every time).
  • Deliver-on-write: the moment you post, the office copies your letter into the mailbox of every one of your followers. Expensive to send (one letter becomes thousands of copies), instant to receive (you just open your own mailbox).

The parameter that decides which is smarter isn't "letters per day" — it's how many followers each sender has. A normal person with 100 followers? Deliver-on-write is fine. A celebrity with 30 million followers? One tweet spawns 30 million copies — the sorting office collapses. That skew is the load parameter, and it's why real systems go hybrid.

⚙️ How It Actually Works

DDIA makes all this concrete with the Twitter timeline problem (numbers from ~2012). Two operations:

  • Post tweet — average 4.6k req/sec, peak 12k/sec. A write.
  • Home timeline — 300k req/sec. A read. Users pull the recent tweets of everyone they follow.

Reads outnumber writes by roughly 65×. That imbalance is the whole ballgame. Let's watch the two designs.

Fan-out on write, in motion

The winning design does the expensive work at write time (rare) so reads (constant) are trivial. When Alice tweets, the system fans out a copy into a precomputed timeline cache for every follower:

Amber arrows = the write fanning out. One tweet becomes N inserts (one per follower). Now Bob, Carol, and Dave each read their own precomputed cache — no joins, no scanning who-they-follow. The 300k reads/sec become almost free.

Why the tail is disproportionately valuable

At Amazon, response-time requirements are described in p999 — even though it affects only 1 in 1000 requests — because the customers with the slowest requests often have the most data on their accounts: they've made the most purchases. They're the most valuable customers, and they're the ones you're keeping waiting.

Tail-latency amplification: if a single user request fans out to many backend calls in parallel, the end-to-end response waits for the slowest of them. Even if only 1% of backend calls are slow, a request touching 100 backends will hit a slow one ~63% of the time. The more you parallelize, the more the tail dominates.

⚖️ The Trade-offs

Fan-out on write vs. fan-out on read is the canonical scalability trade-off: move work to write-time or read-time. There's no universal winner — it depends on your load parameters. Play with them:

🟢 Fan-out on read

Comparison table

DimensionFan-out on writeFan-out on read
Work at post timeHigh — insert into every follower's cacheLow — one insert
Work at read timeVery low — read your own timelineHigh — query & merge everyone you follow
StorageHigh — tweets duplicated across cachesLow — stored once
Best whenReads ≫ writes, modest fan-outWrites ≫ reads, or extreme fan-out (celebrities)
Failure modeCelebrity with 30M followers → write stormUser following thousands → slow, heavy reads
The real answer: hybrid

Twitter's production system does fan-out on write for normal users (fast reads for the 99% case) but fan-out on read for celebrities — their tweets are fetched and merged into your timeline at read time, avoiding the write storm. The distribution of followers per user is the load parameter that drove this design. That's the lesson: pick the parameter that predicts pain, then design around it.

🌍 In the Wild

  • Twitter / X — the textbook hybrid fan-out described above. Precomputed home timelines for regular users; on-the-fly merge for high-follower accounts.
  • Amazon — internal SLOs stated as p999 response time, and famously found that every 100 ms of added latency measurably cut sales. Tail latency is treated as revenue, not vanity.
  • Google & the "tail at scale" — Dean & Barroso's paper formalized tail-latency amplification and mitigations like hedged requests (fire a duplicate to a second replica if the first is slow) and tied requests. Directly from the fan-out math above.
  • Netflix / big SRE shops — dashboards show p50/p95/p99/p999, never averages. Alerts and error budgets are defined on percentiles (e.g., "p99 latency < 200 ms for 99.9% of the month").
  • Prometheus / Datadog / HdrHistogram — the tooling reflects the theory: they store histograms, not just means, precisely so you can compute percentiles after the fact. HdrHistogram exists to record tails accurately and cheaply.

🔭 The SDE 2 Lens

Where this hits your day job
  • Your Grafana "avg latency" panel is lying to you. That flat 80 ms average line looks healthy while your p99 is at 2s and a slice of users is suffering. Add p95/p99/p999 panels — it's usually a two-line change in your query (histogram_quantile). This single habit separates SDE 2s from seniors.
  • That "denormalize for reads" ticket is fan-out on write. Every time you precompute a materialized view, a counter cache, or a denormalized feed table so reads are cheap, you're making the exact trade DDIA describes — paying at write time to make the frequent reads fast.
  • The mysterious p99 spike after you added a microservice. You split one call into five parallel service calls and tail latency got worse even though each service looks fast. That's tail-latency amplification — now you know the name and the fix (fewer hops, hedged requests, timeouts + fallbacks).
  • Load testing that "passes." A k6/Gatling run reporting mean throughput is nearly useless. The real question is: at target RPS, what's the p99? Report the distribution or the test proved nothing.
  • Picking the load parameter in design docs. When you write "expected 5k RPS," a reviewer will (rightly) ask "of what shape?" Reads vs writes, payload size, fan-out — naming the parameter that actually stresses the system is a senior move.

🎯 System Design Interview Playbook

When this comes up

Constantly — Design Twitter/Instagram feed, Design a news feed, Design a notification system, and any "how do you scale this?" follow-up. The load/percentile vocabulary is baseline expectation.

What to say

  • "Let me first define the load parameters — read:write ratio, and crucially the fan-out distribution (followers per user), because that's what actually stresses a feed system."
  • "Reads dominate ~65:1, so I'll fan out on write — precompute each user's timeline — to keep the hot path (reads) cheap."
  • "For high-follower accounts I'll switch to fan-out on read to avoid write storms — a hybrid."
  • "I'll define SLOs in percentiles — e.g. p99 < 200 ms — not averages, because averages hide the tail."
  • "I'll watch for tail-latency amplification across service hops and mitigate with timeouts, fallbacks, and hedged requests."
The trade-off to articulate

"We move work between write-time and read-time. Fan-out on write pays a write cost and storage cost to make reads O(1); fan-out on read keeps writes cheap but makes reads expensive. The choice is dictated by the read:write ratio and the fan-out skew."

⚠️ Common candidate mistake

Quoting a single "average latency" or "handles X requests/sec" as if it settles scalability. It doesn't — the interviewer wants the distribution and the shape of the load. Also: forgetting the celebrity/hot-key case entirely and proposing pure fan-out on write.

💥 Power move

Proactively name the hybrid and justify it with the follower-distribution load parameter, then add: "and I'd track p999 specifically, because our heaviest users — most followers, most data — live in the tail and they're the most valuable to retain." That connects load, performance, and business value in one breath.

🃏 Common Misconceptions (tap a card to flip)

Myth

"Average response time tells me how fast my system is."

tap to reveal truth →
Truth

The mean is skewed by outliers and hides the tail. A few 10s requests can drag the average up while most users are fine — or a healthy average can hide a suffering p99. Use the median (p50) for "typical," and p95/p99/p999 for the tail.

Myth

"Scalability is a property — a system either 'scales' or it doesn't."

tap to reveal truth →
Truth

It's not a one-dimensional label. A system scales along a specific axis for a specific load parameter. It might scale beautifully with more users but fall over on a single hot key. Always ask: scale in which direction?

Myth

"Latency and response time are the same thing."

tap to reveal truth →
Truth

Response time = what the client sees = service time + network + queueing. Latency = the time a request spends waiting to be handled. Under load, queueing delay dominates — which is why response time degrades non-linearly as you approach saturation.

Myth

"To get overall p95, just average the p95 of each server."

tap to reveal truth →
Truth

You cannot average percentiles — it's mathematically meaningless. To aggregate, keep histograms and combine those (e.g. HdrHistogram, or forward-decay t-digests), then compute the percentile from the merged distribution.

🧠 Interactive Zone

🤔 Pause & Think — Twitter's average is ~4.6k tweets/sec. Why did they still redesign their entire architecture around a different number?

Because tweets-per-second is the wrong load parameter. The thing that actually determined the system's fate was the distribution of followers per user — specifically the fan-out multiplier at write time.

Posting a tweet is cheap on its own. But with fan-out on write, one post becomes one write per follower. Averaged over all users that's manageable, but the distribution is heavily skewed: a handful of celebrities have tens of millions of followers. A single tweet from them triggers tens of millions of cache writes. The average hid the outliers that broke the system — so Twitter went hybrid. Lesson: the load parameter that matters is the one whose tail hurts you, not the one that's easy to measure.

🤔 Pause & Think — Your service calls 10 backends in parallel, each with a p99 of 10 ms. Is your end-to-end p99 also ~10 ms?

No — it's much worse. Your response waits for the slowest of the 10 calls. If each call independently has a 1% chance of exceeding 10 ms, the chance that at least one of 10 exceeds it is 1 − 0.99¹⁰ ≈ 9.6%. So roughly 1 in 10 of your end-to-end requests hits a slow backend — your effective p90, not p99, is now in "slow" territory.

This is tail-latency amplification. The more you fan out, the more the tail dominates. Mitigations: reduce the number of hops, set aggressive timeouts with fallbacks, and use hedged requests (send a duplicate to another replica once the first is slow, take whichever returns first).

⚡ Quick Check
A read-heavy feed system with mostly-normal users (a few hundred followers each). Fan-out on write or fan-out on read — and why?
Fan-out on write. Reads vastly outnumber writes, so you want reads to be O(1) — read your own precomputed timeline. Fan-out is modest (hundreds), so the write cost is acceptable. You'd only switch to fan-out on read for the celebrity outliers (the hybrid), keeping their write storms off the hot path.
⚡ Quick Check
Team A reports "p99 = 120 ms" for the whole fleet by averaging the p99 of all 20 servers. What's wrong?
You can't average percentiles. The mean of per-server p99s is not the fleet p99 — a server handling more (or slower) traffic contributes disproportionately to the true tail. Correct approach: export a histogram from each server, merge the histograms, then read the p99 off the combined distribution. Tools like HdrHistogram / Prometheus histograms exist exactly for this.

🔑 Key Takeaways

Scalability is a question, not a label. "Can it scale?" is meaningless until you say along which axis and for which load parameter.
Pick the load parameter that predicts pain. For Twitter it wasn't tweets/sec — it was the skewed distribution of followers per user (the fan-out).
Response time is a distribution. Report percentiles — p50 for typical, p95/p99/p999 for the tail. Never trust an average alone.
The tail is where value hides. Slowest requests often come from your biggest, most valuable users — which is why Amazon targets p999.
Scaling = moving work in time and space. Fan-out on write vs read trades write-cost for read-cost; the hybrid picks per-user. And you can't average percentiles.
