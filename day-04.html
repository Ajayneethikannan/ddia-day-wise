Scalability — When Averages Lie

Chapter 1 · Reliable, Scalable, Maintainable Applications · Describing Load and Performance
Course progress4 / 109 (3.7%)

📉 The Problem: The Dashboard Is Green and the Customer Is Furious

It's 2 PM. Your monitoring dashboard is a wall of calm green. Average response time: 98 ms. CPU is fine, error rate is near zero, the on-call channel is quiet. By every number you watch, the system is healthy.

Then your biggest enterprise customer escalates to the CEO: the app is "unusable — pages take forever, it times out constantly." Their account manager is panicking. You pull up their traffic and... the average for their requests is also low-ish. So who's lying — the customer, or the graph?

Neither. The customer is your heaviest user: they have the most data, the longest follower lists, the biggest shopping carts. Their requests are precisely the ones that land in the slow 1% — the requests that take 3 seconds, not 98 ms. And the average buries them. One 3-second request hidden among ninety-nine 50 ms requests barely moves the mean. The average isn't wrong; it's answering the wrong question.

💡 Why this should change how you think

Reliability (Days 1–3) asks "is it up?" Today we ask the harder question: "can it grow, and how do we even measure whether it's good under load?" The first trap is thinking scalability is a single label — "is it scalable?" — when it's actually a precise conversation about which load grows and which number you measure. Get the measurement wrong and you'll optimize the thing your users don't feel while ignoring the thing that's making them leave.

📖 What Is It? Load, Performance, and the Honest Numbers

Kleppmann is deliberate here: scalability is not a one-dimensional label you stamp on a system. "Is X scalable?" is a meaningless question. The right framing is a question with two halves:

💡 The two-part definition

Scalability = a system's ability to cope with increased load. To reason about it you must answer: (1) "If load grows in this specific way, what are our options for coping?" and (2) "How do we add computing resources to handle the extra load?" Both halves require you to first describe load and performance with numbers, not adjectives.

Step 1 — Describe load with load parameters

Load is described by a handful of numbers Kleppmann calls load parameters. Which ones matter depends entirely on your architecture:

  • Requests per second to a web server (throughput of a stateless tier)
  • Ratio of reads to writes in a database (a 100:1 read-heavy system is a totally different beast from a write-heavy one)
  • Simultaneously active users in a chat room
  • Hit rate on a cache
  • ...or something subtler and architecture-specific — like, for Twitter, the distribution of followers per user. Hold that thought; it's the whole ballgame.

The senior move is to pick the parameter that actually stresses your system. Choose wrong and you'll scale the wrong dimension.

Step 2 — Describe performance, and pick the right metric

Once load is described, ask: what happens to performance as load rises? Two different questions, two different metrics:

MetricWhat it measuresWho cares
ThroughputRecords processed per second / total job timeBatch systems (Hadoop, ETL) — "how much work per hour?"
Response timeTime between a client sending a request and receiving the response — what the user actually feelsOnline systems — "how long do I wait?"
💡 Latency ≠ response time

People use these interchangeably; Kleppmann doesn't. Response time is what the client sees: service time plus network delays plus queueing delays. Latency is the duration a request is waiting to be handled — latent, awaiting service. Response time is the whole round trip; latency is the waiting inside it. Conflating them hides queueing — which is exactly where tail pain lives.

Step 3 — Stop using the average

Response time isn't a single number — it's a distribution. Send the identical request a thousand times and you'll get a thousand slightly different answers: most fast, a few slow because of a TCP retransmit, a garbage-collection pause, a context switch, a page fault. So reporting the mean (average) is nearly useless — it tells you nothing about how many users had a bad experience. Use percentiles instead:

  • p50 (median): sort all response times; the middle value. "Half of requests are faster than this." Your typical experience.
  • p95 / p99 / p99.9: the high percentiles, called tail latencies. p99 = "99% of requests are faster than this; the slowest 1% are worse." These tell you how bad your worst experiences are.

Why do tails matter so much? Because the customers with the slowest requests are often your most valuable ones — they have the most data because they've used you the most (Amazon observed exactly this). And a single slow page can cost you the sale. But chasing percentiles gets expensive fast: reducing p99.9 means fighting random, uncontrollable events, and the cost rises steeply for diminishing relevance — Amazon optimizes p99.9 but not p99.99 because the payoff no longer justifies it.

🧠 The Analogy: The Commute and the Coffee Shop

Percentiles → your daily commute

Your commute "usually takes 30 minutes" (that's your p50). But you don't leave the house 30 minutes before an important meeting — because one day in twenty, an accident on the highway makes it 75 minutes (your p95). For a flight you can't miss, you budget for the once-a-year blizzard-plus-accident nightmare: 2 hours (p99.9). You plan your life around tail latency, not the average, because the average never made you miss a flight — the tail did.

Mapping: the typical 30-min drive = p50; the accident-day 75 min = p95; the once-a-year disaster = p99.9. Notice you'd never describe your commute as "averaging 34 minutes" when deciding when to leave — the spread and the worst case are what you actually act on. Same with response times.

Load parameters → a coffee shop's real bottleneck

A café owner asks "can we handle more customers?" The naive answer is "hire more baristas." But it depends entirely on how the load grows. If 200 customers each order a simple black coffee, the register is the bottleneck. If 50 customers each order an 8-ingredient custom Frappuccino, the espresso machine is. Same headcount of customers, completely different scaling problem — and "hire baristas" only helps one of them.

Mapping: "number of customers" is the obvious load parameter, but the one that actually stresses the shop is order complexity × mix. For Twitter, the obvious parameter is "tweets per second," but the one that actually breaks things is the distribution of followers — a tweet from someone with 30 million followers is a Frappuccino; a tweet from you is a black coffee.

💡 The aha

Both analogies kill the same lazy habit: collapsing a rich, skewed reality into one comforting number. The average commute and the average customer hide the cases that actually hurt. Scalability work starts by finding the right load parameter and the right percentile — only then can you sensibly talk about coping.

⚙️ How It Actually Works

Kleppmann's centerpiece example is Twitter's home timeline — a perfect lesson in how the right load parameter reshapes your entire architecture. Two operations dominate:

  • Post tweet: a user publishes a message. Average ~4.6k writes/sec; peaks over 12k/sec.
  • Home timeline: a user loads the feed of tweets from everyone they follow. ~300k reads/sec.

Reads outnumber writes ~65:1. That read/write ratio is the load parameter that decides everything.

The two designs

Approach 1 · Fan-out on READApproach 2 · Fan-out on WRITE
Post tweetCheap: one insert into a global tweets tableExpensive: insert into the home-timeline cache of every follower
Read timelineExpensive: look up everyone you follow, fetch their recent tweets, merge-sort by time — on every loadCheap: your timeline is precomputed; just read your cache
Who paysThe 300k/sec readersThe 4.6k/sec writers

Twitter started with Approach 1 and the read query crushed their databases — because reads dominate writes 65:1, you want reads cheap. So they flipped to Approach 2: do the expensive merge work once at write time and serve reads from a precomputed list. The load parameter (read/write ratio) chose the architecture for them.

Animated architecture: tail-latency amplification across backends

Here's the trap that makes tail latency worse at scale. A single user request often fans out to many backend services in parallel, and the user waits for the slowest one. Watch four fast backends and one slow one — the gateway can't respond until the straggler returns.

Four backends answer in ~10 ms, but Svc C hit a garbage-collection pause and took 240 ms. The user's response time is 240 ms — set entirely by the straggler. The math is brutal: if each backend is slow (>p99) just 1% of the time, and a request touches 100 of them, then ~63% of end-user requests hit at least one slow backend (1 − 0.99¹⁰⁰). This is tail-latency amplification: the more you fan out, the more the rare slow case becomes the common case.

Step-through: how Twitter serves a timeline (fan-out on write)

Step 1 of 5 · A user posts a tweet
A user hits "Tweet." The write itself is tiny — one short message. The naive instinct is "just store it." But under fan-out on write, posting triggers a cascade of work, because we're going to pay the cost now so that 300k/sec readers don't have to.

The cost shape of fan-out on write

Posting a tweet = write to EACH follower's timeline cache You (200 followers) → 200 cache writes ✅ trivial A mid creator (50k) → 50,000 cache writes 😬 noticeable A celebrity (30M) → 30,000,000 writes 💥 one tweet = a storm Average is ~75 writes/tweet... but the AVERAGE LIES again: the follower distribution is wildly skewed (a power law). The load parameter that actually breaks you isn't tweets/sec — it's the FOLLOWER COUNT of whoever just tweeted. Twitter's fix: HYBRID. • Normal users → fan-out on write (precompute, reads are cheap) • Celebrities → fan-out on read (their tweets merged in at read time)

⚖️ The Trade-offs

The fan-out decision is a textbook read-vs-write cost trade-off, and the right answer depends entirely on your load parameters. Move the work to write time and reads get cheap but writes get expensive (and worse, amplified by follower count). Leave it at read time and writes are trivial but every read does a costly merge. There's no universally right answer — there's only "right for this read/write ratio and this follower distribution."

🏢 In the Wild

Twitter — the canonical fan-out hybrid

Twitter's timeline is the example DDIA uses, and it's real: they ran fan-out-on-read, got crushed by the 300k/sec read load, switched to fan-out-on-write, then discovered the celebrity problem (a Lady Gaga tweet meant tens of millions of cache writes, delaying the tweet's appearance). The production system is a hybrid — most users get precomputed timelines, a small set of high-follower accounts are fetched and merged at read time. The lesson: the load parameter that mattered wasn't tweets/sec, it was the follower-count distribution.

Amazon — the p99.9 obsession and "averages are banned"

Amazon famously targets p99.9 response times and pays attention to them because the customers with the slowest requests are the ones with the most data in their accounts — i.e., the most valuable customers. They also found a 100 ms increase in latency measurably reduced sales. Internally, SLOs are written in percentiles, not averages, precisely because the average hides the customers who are about to churn.

Discord — read fan-out and the "huge server" tail

Discord delivers messages to channels with millions of members. Like Twitter, the per-message fan-out cost depends on a skewed distribution (channel size), and their engineering blog is full of percentile-latency war stories. The very large servers are the tail-latency drivers — the same skew problem in a different costume.

Google / Jeff Dean — "The Tail at Scale"

The classic paper (Dean & Barroso) formalizes tail-latency amplification: when one request fans out to thousands of leaf servers (e.g., a search query), the overall latency is governed by the slowest leaf, so rare slowness becomes near-certain. Their mitigations — hedged requests (send a duplicate to a second replica after a short delay, take whichever returns first) and request-tied cancellation — are now standard tail-latency tools.

Percentiles in your monitoring stack (Prometheus, Datadog)

Every modern observability tool reports p50/p90/p95/p99 latency, and they all warn you not to average percentiles across machines (you can't — the math doesn't work; you must aggregate the underlying histograms). Prometheus histograms and histogram_quantile() exist precisely because the average is the wrong abstraction for response time.

🔍 The SDE 2 Lens

Where this shows up in your day job

You ship features against latency budgets and stare at Grafana every week. Today reframes those numbers so you stop being fooled by them.

  • That latency dashboard with the average line. Switch your alerts to p95/p99. The average is the most reassuring and least useful number on the screen — it goes green while your tail is on fire. The "customer says it's slow but metrics are fine" ticket is almost always a tail-latency story.
  • The "denormalize for read performance" instinct in your data model. That's literally fan-out on write — you precompute/materialize at write time so reads are cheap. Materialized views, denormalized counters, a precomputed feed table: same trade-off, different name. You're choosing who pays.
  • Your service that calls five other services to render one page. That's tail-latency amplification. Your p99 is roughly the combination of all five backends' tails — one slow dependency poisons your whole endpoint. This is why timeouts, fallbacks, and hedged requests exist.
  • The "just add more servers" reflex in standups. Adding capacity helps a throughput bottleneck. It does nothing for a tail-latency problem caused by a GC pause or a hot key — and can make it worse by adding more fan-out targets. First identify the load parameter and the metric; then choose the lever.
  • That one "whale" customer whose account is 100× bigger than everyone else's. They're your celebrity. The query that's fine for normal accounts melts down for theirs. The fix is the same shape as Twitter's hybrid: special-case the heavy entity (pagination, async precompute, a different code path).
💡 Ohhh, THAT'S why

...your SRE keeps saying "don't average the percentiles" and "what's your p99, not your mean?" And why the load test that passed at "1000 req/s average" still fell over in prod — because real traffic is bursty and skewed, and the queue that forms during a burst is what generates the tail. Average throughput is a planning number; the percentile under burst is the user-experience number.

🎯 System Design Interview Playbook

When this comes up

Constantly. "Design Twitter / a news feed / Instagram" is the fan-out question. "Design a metrics/monitoring system" and "how do you measure if this is fast enough?" are percentile questions. And almost every design has a "now it's 10× the traffic — what breaks?" follow-up, which is the scalability question in disguise.

What to say

  • Quantify load first: "Before I design, what's the read/write ratio, the QPS, and the distribution — e.g., follower counts? Those load parameters drive the architecture." This single sentence signals seniority.
  • Reach for fan-out on write for read-heavy feeds: "Reads dominate ~65:1, so I'd precompute timelines on write — pay once at write time, serve reads from a materialized per-user list."
  • Pre-empt the celebrity problem: "Pure write-fanout explodes for high-follower accounts, so I'd go hybrid — fan-out-on-write for normal users, fan-out-on-read merged in for celebrities."
  • Measure in percentiles: "I'd set SLOs on p99/p99.9 response time, not averages, and watch tail-latency amplification across service hops."
  • Name a tail mitigation: "Timeouts + hedged requests for fan-out reads, so one slow replica doesn't set the whole request's latency."

The trade-off to articulate

"Fan-out is a read-vs-write cost trade-off. I push work to write time when reads dominate and the fan-out factor is bounded; I keep it at read time when writes must stay cheap or the fan-out is huge/variable. The hybrid lets me choose per-entity instead of globally."

⚠️ Common candidate mistake

Saying a design is "scalable" without naming what load grows or which metric you'd watch. The second-biggest mistake: optimizing the average. If you say "we'll keep average latency under 100 ms," a sharp interviewer will ask about the tail — and "the most valuable customers live in the tail" is the answer that separates SDE-2 from senior.

⭐ Power move

Drop tail-latency amplification with the math: "If each backend is slow 1% of the time and I touch 100 of them, ~63% of requests hit a straggler — so I'll bound fan-out, add timeouts, and use hedged requests." Then connect it to the data model: "fan-out-on-write is just denormalization — a materialized view I maintain on write." Linking the feed design, the latency math, and the storage trade-off in one breath is a staff-level signal.

🤔 Pause & Think #1 — Why is "p99.9" the kind of number that gets harder and more expensive to improve, while "average" looks deceptively easy to improve?

Both are just statistics over the same requests. Why is one cheap to move and the other a money pit?

Answer: The average is dominated by the common case, so any broad improvement (a faster query, a bigger cache) shifts it visibly — and you can game it: a few faster fast-requests pull the mean down even if the slow ones get worse. The p99.9, by contrast, is determined by rare, often uncontrollable events: a garbage-collection pause, a TCP retransmit, a cold cache, a noisy neighbor on the box, a context switch at the wrong moment. To improve p99.9 you must attack the variance, not the mean — which means hunting down sporadic, hard-to-reproduce stalls. Each increment costs more for a smaller slice of traffic, which is exactly why Amazon optimizes p99.9 but decided p99.99 wasn't worth it: beyond some point the events are so random and rare that the engineering cost outweighs the benefit. The average lies cheaply; the tail tells the truth expensively.

🤔 Pause & Think #2 — Twitter has 65× more reads than writes. Argue from that single number why fan-out-on-write is the right default — and then why it still can't be the whole answer.

Reason it out from the load parameter alone.

Answer: If reads outnumber writes 65:1, then any work you can move from the read path to the write path is done 65× less often. Fan-out-on-write does exactly that: it pays the expensive "gather everyone you follow and merge by time" cost once, at write time, and turns every one of the 300k/sec reads into a trivial O(1) lookup of a precomputed list. Multiplying the rare operation and dividing the common one is almost always the right trade when the ratio is that lopsided. But the write cost isn't uniform — it's O(followers), and follower counts follow a power law. For a normal user (hundreds of followers) the write is trivial; for a celebrity (tens of millions) a single tweet becomes a write storm that delays delivery and hammers the cache. So the average-driven decision ("fan out on write") is right for the body of the distribution and catastrophic for its tail — which is why the real system is a hybrid: write-fanout for the masses, read-fanout for the celebrities. Once again, the average chose the default and the tail forced the exception.

⚡ Quick Check #1

A service calls 50 independent backends in parallel to render one page and returns only when all have answered. Each backend exceeds its latency target 2% of the time. Roughly what fraction of page loads will be slow — and what's the lesson?

The page is slow if any backend is slow. Probability all 50 are fast = 0.98⁵⁰ ≈ 0.36, so ~64% of page loads are slow — even though each backend is "99%-ish fine." This is tail-latency amplification. Lessons: (1) per-service SLOs don't compose into the end-to-end SLO; (2) reduce fan-out where you can; (3) add per-call timeouts with fallbacks and consider hedged requests (fire a duplicate to another replica after a short delay, use whichever returns first) so one straggler can't set your whole response time.

⚡ Quick Check #2

Your PM says "our app averages 80 ms, that's great, ship it." Your biggest customers are complaining it's slow. Without any new instrumentation work, what one change to how you report latency would most likely expose the problem?

Stop reporting the average and report percentiles — specifically p95/p99/p99.9 — and ideally segment them by customer size. The 80 ms average can easily coexist with a p99 of 3–4 seconds; your big customers have more data, so their requests live in that tail. The mean was never wrong, it was just answering "what's typical?" when the question was "how bad are the worst experiences, and who's having them?" Percentiles answer that; averages structurally cannot.

✅ Key Takeaways

"Is it scalable?" is the wrong question. Scalability is always relative to a specific load parameter. Name how load grows (read/write ratio, follower distribution) before you design or judge anything.
The average lies; percentiles tell the truth. Response time is a distribution. Report p50 for typical and p95/p99/p99.9 for the tail — because your most valuable customers live in the tail.
Fan-out is a read-vs-write cost trade-off. Move work to write time (fan-out on write) when reads dominate; keep it at read time when writes must stay cheap or fan-out is huge. Twitter went hybrid because the follower distribution is skewed.
Tail-latency amplification is real. When one request waits on many backends, the slowest wins. 1% slow × 100 backends ≈ 63% slow requests. Bound fan-out; use timeouts and hedged requests.
Throughput ≠ response time, latency ≠ response time. Pick the metric that matches the system (batch vs online), and remember response time includes queueing — which is where bursts create the tail.
