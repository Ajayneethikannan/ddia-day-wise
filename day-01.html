Every system design interview opens with reliability, scalability, and maintainability. Naming these three in the first 2 minutes signals senior-level thinking.
🌩️

The $150 Million Typo

It's February 28, 2017, 9:37 AM. An AWS engineer is debugging a billing issue in us-east-1. The playbook says to remove a small set of servers from a subsystem. He types the command — but mistypes the count. Instead of a small set, he removes a large set of servers supporting S3's index subsystem.

Within minutes, S3 is partially down. And with it: Netflix can't stream. Slack messages won't deliver. GitHub is red. Mailchimp can't send email. Hundreds of companies — all built by excellent engineers — are simultaneously broken. Four and a half hours later, S3 recovers. Estimates put the cost to US companies at $150 million.

The fascinating part? Every one of those broken applications had a database. Most had caches. Many had sophisticated APIs. They were all data-intensive applications. Yet almost none were designed with a clear framework for the three things that determine whether a data system survives real-world conditions:

🛡️
Reliability
Works correctly even when things go wrong
📈
Scalability
Handles growth in load, data, and complexity
🔧
Maintainability
Engineers can work productively over time

S3 itself has 11 nines of durability. The outage wasn't a reliability failure inside S3 — it was a maintainability failure: operational tooling without safeguards against human error. The apps that depended on S3 lacked the reliability design (circuit breakers, fallbacks) to survive the wait. This book teaches you how to think about all three — deliberately.

What Is DDIA — and What Is a "Data-Intensive" Application?

Designing Data-Intensive Applications by Martin Kleppmann (2017) is the most important book in modern backend engineering. It doesn't teach you a specific framework. It teaches you how to think about data systems — what trade-offs are being made, why systems are designed the way they are, and what breaks at scale.

Its central question: what makes an application "data-intensive"?

TypePrimary bottleneckExamples
Compute-intensive CPU cycles, algorithms Video encoding, ML training, physics simulation
Data-intensive Volume, complexity, speed of data Slack, Uber, Twitter, any CRUD app at scale

Data-intensive applications are hard because the data grows in three dimensions simultaneously: volume (1K users → 10M), complexity (more entity types, more relationships), and speed of change (more writes, more real-time requirements).

The Analogy: A Restaurant Kitchen

You open a restaurant. One chef, one waiter, one kitchen. Simple, fast, and maintainable — if something breaks, you fix it in 10 minutes. Then Friday night happens and 200 people show up. The kitchen collapses. You hire more chefs — but now the kitchen is chaotic. The new chefs don't know the recipes. Dishes are inconsistent. One chef bumps another and drops soup. You gained scalability and lost reliability and maintainability simultaneously.

RestaurantData SystemConcern
Kitchen works when a chef calls in sick System tolerates hardware/software faults Reliability
Open a second register when lines form Add nodes / shards to handle more load Scalability
Recipes written so any new chef can follow Codebase easy for new engineers to understand Simplicity
Consistent food quality regardless of who cooked Correct results regardless of which replica answered Reliability
Swap vegetable supplier without re-training chefs Change database without rewriting app code Evolvability
Prep cook labels every ingredient with expiry date Monitoring, logging, metrics, alerting Operability

The key insight: the three concerns are in tension. You could achieve perfect reliability by triple-staffing every station — but now it's too complex to manage. You could simplify to one chef — but you can't scale. The art of data systems engineering is managing this tension deliberately, not accidentally. The Michelin-star restaurant and the neighborhood diner face the same three concerns; they just resolve the trade-offs differently.

The Three Concerns, Precisely Defined

Reliability means the system continues to work correctly even when things go wrong. "Correctly" = performs the expected function, at acceptable performance, preventing unauthorized access. "Things going wrong" includes hardware faults (disk failures, RAM errors), software errors (bugs, crashes), and human mistakes (misconfiguration). The key distinction: a fault is when one component deviates; a failure is when the whole system stops providing service. Good systems tolerate faults so they don't cascade into failures.

Scalability is the system's ability to cope with increased load. Load is described quantitatively — "We handle 10K req/sec. If that grows to 100K, what happens to response times? What resources do we need to add?" Scalability isn't about being internet-scale from day one; it's about having a clear, honest answer to "when this grows 10x, what breaks first?"

Maintainability has three sub-properties: Operability (easy to run, monitor, and operate — good visibility into what's happening), Simplicity (reduced accidental complexity — new engineers can understand the system without weeks of archaeology), and Evolvability (easy to adapt — requirements change, the system can grow with them). Most software cost is in ongoing maintenance, not initial development. This is where most teams hemorrhage money.

In the Wild

SLACK
Started simple, scaled painfully. Slack v1 was a Rails app with a single MySQL database — textbook prioritization of maintainability (simplicity) over scalability. When Slack hit millions of DAUs, they had to re-architect live under traffic: sharded MySQL, a custom channel-membership cache called Flannel, and eventually Vitess. The cost was months of engineer-years. The lesson: simple architectures are great, but they need a planned evolution path before you hit the ceiling.
NETFLIX
Reliability as a product feature. Netflix runs Chaos Monkey — a service that deliberately terminates random production instances — because forced fault exposure builds real fault tolerance. They use Cassandra for watch history (high write scalability, multi-region), EVCache for personalization, and Elasticsearch for search. The operational complexity is enormous, but 200M subscribers demand it. This is the reliability-and-scalability-first end of the spectrum — and a cautionary tale for anyone who copies it without 200M users.
AMAZON S3
11 nines of durability — and still a 2017 outage. The outage happened not from hardware or software failure, but from an operator removing servers faster than S3's internal indexing system could tolerate. AWS's post-mortem fix: rate-limit the operational tooling so S3 subsystems can't be removed faster than a safe threshold. A pure maintainability / operability fix to a reliability symptom.
POSTGRESQL
The maintainability champion. PostgreSQL has been in active development since 1986. Its codebase is one of the most maintainable large open-source projects in existence. New features (logical replication, JSON, partial indexes, generated columns) have been added across 30+ years without breaking existing behavior. Evolvability over decades — that's the true test of a maintainable system.

The SDE 2 Lens — Why This Matters Right Now

You probably face these problems daily — you just didn't have the vocabulary to name them precisely. Here's how the three concerns show up in your work right now:

  • "Why is this feature so hard to add?" — You're hitting an evolvability problem. The original data model didn't anticipate this use case. That's what "technical debt" actually means: it's usually maintainability debt.
  • "Why is the site slow at 5 PM?" — That's a scalability problem. Understanding load parameters (QPS, read/write ratio, cache hit rate) lets you diagnose it precisely instead of blindly throwing hardware at it.
  • "Why did that outage cascade so badly?" — That's a reliability design gap. A single component failure shouldn't take down the whole system. DDIA will teach you the patterns (circuit breakers, bulkheads, replication) that prevent cascades.
  • "Why is oncall so painful?" — Operability debt. The system doesn't have enough visibility, dashboards are wrong, runbooks are stale. Good operability is a design goal, not an afterthought.

The single most impactful mental shift as you level up from SDE 2 to senior: stop asking "will this work?" and start asking "what are the failure modes, how does this scale, and can someone else maintain this in 2 years?"

System Design Interview Playbook

When this comes up: Every system design interview, in the first 5 minutes. "Design Twitter," "Design Slack," "Design a URL shortener" — all start here.

What to say (opening framework):

  • Before diving into components: "Can I clarify the requirements across reliability, scalability, and maintainability?"
  • Reliability: "What's acceptable downtime? If the database goes down, do we fail or serve stale data?"
  • Scalability: "What's our target DAU and QPS? What's the read/write ratio? Is traffic spiky or steady?"
  • Maintainability: "How large is the team? What's the operational maturity? Are we optimizing for velocity or stability?"

The trade-off to articulate: "A distributed setup gives us higher reliability and scalability, but adds significant operational complexity. Given you said the team is 5 engineers, I'd suggest starting with well-tuned single-region Postgres with read replicas and planning the next step when we hit the ceiling."

Common mistake: Jumping directly into "microservices and Kubernetes and Kafka" without understanding load or failure requirements. This signals architecture cargo-culting, not system design thinking.

⚡ Power move: At the end of your design, say: "Let me walk through what happens when each major component fails." This single habit separates senior candidates from mid-level ones in FAANG interviews.

Pause and Think

Your team debates MongoDB vs PostgreSQL. A colleague says "MongoDB scales better." How do you evaluate this using the three concerns?

First, challenge the precision of "scales better." Scales in what dimension? Horizontal write throughput? Read latency at high volume? Both can handle millions of records — the question is what your specific load looks like.

Reliability: Postgres's ACID guarantees are rock solid. MongoDB has had historical durability defaults that were dangerous (writes acknowledged before disk sync), though modern versions fix this. If you need strong transactional guarantees, Postgres wins clearly.

Scalability: MongoDB was designed for horizontal sharding. If you need to write millions of documents/sec across many regions, its native sharding is smoother. But Postgres with read replicas + Citus (sharding extension) scales extremely well too. What's your actual QPS target?

Maintainability: Schema-less MongoDB seems simpler initially, but teams consistently find that lack of schema enforcement leads to data quality problems and harder debugging at scale. Postgres's rigid schema is a feature — it makes the data maintainable. The real maintainability cost is team familiarity and existing tooling.

Conclusion: Use Postgres unless you have a specific, measured reason not to. "MongoDB scales better" rarely holds in practice for most applications. The performance ceiling difference doesn't matter until you're at very high scale — and by then you'll know exactly what you need.

A startup wants "maximum reliability from day one" — triple replication, multi-region, Chaos Engineering. Good engineering?

This is a classic maintainability trap. Adding reliability mechanisms adds complexity — which is a maintainability cost. For a startup with 5 engineers and 100 users, multi-region replication adds months of engineering time, more surfaces for distributed bugs (the hardest kind to debug), and operational overhead that drains the team. It consumes capacity that should go toward product-market fit.

The DDIA principle: Right-size reliability to your actual requirements. A startup's biggest reliability risk isn't hardware failure — it's shipping the wrong product. The "reliability" that matters most at day one is: can you deploy quickly, roll back safely, and iterate fast?

The right approach: Use managed services (RDS Multi-AZ gives you basic reliability for free). Keep application architecture simple. Plan your scaling path for when you actually hit limits. Build complexity when evidence demands it, not ahead of time.

Key Takeaways

  • 1
    Data-intensive ≠ compute-intensive. The challenge is data: its volume, complexity, and rate of change — not CPU. Most backend applications are data-intensive.
  • 2
    Three concerns govern every data system: Reliability (works correctly under fault), Scalability (handles growth), Maintainability (engineers can work on it productively over time). Every architectural decision is a trade-off between these three.
  • 3
    Reliability is not just uptime. A system returning stale or wrong data is unreliable even if technically "up." Reliability requires fault-tolerance and correctness together.
  • 4
    Complexity is the enemy of maintainability. Every component you add (cache, search index, queue) increases the operational surface area. Add complexity in proportion to the load evidence that demands it — not ahead of it.
  • 5
    The "right" architecture is context-dependent. Postgres is correct for a startup. Cassandra is correct at Netflix scale. Copying one without the constraints that motivated it is cargo-culting.
