🎯 Interview Relevance
"What happens when X fails?" is asked in every system design interview. Distinguishing independent hardware faults from correlated software faults is a senior-level signal.
🌩️

3:14 AM — Your Pager Goes Off

You're on call. The dashboard is red. A disk in one of your database servers has failed — a single physical component, in a single machine, in a rack you've never physically seen. Annoying, but fine: hardware dies, that's expected. You replace it.

Then you do the math that every engineer eventually does. Your service runs on a fleet. A single enterprise hard drive has a mean time to failure (MTTF) of about 10–50 years. Sounds reassuring — until you remember you don't have one disk. You have thousands.

With 10,000 disks, that 10–50 year MTTF translates to roughly one disk death every single day. The thing you treated as a rare, surprising event at 3 AM isn't rare at all. At scale, hardware failure isn't an exception — it's the steady-state condition your system lives in.

Today we split "things going wrong" into two fundamentally different beasts: hardware faults (random, independent, and — counterintuitively — the easy kind) and software errors (systematic, correlated, and the kind that takes down your whole fleet in one shot). Knowing the difference changes how you design everything.

📖

Fault vs Failure — The Distinction That Matters

DDIA gives us two words that engineers often use interchangeably but absolutely should not:

  • Fault: one component deviating from its spec. A disk stops responding. A process crashes. A network packet is dropped. Faults are local and expected.
  • Failure: the system as a whole stops providing the service users need. The app is down. Data is lost. Requests time out.

The entire discipline of reliability engineering boils down to one goal: prevent faults from cascading into failures. You cannot stop disks from dying or cosmic rays from flipping bits in RAM. What you can do is design a system where a fault stays contained — a tree falls in the forest and the forest keeps standing.

Kleppmann makes a deliberately counterintuitive point: it can be worth increasing the rate of faults to reduce the rate of failures. Netflix's Chaos Monkey kills random servers on purpose. Why? Because deliberately triggering faults during business hours forces engineers to build systems that tolerate them — so a real fault at 3 AM is a non-event.

Two Categories of "Things Going Wrong"

DimensionHardware FaultsSoftware Errors
CausePhysical: disk crash, RAM bit-flip, power loss, unplugged cableLogical: bugs, bad input, resource leaks, cascading retries
CorrelationIndependent — one disk dying doesn't make another dieCorrelated — same bug exists on every node simultaneously

Primary defenseRedundancy (RAID, replicas, dual power)Testing, isolation, monitoring, careful design
DifficultyEasier — well-understood, redundancy worksHarder — no silver bullet

The key insight: redundancy is fantastic for hardware faults because failures are independent. Two disks rarely die in the same second for unrelated reasons. But redundancy does nothing for a software bug — if the bug is on node 1, it's on the identical replica node 2, ready to fire at the same instant on the same bad input.

✈️

The Analogy: An Airliner vs a Recalled Car Model

Hardware faults are like an airplane engine failing. A modern jet has two (or four) engines, redundant hydraulics, and backup electrical systems. If one engine fails mid-flight, the plane flies on the others and lands safely. Engine failures are independent — one engine flaming out doesn't cause the other to flame out. Redundancy works beautifully here. This is exactly RAID, dual power supplies, and database replicas.

Software errors are like a design flaw that triggers a recall. Imagine a car model where, due to a firmware bug, the brakes fail whenever the odometer hits exactly 100,000 km. Now imagine the manufacturer sold 2 million identical cars. Having a "backup car" of the same model doesn't help — it has the identical flaw and will fail at the identical trigger. The only fixes are: catch it in testing, isolate subsystems, and recall (patch) carefully.

This is why a billion-dollar company can have triple-redundant hardware and still suffer a global outage: the redundancy protects against engine failure, but the outage came from a recall-class software bug that every node shared.

The Hardware Fault Toolkit

For the independent failures, the industry has decades of proven redundancy techniques:

# Classic single-machine redundancy RAID # disks mirror/parity → survive a disk death Dual power supplies # survive a PSU failure or one power feed ECC RAM # detect & correct single-bit memory errors Hot-swappable parts # replace a disk without powering down # The modern shift: tolerate losing whole MACHINES Replication # multiple copies across machines (Day 32) Multi-AZ deployment # survive an entire datacenter/AZ outage Rolling restarts # patch a node without downtime

There's been a strategic shift. Older systems leaned on hardware redundancy inside one big machine. Modern cloud systems assume whole machines will vanish and build software-level fault tolerance across many cheap commodity boxes — which has the bonus that you can do rolling upgrades and reboot nodes for maintenance with zero downtime.

In the Wild

GOOGLE — Commodity hardware doctrine
Embrace failure instead of fighting it. Google famously built its infrastructure on cheap commodity servers rather than expensive "reliable" enterprise hardware. The bet: at their scale, machines will die constantly, so build software (GFS, Borg, Spanner) that tolerates whole-machine loss. Cheaper hardware that fails more often, plus rock-solid software fault tolerance, beat expensive hardware that fails less.
NETFLIX — Chaos Monkey
Cause faults on purpose, during the day. Chaos Monkey randomly terminates production instances during business hours. This deliberately raises the fault rate to drive the failure rate to zero — engineers are forced to build services that survive any single instance vanishing. A textbook application of "increase faults to reduce failures."
2012 LEAP SECOND — The correlated software bug
One line of kernel code, global outage. When a leap second was added on June 30, 2012, a Linux kernel bug caused CPUs to spin at 100%. Reddit, LinkedIn, Mozilla, and many others went down simultaneously — not because their hardware failed, but because they all ran the same buggy code triggered by the same instant in time. The perfect illustration of a correlated software fault that no amount of replication could prevent.
AWS S3 — 11 nines via redundancy
99.999999999% durability. S3 achieves eleven nines of durability by storing each object redundantly across multiple devices and facilities, continuously verifying data integrity with checksums and self-healing from any detected corruption. This is hardware-fault tolerance taken to its logical extreme — and it works precisely because device failures are independent.
🔬

The SDE 2 Lens — Why This Matters Right Now

This isn't abstract distributed-systems theory — it explains things you've already lived through:

  • "We had three replicas, so why did the whole service go down?" — Because the outage was a correlated software fault, not an independent hardware one. Replicas only protect against independent failures. Your bug was on all three.
  • "A single bad request took down the whole pod." — Classic missing isolation. One poisoned input shouldn't crash the process. Validate at the boundary; consider per-request sandboxing or graceful degradation.
  • "Our retries made the outage worse." — A retry storm. When everything is already failing, naive retries amplify load. This is why you need exponential backoff, jitter, and circuit breakers — design topics that flow directly from this fault/failure distinction.
  • "Why does the SRE team run game days?" — Same logic as Chaos Monkey. Practicing faults in a controlled setting means the real one isn't a surprise.

The mental upgrade: when you design any feature, ask "is the failure I'm guarding against independent or correlated?" Redundancy answers the first. Only testing, isolation, and monitoring answer the second.

🎯

System Design Interview Playbook

When this comes up: The moment you sketch any multi-node architecture, a strong interviewer asks "what happens when this node/disk/datacenter fails?" Reliability is a guaranteed line of questioning.

What to say:

  • "Let me separate independent hardware faults from correlated software faults — they need different defenses."
  • "For hardware: I'll replicate data across nodes and across availability zones, so losing a disk, a machine, or a whole AZ degrades capacity but not availability."
  • "For software: replication doesn't help, so I'll add input validation at the edge, process isolation, circuit breakers with backoff+jitter, and tight monitoring on error rates."
  • "I'd quantify it: with N machines at failure rate R, I expect N×R failures per year, so my system must treat node loss as routine, not exceptional."

The trade-off to articulate: "Hardware redundancy on one big box is simple but expensive and can't survive whole-machine loss; software fault tolerance over commodity nodes is cheaper and more robust but adds operational complexity. Given the scale, I'd choose [X]."

Common candidate mistake: Answering "I'll add replicas" to every reliability question — including ones about bad deploys or poison inputs, where replicas are useless.

⚡ Power move: Bring up the failure math unprompted: "At 10,000 disks even a great MTTF means roughly one failure per day, so self-healing re-replication has to be automatic — there's no human in the loop at that rate." That single sentence signals you've operated real systems.

Pause and Think

Your service runs 3 identical replicas behind a load balancer. A deploy goes out at noon and within 60 seconds all three replicas are crash-looping. What category of fault is this, and what would actually have prevented it?

This is a correlated software fault, not a hardware fault. The dead giveaway is the timing and the totality: all three nodes failing within seconds of a deploy means they share a common cause — the new code or config — not three independent coincidences.

What would NOT have prevented it: Adding a fourth or tenth replica. They'd all run the same broken deploy and crash too.

What actually prevents it:

  • Staged/canary deploys: roll the new version to 1 node (or 1% of traffic) first; if error rates spike, halt and roll back before it reaches the rest.
  • Automated rollback: tie deploy health to metrics so a bad version auto-reverts.
  • Pre-prod testing: the bug should ideally be caught before it ever ships.
  • Process isolation + graceful handling: so a crash on one request doesn't take the whole process with it.

The lesson: the defense for correlated faults is about your deploy and test process, which is exactly why this topic bleeds into maintainability and human-error reliability (Day 3).

Netflix deliberately kills its own production servers with Chaos Monkey. From a pure "uptime" view this seems insane. Why is it good reliability engineering?

It's the "increase faults to decrease failures" principle in action. The reasoning:

  • Faults are inevitable anyway. Instances will die in production — the only question is whether it happens at 3 AM during a traffic spike (worst case) or at 2 PM on a Tuesday while engineers watch (best case).
  • Untested fault tolerance is fictional fault tolerance. A failover path that's never exercised is probably broken — stale configs, wrong DNS, a runbook nobody updated. Chaos Monkey continuously proves the recovery paths actually work.
  • It changes incentives. If your code might be killed at any moment, you're forced to make every service stateless-or-replicated and resilient from day one, instead of bolting it on after the first outage.

So the apparent madness is actually rigor: by making single-instance faults a constant, boring occurrence, Netflix ensures that no single fault can ever escalate into a user-visible failure.

Key Takeaways

  • 1
    Fault ≠ failure. A fault is one component deviating from spec; a failure is the whole system not delivering service. The job of reliability engineering is to stop faults from becoming failures.
  • 2
    At scale, hardware failure is the steady state. 10,000 disks with a great MTTF still means ~one failure per day. Self-healing must be automatic; there's no human in that loop.
  • 3
    Hardware faults are independent; software faults are correlated. Redundancy crushes the first and does nothing for the second — the same bug lives on every identical replica.
  • 4
    Software faults need process, not parts. Testing, input validation, isolation, circuit breakers, and monitoring — you cannot buy your way out with better hardware or more replicas.
  • 5
    Sometimes raise faults to lower failures. Chaos Monkey and game days deliberately trigger faults so recovery paths are proven and a real fault becomes a non-event.
